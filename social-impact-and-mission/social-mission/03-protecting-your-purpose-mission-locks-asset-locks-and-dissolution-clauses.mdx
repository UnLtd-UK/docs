# Protecting Your Purpose: Mission Locks, Asset Locks, and Dissolution Clauses

*This resource is derived from a webinar hosted by Hogan Lovells' HL BaSE practice in partnership with Unlimited. It covers the legal mechanisms available to UK social entrepreneurs to secure their social mission.*

## 1. Core Definitions

Before choosing a legal structure or amending your articles, it is vital to understand the three core mechanisms that define a social enterprise legally.

### What is a Mission Lock?
A **Mission Lock** ensures that an entity's social purpose remains its primary objective in the long term[cite: 28].
* **Purpose:** It gives directors clear instructions to consider stakeholders (beneficiaries, environment) rather than just shareholder financial returns[cite: 29].
* **Function:** It prevents "mission drift" by legally locking the purpose into the company's constitution[cite: 28, 31].

### What is an Asset Lock?
An **Asset Lock** is a provision that prevents the assets (money, property, profits) of an organization from being used for private benefit[cite: 69, 79].
* **Scope:** It covers distributions to members (dividends) during the life of the company and the distribution of assets when the company is wound up[cite: 73, 80].
* **Requirement:** An effective lock ensures surpluses are principally reinvested into the business or community[cite: 23].

### What is a Dissolution Clause?
A **Dissolution Clause** dictates what happens to the company's remaining assets if the company is closed down (wound up)[cite: 84].
* **Standard Company:** Assets are usually divided among shareholders[cite: 85].
* **Social Enterprise:** The clause should state that residual assets must be transferred to another asset-locked body (like a charity or CIC) or used for a similar social purpose, rather than given to members[cite: 87, 159].

## 2. The Sliding Scale of "Locks"

There are different ways to embed these protections, ranging from flexible to highly regulated.

### Level 1: Light Touch (Private Limited Company)
You can include a mission statement in your Articles of Association.
* **Pros:** High flexibility.
* **Cons:** Can be changed or removed by a shareholder vote of 75%[cite: 50]. This is often viewed as insufficient evidence of social purpose for grant funders[cite: 52].

### Level 2: Enhanced Constitutional Locks
To make a Private Limited Company harder to change, you can add specific restrictions:
* **Unanimous Consent:** Require 100% of shareholders to agree to change the mission[cite: 108].
* **Golden Shares:** Issuing a specific share with veto rights (see below)[cite: 58].

### Level 3: Regulated Entities (Hardest to Change)
* **Charities:** Subject to strict asset locks under charity law. Assets *must* be used for public benefit[cite: 92].
* **Community Interest Companies (CICs):** Must satisfy the "Community Interest Test." Assets cannot be transferred for less than market value unless to another asset-locked body[cite: 95].

## 3. Deep Dive: The Golden Share

A **Golden Share** is a mechanism used in companies limited by shares to protect the mission without restricting day-to-day management[cite: 58].

* **How it works:** It is a share that holds specific voting rights—essentially a veto power[cite: 247].
* **Usage:** It can stop incoming shareholders from ignoring the social mission[cite: 58].
* **Best Practice:** Ideally, this share is held by a third party with no economic interest in the company, such as a charity or foundation with similar goals. This separates the protection of the mission from financial incentives[cite: 264].

> **Note:** While some view Golden Shares as complex, they allow a social enterprise to operate freely while robustly safeguarding its purpose[cite: 59].

## 4. Constitutional vs. Contractual Locks

When seeking funding, you may encounter requirements for either constitutional or contractual locks.

| Feature | Constitutional Lock | Contractual Lock |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Location** | Written into the Articles of Association (the company's DNA)[cite: 116]. | Written into a specific agreement (Grant, Loan, or Shareholder Agreement)[cite: 90, 112]. |
| **Durability** | Lasts for the lifetime of the company (unless formally amended)[cite: 241]. | Only lasts as long as the contract or agreement is active[cite: 114]. |
| **Examples** | Asset lock clauses in Articles; CIC regulations. | "Reserve matters" in a shareholder agreement; veto rights in a grant contract[cite: 118]. |

## 5. Case Study: Adapting for Grant Funding

*Based on the "Save the World Ltd" scenario presented by HL BaSE.*

**The Scenario:** A private company limited by shares needs grant funding. The funder requires:
1.  A clear social mission analogous to a charitable purpose[cite: 132].
2.  Reinvestment of at least 51% of surpluses[cite: 133].
3.  A lock on the mission and assets[cite: 133].

**The Solution:**
Instead of converting to a CIC or Charity (which adds regulation), the company amends its Articles of Association:
1.  **Object Clause:** Drafted to match a charitable "head" (e.g., environmental protection)[cite: 145].
2.  **Directors' Duties:** Added a clause requiring directors to prioritize impact on community and environment[cite: 150].
3.  **Distribution Clause:** Capped dividends to ensure >50% of profits are reinvested[cite: 155].
4.  **Dissolution Clause:** Mandated that on winding up, assets go to a similar organization, not shareholders[cite: 159].

## 6. Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: Do private companies with asset locks get tax relief?**
**A:** Generally, no. A private company with an asset lock does not get the same tax benefits as a charity. However, investors *might* be eligible for SEIS or EIS tax relief, though this applies to non-social companies as well [cite: 225-227].

**Q: Can I sell a company that has an asset lock?**
**A:** Yes, you can sell shares. However, the buyer must understand that the restrictions (mission and asset locks) remain in place. If the company is wound up, the asset lock prevents the buyer from stripping the assets for personal gain[cite: 223, 261].

**Q: What happens if I breach a lock?**
**A:**
* **Constitutional:** Actions taken in breach may be voided or cancelled. For regulated entities (CICs/Charities), this can lead to being struck off [cite: 209-210].
* **Contractual:** Can lead to termination of funding, repayment demands, or liability for damages [cite: 213-214].
* **Reputational:** Severe damage to trust with stakeholders[cite: 215].

**Q: Does B Corp certification count as an asset lock?**
**A:** No. B Corp certification requires a mission statement in your articles regarding ethical operation, but it does not constitute a financial asset lock[cite: 233, 256].
