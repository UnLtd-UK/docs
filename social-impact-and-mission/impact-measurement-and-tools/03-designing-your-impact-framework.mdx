## Overview

An impact framework brings together your problem definition, stakeholder map, Theory of Change, and measurement plan into a coherent system. This guide walks through the process developed by UnLtd for social entrepreneurs.

By the end of this process, you should have:

- A clear definition of the problem you are solving
- A map of your key stakeholders and what they need to know
- A Theory of Change framework linking activities to impact
- A plan for what data to collect and how

## Step 1: Define the Problem

The first step is identifying the problem you are solving in enough detail to guide your measurement approach. Use these five perspectives:

1. **What is the key issue** you are trying to address? Why is it important? Be specific.
2. **Who is it a problem for?** Describe your beneficiaries, including demographics and circumstances.
3. **What PESTLE factors** shape this problem? Consider Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Legal, and Environmental dimensions.
4. **What evidence** do you have that this is worth the investment? Reference external research, data, and reports.
5. **What alternatives** exist? What are the advantages and disadvantages of current solutions?

This problem definition feeds directly into your business plan, your pitch to investors, and your Theory of Change.

## Step 2: Map Your Stakeholders

Not all stakeholders need the same impact information. Understanding who needs what helps you design targeted, relevant reporting.

Classify your external stakeholders into three groups:

| Category | Question | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Funders and Customers** | Who pays for your activities? | Grant-makers, investors, corporate partners, paying customers |
| **Beneficiaries** | Who benefits from your work? | Service users, communities, families |
| **Partners and Associates** | Who else do you work with? | Delivery partners, referral organisations, sector bodies |

For each key stakeholder, answer:

- **What do they want to know?** A funder may want outcome data and cost-effectiveness. A beneficiary may want to understand how the service will help them.
- **How will you inform them?** Consider the format, frequency, and level of detail appropriate for each audience.

## Step 3: Build Your Theory of Change

Using the problem definition and stakeholder map, construct your Theory of Change using the pyramid structure:

### Exercise

1. Start with **Impact** (1-2 items): What is the meaningful change for a group of people, environment, or society you want to see?
2. Define **Outcomes** (up to 5-6 items): How will you affect change for your users? Think about changes in knowledge, attitudes, and practices. Separate short-term from long-term where possible.
3. List **Outputs** (up to 5 items): What are the main products or services your organisation provides that lead to those outcomes?
4. Map **Activities and Resources** (up to 10 items): What does your organisation need to do and have to deliver those outputs?

### Sense-Check

Review your framework against three criteria:

- **Specific and clear** -- each element addresses a distinct stage and is understandable to someone outside your organisation
- **Relevant** -- does it align with what your key stakeholders want to know?
- **Logical** -- do all stages connect? Is the causal chain credible?

Work through the logical chain from activities up to impact. If any connection feels forced, revisit that part of the framework.

## Step 4: Create Your Social Impact Canvas

The Social Impact Canvas is a one-page summary that distils your framework into four rows:

| Section | Content |
| --- | --- |
| **Impact** | The overarching social change you aim to achieve |
| **Outcomes** | The changes you expect to see in your beneficiaries |
| **Performance Measures (Outputs)** | The quantifiable deliverables that indicate progress |
| **Strategies (Activities)** | The specific actions and interventions you will undertake |

This canvas is useful for workshops, planning sessions, and quick communication of your impact strategy. It should be a living document that evolves as your understanding deepens.

## Collective Impact Frameworks

For organisations working across a sector or supporting multiple ventures, a Collective Impact Framework (CIF) provides a standardised menu of metrics. UnLtd developed a CIF for its Solutions for an Aging Society impact area in partnership with the University of Northampton, offering a "pick and mix" approach where each venture selects metrics appropriate to their intervention type.

A CIF serves two functions:

- **For social entrepreneurs** -- a framework of quality-assured metrics to start and improve impact measurement
- **For support organisations** -- standardised, comparable reporting across a portfolio of ventures, enabling aggregate impact claims and evidence-based advocacy

Building a collective evidence base takes time. Start with case studies, build a core bank of comparable data, and progress towards confident aggregate reporting as your evidence grows.
