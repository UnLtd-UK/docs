## What Is Social Enterprise?

Social entrepreneurship is about running a business with a deep passion for tackling a particular social or environmental need. It combines doing business with doing good, making it one of the most exciting and fast-growing sectors in the UK and internationally.

A social enterprise differs from a straightforward charity because it is run as a business, generating revenue from trade and making profits which, in part or full, are reinvested to generate greater social benefit. It also differs from a conventional business by placing emphasis on **profit optimisation** rather than profit maximisation, encouraging sustainable and socially impactful growth in the long term.

### Common Characteristics

A social enterprise typically:

- Trades with the primary aim of tackling social needs by improving communities, people's life chances, or the environment
- Has a clear sense of its mission and how its activity addresses those needs
- Generates revenue from trade and reinvests profits to create greater social benefit
- Prioritises social impact alongside financial sustainability

### The Sector in Numbers

The social enterprise sector in the UK is substantial and resilient:

- Approximately **70,000** social enterprises operate in the UK
- They generate sustainable employment for close to **1 million people**, including those most disadvantaged from accessing the labour market
- Social enterprises have **three times the start-up rate** of the mainstream SME sector
- Close to a third of all social enterprises are three years old or younger
- During the recession, **38%** of social enterprises reported growth, demonstrating resilience
- The sector contributes an estimated **£18.5 billion to £55 billion** to the UK economy

### What Social Enterprises Need to Thrive

For the sector to achieve its full potential, social enterprises need access to:

- **Talent** -- skilled people who combine business acumen with social purpose
- **High-quality advice** -- from mentors, advisers, and specialist support organisations
- **Funding and investment** -- that helps maintain and grow social impact
- **Markets** -- including government procurement, where social value is increasingly considered

## What Is Social Innovation?

Social innovation describes new ideas, strategies, and methods that meet social needs more effectively than existing approaches. It spans the public and private sectors, civil society, and the household economy.

Social innovation is about creating new and more effective answers to the biggest challenges of our time: how to cut carbon footprints, how to keep people healthy, and how to end poverty.

### The Six Stages of Social Innovation

Drawing from the work of NESTA and The Young Foundation, social innovation typically follows six stages:

1. **Prompts, inspirations, and diagnoses** -- Recognising a problem and understanding its root causes. This includes using data, lived experience, and creative approaches to frame challenges in new ways.

2. **Proposals and ideas** -- Generating possible solutions through brainstorming, design thinking, and drawing on approaches from other fields. The best ideas often come from combining insights from different sectors.

3. **Prototyping and pilots** -- Testing ideas in practice on a small scale. This involves learning quickly, iterating based on feedback, and refining the approach before committing to large-scale implementation.

4. **Sustaining** -- Making the innovation financially viable and operationally robust. This means developing a sustainable business model, building the right team, and establishing reliable processes.

5. **Scaling and diffusion** -- Growing the impact of proven innovations. Scaling can happen through organisational growth, replication, franchising, licensing, or inspiring others to adopt similar approaches.

6. **Systemic change** -- Achieving fundamental shifts in the systems that perpetuate social problems. This involves changing policies, markets, norms, and institutions.

These stages are not always linear -- innovations may loop back, skip stages, or work on several simultaneously.

## The Changemakers Mindset

UnLtd's Changemakers Toolkit identifies three mindsets that work together to drive effective change:

| Mindset | Motto | Strength |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Collaborator** | Collaboration is the key to solving problems | Gets diverse perspectives together at the start of a project, ensuring wide input and building momentum |
| **Investigator** | You can't make effective change without being data-led | Ensures evidence of ideas changing behaviour and delivering impact, not just positive feedback |
| **Experimenter** | Don't let perfect be the enemy of good | Keeps focus on clearly defined problems and drives ideas to action |

Most organisations tend towards the Collaborator mindset, with fewer Experimenters. Effective innovation requires all three perspectives.

### The Design Journey

The Changemakers Toolkit uses a four-phase design journey:

1. **Discover** -- Notice a problem and understand it deeply
2. **Define** -- Make sense of what you have learnt and create a clear problem statement
3. **Develop** -- Generate ideas and potential solutions
4. **Deliver** -- Test which idea would be most effective

The journey moves between "designing the right things" (Discover and Define) and "designing things right" (Develop and Deliver), with a clear problem statement connecting the two halves.
