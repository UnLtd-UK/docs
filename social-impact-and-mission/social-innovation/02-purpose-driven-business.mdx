## The Case for Purpose

The idea that businesses exist solely to maximise shareholder value is increasingly being challenged. Research from Oxford University and Mars Incorporated, among others, has shown that businesses which define and embed a clear social purpose tend to perform better in the long term -- not despite their purpose, but because of it.

Purpose-driven business is about more than corporate social responsibility bolted on to a conventional business model. It requires fundamentally rethinking what success means and how your business creates value for all its stakeholders.

## The Economics of Mutuality

The Economics of Mutuality is a framework developed through a multi-year research partnership between Mars Incorporated and Oxford University's Saïd Business School. It provides a practical approach to putting purpose into practice.

### Core Principles

The framework rests on several key ideas:

**Mutual benefit over extraction:** Your business should create value for all stakeholders -- employees, suppliers, communities, and the environment -- not just shareholders. When you create mutual benefit, you build stronger relationships, more resilient supply chains, and more loyal customers.

**Measuring what matters:** Traditional financial accounting captures only part of the value your business creates (and destroys). The Economics of Mutuality calls for measuring human, social, and natural capital alongside financial capital.

**Ecosystem thinking:** Your business operates within an ecosystem of stakeholders and relationships. Understanding and nurturing this ecosystem is essential to long-term success.

**Purpose as strategy:** Your social purpose is not separate from your business strategy -- it should be the foundation of it. The most effective purpose-driven businesses align their commercial model with their social mission so that financial growth and social impact reinforce each other.

### The Three Capitals

The Economics of Mutuality encourages you to measure and manage three forms of capital beyond financial:

| Capital | What It Includes | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Human capital** | Skills, health, wellbeing, and capabilities of individuals connected to your business | Employee satisfaction, supplier workforce conditions, customer health outcomes |
| **Social capital** | Trust, relationships, networks, and social cohesion | Community engagement, supply chain relationships, stakeholder trust |
| **Natural capital** | Environmental resources and ecosystem services | Carbon footprint, water usage, biodiversity impact, waste reduction |

## Putting Purpose Into Practice

### Step 1: Define Your Purpose

Your purpose should articulate the positive change your business exists to create in the world. It should be:

- **Authentic** -- grounded in what your business actually does and cares about
- **Specific** -- clear enough to guide decision-making
- **Ambitious** -- aspirational enough to inspire and drive long-term thinking
- **Measurable** -- connected to outcomes you can track

### Step 2: Embed Purpose in Governance

Purpose needs to be more than a statement on your website. Consider:

- Writing your purpose into your Articles of Association
- Using a mission lock to protect your purpose (see the Social Mission section)
- Including purpose-related criteria in board and management evaluations
- Reporting on purpose alongside financial performance

### Step 3: Align Your Business Model

Review whether your business model creates or undermines the value you seek to create:

- Does your supply chain support or harm the communities you depend on?
- Does your pricing model enable access for your target beneficiaries?
- Does growth in revenue correspond to growth in social impact?
- Are there tensions between financial targets and social outcomes?

### Step 4: Measure Broadly

Develop metrics that capture value creation across all four capitals:

- **Financial:** Revenue, profit, return on investment
- **Human:** Employee wellbeing, skills development, beneficiary outcomes
- **Social:** Community impact, trust levels, partnership strength
- **Natural:** Environmental footprint, resource efficiency, ecological contribution

## Types of Social Entrepreneurs

Research into bottom-up urban development in the Netherlands has identified three types of social entrepreneurs, which offer a useful lens for understanding how social ventures evolve:

### Type A: The Pioneer

Early-stage individuals, often from creative or professional backgrounds, who identify a problem and start building a solution. Pioneers typically:

- Combine the roles of developer and end-user
- Are driven by personal motivation and a desire to create working solutions
- Have limited networks and few resources initially
- Seek collaboration with established actors rather than opposing them

### Type B: The Consolidated Organisation

As pioneers' efforts gain traction, more formalised organisations emerge. These:

- Have built substantial networks and secured necessary resources
- Maintain the developer and end-user roles but with greater capacity
- Can attract investment, form partnerships, and deliver at scale
- Often include the original pioneers alongside new members and specialists

### Type C: The Intermediate Agent

These actors facilitate and connect rather than directly developing solutions. They:

- Function as bridges between various stakeholders
- Focus on communicating, facilitating, and connecting goals and interests
- Aim to realise broader policy goals rather than specific developments
- Build power through reputation and strategic relationships

Understanding which type you most closely resemble can help you identify what resources, networks, and strategies you need to grow your impact.
