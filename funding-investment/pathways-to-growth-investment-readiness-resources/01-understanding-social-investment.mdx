## What Is Social Investment?

Social investment means finance provided for voluntary, community, and social enterprise (VCSE) organisations, which investors expect to both get back and to create social impact.

Social investors include specialist banks, individuals, charitable trusts, and dedicated social investment funds. What all social investors have in common is:

1. They expect to get their money back, often with interest
2. They want to see positive social change take place as a result of their investment

Social investment is not a grant or a donation. It is money provided to enable your organisation to generate more income or be more effective: growing your business, putting in place better systems, doing more social good, and repaying the investment in the process.

## Why Social Investment Matters

1. **It recycles money:** Because social investment returns to the investor, it can be used again to support more charities and social enterprises, and help ensure grants go where they are needed most.
2. **It attracts new money:** Social investment can attract money from new sources -- individuals, mainstream finance, or the private sector -- adding to existing resources.
3. **More organisations can use it:** More VCSE organisations are trading, meaning income is being earned and there is potential for money to be invested and paid back.

This does not mean social investment is necessarily right for your organisation. Not all VCSE organisations can or should be trading organisations, and many organisations seek to blend grants and investment.

## Is Social Investment Right for You?

### Does Your Organisation Need Investment?

Investment comes with a cost and you have to decide whether the potential benefits outweigh the costs. It could be that the best way forward is to build up money gradually by selling products or services at a profit, rather than looking to raise a lump sum from an investor.

Your organisation may need investment for four main reasons:

- **Getting started** -- raising money for a new start-up
- **Keeping going** -- supporting existing activities
- **Buying assets** -- purchasing buildings or other equipment
- **Scaling up** -- growing and expanding

### Should You Look for Grants Instead?

Situations where a grant may be useful and available:

- You need funding to test whether your idea works
- You need funding to find out whether anyone wants to pay for your products and services
- You need funding to sustain an activity that delivers great social value but does not bring in enough income
- You need funding to help you reach the point where you can take on investment
- You need a mix of finance that requires both grant and investment

### Why Investment May Be More Appropriate Than Grants

- **Flexibility:** Investors are likely to be more flexible about what you do
- **Active involvement and support:** Investors are more likely to want to get actively involved
- **Less restricted:** Social investment tends to be less restricted than grant funding
- **Business discipline:** Requires improved financial and business processes

### What About Conventional or Mainstream Investment?

Potential advantages of conventional investment:

- **Less complicated relationships:** Straightforward lending based on ability to repay
- **Better deals:** Mainstream banks may offer better rates due to greater scale
- **Quick and accessible:** Branch networks and online applications speed up the process

### Why You Might Choose Social Investment

- You cannot get the money from a bank
- You may get a better deal due to government or philanthropic subsidies
- A social investor is more likely to understand your business
- A social investor is more likely to be patient if circumstances change
- It supports the sustainability of the social economy

## Types of Social Investment

### Debt

An investor puts money into your organisation and wants to get it back, often with interest.

- **Secured loans** -- Loans against an asset (often a building) as collateral. Lower interest rates but risk of losing the asset if you cannot repay.
- **Unsecured loans** -- Loans not secured against an asset. Higher interest rates but no asset requirement.
- **Quasi-equity** -- A flexible debt product where repayments are based on business performance (profit or turnover). Useful for charities and CICs that cannot sell shares.
- **Charity bonds and crowd-funded loans** -- Loans from many investors through crowd-funding websites or charity bonds.

### Equity

An investor puts money in your organisation in exchange for part-ownership. The investor gets a right to share in dividends and may sell their shares as the business grows.

Most charities and social enterprises cannot sell shares. However, CIC Limited by Shares, community benefit societies, and some cooperative structures can issue shares within certain guidelines.

- **Community shares** -- Sale of shares in enterprises serving a community purpose (shops, pubs, community buildings, renewable energy)
- **Social impact bonds (SIBs)** -- Investors fund a special purpose vehicle; if charities deliver anticipated outcomes, government pays back investors with interest

## Working Out What You Need

### Start-Up Investment

Start-up enterprises are relatively high-risk investments because they lack a track record. For most social start-ups, it is unlikely the organisation can offer investors a large financial return.

**Possible uses for start-up investment:**

- Product or service design and development
- Delivering products and services to customers
- Sales and marketing
- Investment in systems (IT, online, data)
- Staffing

**Key considerations:**

- Be realistic about the amount of investment needed
- Be clear about how you will pay the money back
- Consider what support or input you want from investors

### Working Capital

Investment to support existing activities when facing cash flow challenges or needing working capital to sustain operations.

### Asset Purchase

Investment for purchasing buildings, equipment, or other capital items that support your organisation's mission.

### Growth Investment

Investment to grow and expand your operations, reach new markets, or increase your impact.

## Understanding the Market

The social investment market includes specialist banks, charitable trusts, social investment funds, and individual investors, each with different motivations but all seeking both financial return and positive social change.

## Where to Get Support

Specialist investment readiness programmes are specifically focused on helping your organisation get to a point where you can find investment. They provide organisations with grants to buy support from specialist experts and advisors.

- **Big Potential:** Grants of 25,000 to 75,000 pounds for specialist support
- **ICRF (Investment and Contract Readiness Fund):** Grants of 50,000 to 150,000 pounds for specialist support
- **Support programmes:** School for Social Entrepreneurs' Scale-Up, UnLtd's Big Venture Challenge / Fast Growth, and various accelerators

## Glossary

| Term | Definition |
|------|-----------|
| **Collateral** | An asset used to secure a loan |
| **Community shares** | Shares sold in enterprises serving a community purpose |
| **Debt** | Investment that must be repaid, often with interest |
| **Equity** | Investment in exchange for part-ownership of a business |
| **Quasi-equity** | A flexible debt product with repayments based on performance |
| **Social impact bond (SIB)** | An investment product enabling delivery of payment-by-results contracts |
| **Social investment** | Finance for VCSE organisations that investors expect to get back while creating social impact |
| **VCSE** | Voluntary, community, and social enterprise organisations |
