## What is a Theory of Change?

A Theory of Change explains the changes you hope to bring about (outcomes) for your beneficiaries and how you intend to achieve them (activities). It should be transparent, logical, and accessible. It is often expressed as a diagram or table.

A Theory of Change follows a logical flow:

| Inputs | Activities | Outputs | Outcomes |
|--------|-----------|---------|----------|
| Staff time, beneficiaries, materials, volunteers | The programmes and services you deliver | Countable results of your activities | The changes experienced by your beneficiaries |

Everything is connected by your **mission**.

## Why it matters

Your Theory of Change is not just an internal planning tool -- it is a critical document for investors. Here is why:

- It articulates what you hope to achieve and how.
- It helps identify assumptions in your activities.
- It forms a basis for claims around attribution of outcomes.
- It determines what changes need to be measured.
- It identifies and hypothesises a rationale for causality.
- The logic will appeal to funders and help in their funding decisions.

Social investors will review your Theory of Change during due diligence. They want to see that your impact model is documented, realistic, and impactful -- and that financial growth is clearly tied to the scaling of social impact.

## How to build your Theory of Change

### Use existing evidence

Draw on research, data, and published studies to inform your theory. Your Theory of Change should be grounded in evidence, not just aspiration.

### Keep it simple

Make it accessible to all stakeholders -- from board members to beneficiaries. If it is too complex to explain in a few minutes, it needs simplifying.

### Run a workshop

Develop your Theory of Change collaboratively. Bring together a wide range of stakeholders:

- Trustees and board members
- Staff and management
- Volunteers
- Beneficiaries

### Acknowledge limitations

Be honest about what you do not know. A credible Theory of Change recognises complexity and uncertainty rather than pretending everything is certain.

### Treat it as a living document

Your Theory of Change should be updated regularly to reflect practical experience, new evidence, and changes in your operating environment.

## Refining your Theory of Change

Once you have a basic framework, refine it by:

- Separating outcomes into **short-term**, **medium-term**, and **long-term**.
- Deciding which outcomes you will measure, and how.
- Making clear any **unverified assumptions**.
- Connecting activities and outcomes to **existing academic evidence**.

## Worked example

**Problem statements:**
- Youth offenders lack opportunities to engage with drama as a means to improve their soft skills.
- There is an absence of theatre provision in the local area.

**Mission:** Improve life chances of young people through theatre.

| Activities | Outputs | Short-term Outcomes | Medium-term Outcomes | Long-term Outcomes |
|-----------|---------|-------------------|---------------------|-------------------|
| Drama workshops aimed at young people who have been through the justice system | 1 workshop per week; end of term production; beneficiary self-evaluation | Greater sense of routine for beneficiaries; fewer exclusions amongst participants | Improved levels of self-confidence; development of empathy-related behaviours | Re-engagement with formal education provision or employment |
| Mentoring programme: beneficiaries go into schools to engage younger people at risk of exclusion in drama | 1 mentoring session per week | | | |

**Key assumptions:**
- Drama workshops contribute to the development of self-confidence and empathy.
- Re-engagement with formal education can be linked to drama workshops (attribution).
- Reduction in exclusion can be linked to the success of the mentoring programme (attribution).

**Areas for development:**
- Identifying a rigorous evidence base for social outcomes associated with drama workshops.
- Identifying longer-term outcomes for the mentoring programme.
