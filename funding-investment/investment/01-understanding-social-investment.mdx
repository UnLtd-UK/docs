## What is social investment?

Social investment is finance provided to voluntary and community organisations or social enterprises (VCSE organisations) where the investor expects both a financial return and a positive social impact. It is not a grant or a donation. It is money provided to help your organisation generate more income or operate more effectively -- growing your business, strengthening your systems, delivering more social good, and repaying the investment in the process.

Social investors include specialist banks, individuals, charitable trusts, and organisations set up specifically to make social investments. They all share two expectations:

1. They expect to get their money back, often with interest.
2. They want to see positive social change as a result of their investment.

## Why social investment matters

There are three compelling reasons to consider social investment:

- **It recycles capital.** Because social investment returns to the investor, the same money can support multiple organisations over time. In a world where grant funding is increasingly scarce, this is a powerful advantage.
- **It attracts new money.** Social investment opens the door to capital from individuals, mainstream finance institutions, and the private sector -- sources that would not typically fund VCSE organisations through grants.
- **More organisations can use it.** As more VCSE organisations develop trading income, they gain the revenue base needed to take on and repay investment.

## The UK social enterprise landscape

The UK has one of the most vibrant social enterprise ecosystems in the world. Research by the Design Council found that:

- 15% of UK SMEs are social enterprises.
- Social enterprises employ two million people.
- They contribute 55 billion pounds to the national economy.

Despite this, many social ventures remain unaware of the full range of finance options available to them. Discovery of social finance is often accidental rather than systematic, and understanding of repayable finance can be low. If you are reading this guide, you are already taking a proactive step.

## Is social investment right for you?

Investment comes with a cost, and you need to decide whether the potential benefits outweigh those costs. Start by being clear about what taking on investment will enable you to do that you cannot do already.

Organisations typically need investment for one of four purposes:

| Purpose | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Getting started** | Raising capital for a new venture or start-up |
| **Keeping going** | Managing cashflow when income does not arrive before bills are due |
| **Buying assets** | Purchasing buildings, equipment, or other long-term assets |
| **Scaling up** | Growing operations, bidding for bigger contracts, or expanding geographically |

### When a grant may be more appropriate

Not all activities suit investment. A grant may be more appropriate if:

- You need funding to test whether your idea works.
- You need to find out whether anyone will pay for your products or services.
- You need to sustain an activity that delivers great social value but does not generate enough income to cover costs.
- You need funding to reach the point where you can take on investment.

### Why investment can be better than grants

- **Flexibility** -- investors tend to be more flexible about how you use the money.
- **Active support** -- investors are more likely to get involved and offer guidance.
- **Fewer restrictions** -- social investment is usually less tied to specific projects.
- **Business discipline** -- investment encourages stronger financial and business processes.

## Types of social investment

### Debt (borrowing)

Debt investment means taking out a loan that you agree to repay over a set period, usually with interest. For example, an investor loans your organisation 10,000 pounds and you repay a total of 11,000 pounds at 229 pounds per month over four years.

| Type | How it works |
|------|-------------|
| **Secured loans** | A loan against an asset, often a building. Lower interest rates, but you risk losing the asset if you cannot repay. |
| **Unsecured loans** | No asset required as security. Higher interest rates to compensate for the greater risk to the investor. |
| **Charity bonds and crowd-funded loans** | Loans from many small investors, often facilitated through online platforms. |

### Equity (shares)

Equity investment involves selling a share of ownership in your organisation to an investor. The investor provides capital in exchange for a stake, and expects to receive dividends from earnings or capital gains when their shares are sold.

Most charities and traditional social enterprises cannot issue shares, but some legal structures can:

- **CIC Limited by Shares** -- Community Interest Companies structured with share capital.
- **Community benefit societies** -- Can issue community shares to members.
- **Cooperatives** -- Some cooperative structures permit share issuance.
- **Social impact bonds** -- Investment in a special purpose vehicle linked to outcomes-based contracts.

### Quasi-equity

If your organisation cannot issue shares, quasi-equity offers an alternative. A quasi-equity investment allows an investor to benefit from your future revenues through a royalty payment -- a fixed percentage of income. The investor may gain nothing if the organisation does not perform, making it similar to conventional equity but without requiring share issuance.

Quasi-equity payments are usually linked to income rather than profit, since many charities and social enterprises are not structured to distribute profits.

## Where to find social investment

| Type of investor | Examples |
|-----------------|----------|
| **Social banks** | Triodos, Charity Bank, Unity Trust (typically larger investments, often secured) |
| **Specialist providers** | Big Issue Invest, Key Fund, Social Investment Business Group, Bridges Ventures, CAF Venturesome |
| **Online platforms** | Crowdfunding and community share platforms |
| **Angel investors** | Individual early-stage investors providing riskier finance |
| **Trusts and foundations** | Charitable bodies increasingly engaging in social investment |
| **Brokerages** | ClearlySo, Social Finance |

## Factors that affect your decision to seek finance

Before pursuing social investment, consider these factors honestly:

- **Your attitude to risk.** Many social ventures are cautious about taking on debt, particularly where personal assets might be at risk. Your comfort with risk is shaped by previous experience, your understanding of financial products, confidence in revenue projections, and your board's attitude.
- **Your attitude to growth.** Not all organisations want to grow. Some prioritise depth of impact over breadth. If you are seeking growth, the challenge is finding finance that matches your stage and ambitions.
- **Your relationship with the public sector.** Heavy dependence on public sector contracts creates both opportunities and vulnerabilities, particularly during periods of austerity.

## Key terms to know

Understanding these terms will help you navigate conversations with investors:

| Term | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| **Investment readiness** | Having a proposition worth investing in, with the documentation and evidence to support it |
| **Patient investor** | An investor willing to wait longer for returns, accepting lower short-term performance |
| **Payment by results** | Funding linked to achieving specific measurable outcomes |
| **Working capital** | Money needed to cover day-to-day operational expenses |
| **Track record** | Evidence of past performance that demonstrates your ability to deliver |
