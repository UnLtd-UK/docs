## What does it mean to be investment ready?

Being investment ready means you have a proposition worth investing in -- and you can prove it. You know what you are asking for, you have the documentation and evidence to back up your plan, and you can present it convincingly to investors.

Investment readiness is a combination of:

- Knowing what you need and what you can reasonably expect from the market based on your size, past performance, and future plans.
- Having your materials packaged up and ready to "sell" to investors.
- Being able to answer tough questions with confidence, data, and conviction.

## Types of investment

There are two main types of social investment:

1. **Borrowing (debt)** -- Taking out a loan that you agree to repay over a set period of time. Most debt investments are paid back with interest. For example, an investor loans your organisation 10,000 pounds and you repay a total of 11,000 pounds at 229 pounds per month over four years.

2. **Shares (equity)** -- Selling shares in your organisation to an investor. Equity investors receive a share of any profits and get a say in how the organisation is run. For example, an investor pays 10,000 pounds to own 10% of your organisation.

These are most commonly provided through [social investment funds](https://www.goodfinance.org.uk/understanding-social-investment/social-investment-funds).

## Core questions every investor will ask

In nearly all investment conversations, you will face these fundamental questions:

- **How much do you need?**
- **What form of capital do you need it in?** (Debt, equity, revenue share, or a mix)
- **Why do you need it?**
- **What round is this, and how many rounds do you anticipate?** What do future rounds look like?
- **Who are you looking to raise from?** What type of investor, and what is the current size and interest from your network?

## Establishing your proposition

Having convincing, emotive, and robust answers to these questions is crucial:

- **Why are you addressing this problem?** Size the opportunity beyond TAM, SAM, and SOM -- include the cost to those experiencing the pain.
- **Why have you proposed this solution?** What have you built to tackle this problem and why is this your approach?
- **Why now?** What has changed in the market, technology, or human behaviour to make now the right time?
- **Why you?** What is special about you and your team that will allow you to win?
- **Do you have a profitable revenue model?** If not, do you already have a committed sales pipeline?

## In-depth investor questions

You do not need answers to every question below to be investment ready, but you should be able to address at least 75% of them. Social enterprises take time to get right. If you do not have something in place yet, show investors you have a plan for it.

### Plans for growth

Investment in early-stage social enterprises is typically used for growth -- whether explicit (funding marketing and sales) or implicit (building capacity to future-proof the business). Growth and sales performance drive up the value of the business and ensure a return on investment.

- What are your plans for growth? What is your vision of scale, and is it appropriate for the market?
- What pace of scaling will you adopt and why? Are you planning to scale by region, block contracting, or a growth rate percentage?
- Are there crucial pieces of the jigsaw missing? How do your plans or investment solve these challenges?
- Are your sales and cashflow forecasts sensible and sustainable? Are you overly reliant on one or two large clients?
- Is there a clear and sensible runway for launching new products, services, or markets? Is the development budgeted in your projections?
- What do you feel are the main risks in the growth plan? What are you doing to mitigate them?
- Have you set clear and achievable milestones for operational and organisational growth?
- To what extent is financial growth clearly tied to the scaling of social impact? Social investors are keen to see how profit and purpose are linked.
- What are the long-term plans? Do you want to position the business for acquisition by a larger player, or are you in it for the long term?

### Need for support

Many social investment programmes come with non-financial support -- consultancy, mentoring, coaching, or technical input. Your need for support may form part of the decision-making process.

- If partner organisations are required for routes to market or delivery, are the partnerships in place? Are the partners sufficiently strong, credible, and scalable?
- Does the organisation have additional external supporters such as accelerator programmes, advisors, or networks?
- Who are the existing investors or board members, and what experience do they bring?

### Business model

Your business model underpins your growth predictions. The two must be consistent.

- Is the business model documented and proven?
- What are the major risks? How are they being mitigated? Are you entering new markets, launching new products, or facing external influences like policy changes?
- Are there barriers to entry or a clear competitive advantage? How well do you understand your competition?
- Does all relevant IP reside cleanly with the venture? If not, how are investor interests protected?
- What is to stop a bigger player entering the market?
- Is the model truly scalable? Do margins increase as turnover grows? Is the market large enough? Will impact scale with the organisation?
- How is performance monitored, reported, and reviewed? Learn the key numbers before any pitch.
- Is there a track record of selling sustainably? Is income diverse, but not too diverse?
- What history do the individuals involved have of hitting sales targets in previous roles?

### Social impact

Social investors will scrutinise your social impact model. Understanding how impact and business activities relate to each other is core to an investable proposition.

- Is the social impact model (Theory of Change) documented, realistic, and impactful?
- Are the operational and measurement process plans clear and realistic?
- Is there a social mission lock in place? Is it appropriate for the business?
- How do you currently collect impact data? Is your approach appropriate?
- Is there a positive trend on impact metrics? Are you achieving your targets?
- Is the Theory of Change being executed as intended? Is social impact being delivered as anticipated?

### People and team

Investors invest in you as much as the business itself. Demonstrating that you have built a great team and are establishing your place in the market is crucial.

- Is your growth plan realistic given the team's skills and track record?
- What are the founders' strengths? What challenges do they face? Where are the skill gaps?
- Can you demonstrate focus and commitment to the plan, while remaining flexible and responsive to feedback?
- Are you and your team sufficiently financially literate to engage with investors and manage the business?
- How much are key management getting paid? Are all costs reflected in the financial projections?
- Are there skill gaps? Does management recognise them and show willingness to fill them?
- What is the quality of your board or advisers? How active are they? Is there evidence of diverse experience?
- How have you developed your brand and reputation? How are you engaging with key stakeholders?

### Financial and general questions

These questions are likely to come up during due diligence:

- Comment on any significant information in the balance sheet.
- Does the venture have the liquid assets to pay amounts outstanding? Can it pay trade creditors within 30 days? Can it make monthly loan or hire purchase payments?
- If debtors represent more than 60 days of sales, can you explain the ability to get people to pay? Are there disputes, financial trouble, or dissatisfaction?
- Why are you asking for this much? What is the company's valuation? Who are the current investors?
- What are your future investment needs, and why?
