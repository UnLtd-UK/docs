## Core Competence

Before you can sell effectively, you need to articulate what sets you apart. Your core competence is the answer to a simple question: **what do you have that no one else has?**

This might be a unique combination of skills, deep domain expertise, a proprietary methodology, or a distinctive relationship with your community. Whatever it is, name it clearly -- it becomes the foundation for everything that follows.

## Value Proposition Frameworks

Your value proposition tells a prospective buyer why they should choose you. It should answer two questions: "Why should I buy this product or service?" and "Why should I do anything at all?" A strong value proposition is a clear, specific statement about the tangible benefits of your offering, stated in terms your target customer understands and accepts.

Use whichever of the following frameworks works best for your context.

### Steve Leech Framework

Build your value proposition in one or two sentences:

**Sentence one:**

- Because we have *(skills, experience, knowledge, or other attribute)*
- We are able to *(provide a service, reach more people, or other deliverable)*
- This means *(benefits the client will value)*
- For *(the client)*

**Sentence two (optional):**

- Unlike *(primary competitive alternative)*,
- our service *(statement of primary differentiation).*

A simplified version: "Because we... we can... so you get..."

### Brand Ethos Canvas

Distil your proposition into a single sentence: **"We help X do Y by doing Z."**

This format forces you to be specific about your audience (X), the outcome you deliver (Y), and how you deliver it (Z).

### Positioning Statement

Use this template to craft a formal positioning statement:

> For *(target customer)* who *(statement of need or opportunity)*, our *(product or service name)* is a *(product category)* that *(statement of benefit)*.

### Golden Circle (Simon Sinek)

Simon Sinek's framework starts from the inside out: **Why, How, What.** "People don't buy what you do; people buy why you do it."

| Layer | Question | Example (Apple) |
|---|---|---|
| **Why** | Why does your organisation exist? | We believe in challenging the status quo and doing things differently. |
| **How** | How do you deliver on that belief? | Our products are beautifully designed and easy to use. |
| **What** | What do you actually make or sell? | We make computers. |

Work through these three layers for your own organisation. Start with your purpose (Why), then describe your approach (How), and finally state your products or services (What).

## Competitor Mapping

The price and quality of your products and services will always be evaluated against what else is available. There are three sources of competition to consider:

1. **Direct competitors** -- other products and services that solve the same problem.
2. **Internal solutions** -- workarounds that an individual or organisation has created to solve the problem themselves.
3. **Doing nothing** -- the prospect decides the problem is not important enough to address.

### Analysing Direct Competitors

For each competitor, capture:

| Competitor | Value Proposition | Price |
|---|---|---|
| *(Name)* | *(What they promise)* | *(Their price point)* |

### Analysing Internal Solutions

For each internal solution your prospects might already use, note:

| Internal Solution | Limitations | Estimated Cost |
|---|---|---|
| *(Description)* | *(e.g., high cost, inefficiency, poor quality)* | *(Approximate spend)* |

### Understanding Inaction

Consider why your prospects might choose to do nothing. What other problems do they perceive as more important? What makes existing workarounds feel "good enough"? Understanding inaction helps you craft messaging that creates urgency.

### Position Analysis Matrix

Map your competitors on a 2x2 matrix. Place **price** on one axis and a **key customer value driver** on the other (such as quality, efficiency, safety, or convenience). Plot each competitor in the appropriate quadrant to visualise where gaps and opportunities exist in the market -- and where you sit relative to the competition.

## Pricing

There are four approaches to consider when setting your price. You may use a combination depending on your product or market.

| Approach | Description |
|---|---|
| **Cost-based pricing** | Map out your products in detail. What are the variable costs? Calculate your current profit margin against fixed costs and set a price that covers both. |
| **Competition-based pricing** | Use your competitor mapping to benchmark your price against what others charge. |
| **Customer value-based pricing** | Set your price based on what the product or service is worth to the customer, not what it costs you to deliver. |
| **Contrast pricing** | If you offer multiple packages, use contrast to highlight value differences between tiers. See [Personal MBA on Contrast](https://personalmba.com/contrast/) for more on this technique. |

## Routes to Market

Routes to market are the methods you use to get your product, service, or value proposition in front of customers. Broadly, there are three ways to reach a customer:

- **Leverage existing assets** -- sell to people who already know you, such as existing customers, email subscribers, or past contacts. This is your lowest-hanging fruit.
- **Create relationships** -- build new connections through social media, networking events, partnerships, and referrals.
- **Rent access to attention** -- pay for advertising, sponsorship, or other channels that put your message in front of a new audience.

### Mapping Your Routes

Use a simple matrix to map your current and potential routes:

|  | Direct | Indirect |
|---|---|---|
| **Existing customers** | *(e.g., email campaigns, account management)* | *(e.g., resellers, partner referrals)* |
| **Potential customers** | *(e.g., social media, events)* | *(e.g., distributors, affiliate networks)* |

Think about how you currently reach customers and whether there are routes you have not yet explored. Are you reaching people directly through your own channels, or indirectly through a distributor or partner? Where are the untapped opportunities?

## Customer Profiling

Understanding your ideal customer is essential for focusing your sales effort. Consider the following dimensions:

- **Who is your perfect client?** Build a pen portrait that describes them in detail -- their role, their challenges, their goals, and what motivates their buying decisions.
- **Are they the budget holder?** If not, what is the decision-making process? How many people are involved in saying yes?
- **What type of organisation are they?** Are you targeting corporates, SMEs, public sector bodies, or charities? Each has different buying behaviours and timelines.
- **Can you segment the market?** Group potential customers by industry, size, geography, or need so you can tailor your approach.

### Consultative Selling Mindset

Adopt a consultative approach from the start: focus on finding out the customer's problem and providing a solution, rather than leading with features. This builds trust and positions you as a partner rather than a vendor.

## Customer Journey

Bring your customer profile and routes to market together by mapping the customer journey. This traces the path a potential buyer takes from first becoming aware of you through to making a purchase and becoming a repeat customer.

Key stages to map:

1. **Awareness** -- How do they first hear about you?
2. **Consideration** -- What information do they seek? What questions do they ask?
3. **Decision** -- What tips them from interested to committed? What barriers could stop them?
4. **Purchase** -- How easy is the buying process itself?
5. **Post-purchase** -- What happens after the sale? How do you build the relationship for future business?

Understanding each stage helps you identify where prospects drop off and where you can improve the experience to convert more leads into customers.
