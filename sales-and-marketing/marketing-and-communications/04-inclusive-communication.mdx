## Create a Culture of Safe Misunderstanding

Effective inclusive communication starts with accepting that misunderstandings are normal -- and creating an environment where they can be resolved without blame or defensiveness. Research by Damian Milton into the **Double Empathy problem** has shown that communication challenges between autistic and non-autistic people are mutual, not one-sided. Both parties may struggle to interpret each other's intentions, tone, and meaning.

When you recognise that miscommunication can happen in any interaction, you free yourself and others to clarify, ask questions, and try again without fear of judgement.

## Ask Good Questions

Communication can take subtle skill, patience, and artistry. One of the most powerful things you can do is shift your focus from giving answers to asking better questions.

### Questions That Can Trigger a Reactive State

Some questions, while natural, can unintentionally put the other person on the defensive or escalate tension:

- "What do you mean?"
- "Why did you say that?"
- "What do you want from me?"
- "Did you understand that?"

These are things you may genuinely want to know, but the way they are framed can contribute to a reactive state in the person you are speaking with.

### More Helpful Alternatives

Helpful questions are non-reactive, curious, and explorative rather than confrontational. They also reduce the level of perceived demand on the other person:

- "Can you tell me what you would like to have happen?"
- "Would you be happy to tell me what you were thinking just before you asked me that?"
- "What is your concern about this?"
- "I'm not sure what that word means to you in this context -- could you explain a bit more?"

<Tip>
**Create a helpful question sheet.** In moments of heightened emotion or stress, recognition is easier than recall. Having a printed or saved list of helpful questions nearby can support you in choosing better phrasing when it matters most.
</Tip>

## Understand That Different Realities Exist

Inclusive communication also means recognising that the person you are speaking with may be experiencing the world quite differently from you. Several factors shape how people perceive and process information:

- **Different sensory experiences** create different realities. What feels neutral to one person may be overwhelming or imperceptible to another.
- **Different processing speeds** can mean conversations are out of sync. One person may need more time to formulate a response.
- **Culture, life experience, body budget, and emotional state** are all used by the brain to frame, filter, and interpret incoming signals.
- **Time blindness, alexithymia, high sensitivity, and internal versus external locus of evaluation** are among the many factors that affect how people perceive salience and frame their reality.

Approaching every conversation with curiosity and humility -- rather than assuming your experience is the shared default -- is the foundation of genuinely inclusive communication.
