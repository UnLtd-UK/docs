## Getting Started with PR

Public relations can be one of the most cost-effective ways to raise the profile of your social enterprise. Before you begin any outreach, start with a fundamental question: **what do you want the result of this work to be?** Whether your goal is to attract investment, gain recognition, drive sales, or simply get your name in front of the right people, the answer will shape who you approach and how you build each relationship.

Keep in mind that PR for social enterprises is most often about you as the founder or social entrepreneur, rather than about the organisation itself -- though it can be either, depending on the story.

## Core PR Principles

### Know Your Audience

Think carefully about the audience of each publication and media outlet. Who are you trying to reach, and why? Tailor your message to what each outlet's readers, listeners, or viewers care about.

### Find the Right Angle

Everything can be news if you have the right angle. This is especially true with niche publications or local news outlets, which are often looking for stories. Consider what is happening in the news at the time, what your industry is discussing, and how your message fits into that broader conversation.

### Be Realistic About Frequency

Don't expect regular features from any single media source. As a general rule, you can expect to be published by a given outlet about once every six to twelve months at most.

### Diversify Your Media Approach

Be diverse in the types of media you approach. Think beyond newspapers -- consider podcasts, radio, television, online magazines, blogs, and industry publications.

### Use Your Network

Go through your network to establish warm leads to people who could get your story published. Opportunities are either reactive (responding to current events) or proactive (pitching your own story). Often the best results come from approaching people who have something in common with you.

### Build a Systematic Process

If you want to make PR a repeatable part of your operations, treat it like a structured process:

1. **Plan** who you want to approach.
2. **Define** how you will approach them.
3. **Develop** a common, proven method of outreach (for example, adapt your most successful previous pitch as a starting template).

### Finding Journalists

[Muck Rack](https://muckrack.com/) is a useful platform for finding journalists who cover topics relevant to your work. You can search by beat, publication, and location to identify the right contacts.

## Writing a Press Release

A well-structured press release makes it easy for journalists to pick up your story. Below is a step-by-step template you can follow.

### Choose Your Release Timing

Decide whether your press release is for **immediate release** or **embargoed** until a specific date. If embargoed, state the date and time clearly at the top. Always think about the publication's deadlines when choosing your timing.

### Headline

Your headline should be exciting and attention-grabbing. Use it as both the email subject line and the title at the top of your press release. A strong headline makes the difference between your email being opened or ignored.

### Opening Paragraph

Your first paragraph gives a concise summary of the story. Answer the key question: **who is this about, and what is happening?** Find the strongest story angle and lead with it. This is your chance to hook the journalist immediately.

### Second Paragraph

Expand on the remaining details -- the when, where, why, and how -- but with gradually decreasing importance. Write objectively in the third person rather than the first person. Journalists may cut from the bottom up if space is limited, so keep the most important information near the top.

### Quote

Include a direct quote from yourself or a key spokesperson. This is your chance to say exactly what you think in your own words. Journalists rarely make major changes to quotes, so use this space to be personal, emotive, and to reinforce a key message.

### Background Paragraph

By the fourth paragraph, provide additional background and context. If the journalist has limited space, this section may be cut -- so make sure it contains useful but not essential information.

### Closing

Finish your press release by directing readers to where they can find more information. Include your website address and a phone number.

Mark the end of the press release with the word **"Ends"** on its own line.

### Notes to Editors

This section sits below the main press release and includes:

- **Contact details** for the journalist to reach you or your press contact.
- **Background information** such as key statistics (for example, "It costs [amount] per day for [organisation] to provide its services").
- **Availability for interview** -- state who is available and when.
- **A high-quality photo** -- always include or offer one. Many stories are selected partly because a good image is available.

## Practical Tips for Success

- **Be available.** If a journalist phones, respond promptly -- they work to tight deadlines.
- **Be conscious of deadlines.** Research each outlet's publishing schedule before you pitch.
- **Follow up.** Consider phoning the paper or radio station to confirm they received your press release.
- **Invite journalists to events.** Smaller newsrooms may not be able to attend, which is why your press release should always tell the full story and include a photo.
- **Invest the time.** Getting PR right requires time, effort, consistency, and good matches between your story and each outlet.
