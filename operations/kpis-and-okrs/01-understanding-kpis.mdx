## What is a KPI?

There are many metrics in a company. All KPIs are metrics, but not all metrics are KPIs. KPIs are the metrics that tell your company whether it has achieved — or is likely to achieve — its objectives for a specific period. These objectives are linked to your overall company goals.

KPIs measure a quantity of something over a time period. The quantity must be measurable, objective and unambiguous. The time period must be meaningful in the context of managing to a specific outcome.

A KPI can only assess quality if the measure is quantifiable in some way — for example, when a satisfaction survey is converted to marks out of ten. KPIs relate to ongoing processes within the business. Targets for one-off events, activities or deliverables are milestones, not KPIs.

## Why Have KPIs?

Think of KPIs like the instruments in a car. As drivers, you create your own performance measures without realising. For example, "for the duration of the journey there should be no instances where the speed exceeds the limit" is an unwritten KPI when you want to avoid a speeding ticket. If you follow these performance measures, you are likely to achieve your objectives, and if you achieve those objectives, you move towards your goal.

In companies it works the same way. Company goals are converted into measurable objectives. KPIs help you understand and quantify your progress towards those objectives. They tell you what you are doing over time and what that activity is achieving in terms of results. By extrapolating activity and results, you can predict future performance, such as the likely end-of-year result.

## What Do Good KPIs Look Like?

Like objectives, KPIs should be **SMART**:

| Letter | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **S** | Specific, significant, stretching |
| **M** | Measurable, meaningful, motivational |
| **A** | Agreed upon, attainable, achievable, action-oriented |
| **R** | Realistic, relevant, reasonable, results-oriented |
| **T** | Time-based, time-bound, timely, trackable |

### Essential Characteristics

Your KPIs should also be:

- **Key** — does the KPI tell an important story about your business? There are plenty of performance indicators; you need the *key* ones
- **Simple** — people who are responsible for KPIs need to fully understand them and the rationale behind them
- **Quantifiable** — you must be able to put a number to the measurement, giving a clear indication of success or failure
- **Measurable** — it must be possible to acquire the data. If the data source is questionable or no-one believes the numbers, the KPI has no value
- **Objective** — everyone should see the same message, keeping the focus on problems and opportunities rather than personalities
- **Unambiguous** — if two people independently measured the same KPI for the same period, they should get the same answer
- **Appropriate** — the time period and units should be meaningful for the activity being measured
- **Timely** — the KPI should alert you in time to take action, not just tell you what already happened
- **Aligned to objectives and goals** — every KPI should trace back to a company objective and a corporate goal
- **Driving the right behaviour** — watch for mutually exclusive goals given to different people or functions

### Describing Your KPIs

Each KPI description should include:

- **A name** — this becomes part of your company language so everyone knows what is being discussed
- **The company goal it relates to** — so everyone knows why the KPI exists
- **The performance question** — what question is the KPI addressing (e.g. "to what extent are we meeting our delivery commitments?")
- **How data is collected** — where the data comes from, who collects it, how often, and who sees the reports
- **Who owns the KPI** — the person responsible for ensuring the target is achieved
- **What the target is** — what makes a "good" number, and what does a good or bad number mean for the organisation

## Where KPIs Fit in Your Organisation

Your company will have goals, objectives, strategies and processes. KPIs play a central role by providing concrete information on your progress towards those goals.

The hierarchy works as follows:

1. **Goals** describe the aspiration — where you want to be at a certain point in the future (e.g. "to be one of the top five sellers of bread in two years")
2. **Objectives** provide specific targets (e.g. "to sell at least 2,000 loaves per day in two years")
3. **Strategy** relates to *how* the objective will be reached — focusing on existing or new customers, different products, cost or premium positioning
4. **Processes** are informed by the strategy and provide the operational roadmap
5. **KPIs** are placed around the processes to ensure things are being done correctly, frequently enough, and are achieving progress towards objectives

## How KPIs Are Used

All KPIs share the same characteristics — they measure a quantity over time. The most common applications are:

| KPI Use | What It Does | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Input indicator** | Measures the amount of resource used to create a particular output or outcome | Are you using more flour and eggs than planned? |
| **Process indicator** | Gives insight into the efficiency of a particular activity or process | Do you burn more loaves than you would like? |
| **Output/outcome indicator** | Monitors the results of the process | Did every customer get everything they ordered? |

### Leading vs Lagging Indicators

Each of the above indicators is a **lagging indicator** — it tells you what has happened. However, these can be adapted to act as **leading indicators** that predict future outcomes:

| Type | What It Does | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Lagging** | States what has happened as a result of a process | Sales last month, units shipped today |
| **Leading** | Predicts the future outcome of a process | Trending sales data extrapolated to forecast year-end performance |

Leading indicators are derived in several ways:

- **Trends** — spotting patterns across multiple periods and extrapolating likely future results
- **Breaking down infrequent events** — monitoring smaller steps (e.g. tracking sales calls as a predictor of eventual orders)
- **Period comparisons** — comparing results to budgets or previous years to predict end-of-year outcomes

## The Unattainable Triangles

### Customer Triangle: Price, Speed and Quality

You can generally have any two of fast, cheap or good — but not all three. A fast-food restaurant serving quick, cheap food will have different KPIs to a Michelin-starred restaurant offering high quality at a premium price.

### Production Triangle: Cost, Throughput and Quality

When making a product or service, you can optimise for two of these dimensions but not all three. A high-cost product might be produced quickly *or* to a demanding specification, but not both.

Understanding how your organisation balances these trade-offs is part of your strategy. It will determine the KPIs you set, both to ensure your strategy is being followed and to measure whether it is working.

## What KPIs Will Not Do

KPIs will not run your business or make your decisions. They will make running the business a more informed activity, leading to better decisions. They are a tool, not a substitute for good management.

### The Human Factor

KPIs can get a bad name for people-related reasons. Watch for:

- **Poor execution** — a good process can be destroyed by poor staff selection, training, or ineffective line management
- **People going the extra mile** — excellent results may be down to loyal staff covering inadequacies in the process
- **Poor documentation or reporting** — the KPI may be perfect in planning but something goes wrong in implementation
- **Bad definition** — if you get the definition of quality wrong, undesirable outputs could reach your customers
- **Narrow perspective** — record order levels might mean the sales department is hitting its KPIs, but unprofitable orders are not good for the business
