## The KPI Development Process

Developing KPIs is not complicated. The complexity lies in framing the right KPIs and reducing them to the minimum while still tracking the right things. The process follows seven steps:

| Step | Description |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Identify and agree company goals and objectives | All KPIs link back to goals and objectives — these are prerequisites |
| 2. Objective breakdown | A high-level look at what needs to happen to achieve each objective |
| 3. Identify key company processes | Lay out the operational processes that deliver your objectives |
| 4. Match key processes to company objectives | Apply objectives to processes to see operational implications |
| 5. Drill down | Ensure KPIs cover all critical aspects — inputs, throughput and outputs |
| 6. Ownership and target numbers | Distil the list, assign ownership and set targets |
| 7. Deployment and review | Transfer ownership and ensure data collection and review is happening |

## Step 1: Company Goals and Objectives

Everything stems from your overall company goals. It makes sense to have everyone's efforts aligned with and contributing to these aims. Goals and objectives are typically set around key outcomes:

| Goal | Objective |
| --- | --- |
| "Have great social impact" | Create a validated Theory of Change by end of Q1; implement aligned measures by end of Q2 |
| "Become a profitable business and start to build reserves" | Increase profitable revenue by 30% for the year |
| "Increase customer satisfaction and retention" | Increase percentage of good and outstanding scores in customer survey by 25% |

**Goals** describe where you would like to be and when you want to get there. **Objectives** are the steps you must take to get there — what you need to do and by when.

## Step 2: Objective Breakdown

This step helps you take a big objective and break it into smaller, manageable chunks. Imagine having the celebration party for achieving the objective. What would need to be in place to make that success possible?

For example, to **increase profitable revenue by 30%** (assuming the same products and customer segment):

- Opportunity creation would have improved, increasing high-quality opportunities in the sales funnel
- Sales funnel conversion rates would have improved, making sales activity more efficient
- Delivery costs for higher order volumes would have been kept low enough to ensure good margins
- Invoicing and cash collection would have improved to handle higher volumes

This shows the burden is not solely on the sales team — delivery and finance also need to step up.

## Step 3: Key Company Processes

For KPI purposes, consider five main processes:

1. **Impact model** — your theory of change and the operational processes behind it
2. **Revenue process** — how you generate orders and revenue
3. **Delivery process** — how you fulfil customer commitments and turn orders into revenue
4. **Accounting process** — how you collect and account for revenue
5. **Staff model** — ensuring the right people are in the right roles with the right skills

List your key processes and add detail around the inputs, activities and outputs for each. For example, a bakery's baking process:

| Inputs | Activities | Outputs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| List of customer orders | Aggregate orders into bread types, create preparation and baking schedules | Delivery van loaded with completed orders |
| Raw materials from purchasing | Decide and allocate tasks to staff, prepare dough, bake bread | Delivery schedule and route plan |
| Consumables, power, etc. | Sort into orders, pack, create delivery notes, load van | |

## Step 4: Match Processes to Objectives

When you match company objectives to each key process, you start to understand the total workload implications. If you want a 30% increase in revenue, what does this mean for each process?

For the bakery example: more raw materials (more working capital), potentially more staff, extra ovens and cooling space. Bigger orders to the same customers might change pricing and discounting. More customers might mean more complex order sorting.

Key questions arise:

- Are the objectives achievable with current resources?
- Which parts of the company will take most responsibility for delivering the objectives?
- What must each process deliver for the business?

## Step 5: Drilling Down

For each high-level process, ask three key questions:

1. **What do you need to get right** to achieve the objectives?
2. **How many times or how often** do you need to get things right?
3. **How much should it cost** to get it right enough times?

At each process stage (input, activity, output), consider KPIs across three dimensions:

- **Quality** — are you effective in what you do, or is there significant waste?
- **Throughput** — how many do you achieve or consume in any period? Is the rate trending up or down?
- **Cost** — do you achieve the desired output at an affordable cost that fits your business model?

### Example: Bakery Process KPIs

| Dimension | Possible KPIs |
| --- | --- |
| Quality | Percentage of dough rejected; percentage of bread rejected; percentage of orders fully completed on time |
| Throughput | Value of orders fulfilled per period; percentage of staff and oven capacity utilised |
| Cost | Variable costs per period (staff, consumables, energy); raw material costs per period |

### Refining Your KPIs

When defining possible KPIs, consider:

- **Leading and lagging** — do your draft KPIs give information about both what has happened and what is likely to happen? Look at trends (direction of the metric over time) and run rates (throughput changes over time as a predictor of future results)
- **Activity and results** — for long sales cycles, monitoring activity (e.g. calls made) is more useful than waiting for end-of-period results. But ensure activity measures account for quality, throughput and cost
- **Ratios** — single metrics often fail to give the right information. Expressing costs as a ratio against output value (e.g. average variable cost percentage per order) is much more informative

## Step 6: Ownership and Target Numbers

### Removing Duplication

Revisit your draft KPIs to check for duplicates. Sometimes different metrics monitor the same question. Ask: "Does this KPI answer different questions to another KPI?" It may be prudent to track multiple KPIs initially and remove redundancies over time.

### Assigning Ownership

If a KPI is not someone's job to deliver, it probably will not happen. If a KPI is not useful in someone's job, it is probably not valid.

To assign KPIs to roles:

1. **Starting at the highest level**, define what each role will deliver for the business — what assets (outputs and outcomes) will that role create?
2. **Match the assets** identified with your draft KPIs
3. **Repeat for each department** — what does each role deliver for the department?

People need KPIs suited to their job and appropriate for their role and authority. They need the authority, knowledge, skills and resources to act when a KPI is not being achieved.

### Setting Target Numbers

Once KPIs have been allocated to roles, set the target figures. These tell you whether the right things are being done, that enough is being done, and whether required results are being achieved.

The numbers should link back to overall objectives: if all KPIs are achieved, objectives should also be achieved, and the company should have moved towards its goals.

## Step 7: Deployment and Review

Introducing new KPIs involves change, so deployment needs care and thoughtful change management:

- **Early involvement** — get managers and staff involved in the KPI development process to foster ownership
- **Set expectations clearly** — take time to explain what is expected of each individual
- **Over-communicate** — take every opportunity to explain the what, why, how and implications

### Tips for Successful Deployment

- Invest time to make sure everything is understood, accepted and working
- Make sure everyone has the skills, resources and authority to take necessary actions
- Review plans to cascade KPIs if there are teams or layers of management
- Recognise that KPIs take time — time to implement, collect data, and review. Demonstrate that this time matters
- Use the KPIs yourself. If leadership does not bother, neither will others

## Key Messages to Remember

**KPIs are not a substitute for good management.** They provide information — what you do with it is up to your staff, managers and leadership team.

**See KPIs in the round.** For every revenue increase there is a cost increase. Set KPIs that answer all the questions you need to ask.

**KPIs should tell you everything you need to know to sleep at night.** If there is still a niggling doubt, fix it by adjusting existing KPIs or introducing new ones.

**KPIs must be aligned to your goals for a specific time period.** Company priorities change, and KPIs must change with them.

**KPIs must be clear to the person they are assigned to.** If the KPI is not clear, it will fail. Keep them simple and invest time in a proper handover.

**KPIs must be measured, reported and acted upon.** When a KPI is used properly and overtly, people take it and the targets it represents more seriously.

**No part of the business should succeed while the business fails.** When allocating KPIs, ask: "Is it possible for someone to achieve all their KPI targets while the company fails to meet its goals?"

**Watch for unintended consequences.** A target of zero customer complaints could result in managers hiding issues rather than solving them.

**Review KPIs regularly.** What is right for your business now might change in the future. Never be afraid to add, change or delete KPIs.
