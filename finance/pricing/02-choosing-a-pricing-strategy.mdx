## The Three Foundations

Every pricing strategy builds on three areas: **customer**, **cost**, and **competition**. Once you are confident in these fundamentals, you can explore more advanced approaches.

As a general rule, most businesses should be charging more than they think — and should not feel worried about doing so.

## Bottom-Up: Cost-Based Pricing

Cost-based pricing is the simplest model to start with. You work out your variable costs per unit, add a profit margin, and arrive at a price.

**Example — Selling a bar of chocolate:**

| Component | Amount |
|---|---|
| Variable cost to produce | 40p |
| Profit margin (40%) | 27p |
| **Selling price** | **67p** |

This is a great starting point, but you need industry knowledge to make it work well — what constitutes a reasonable profit margin, a reasonable cost base, and what margin you need to overcome the fixed costs of the business.

**Steps to apply cost-based pricing:**

1. Map out your products or services in detail — what is the customer paying for? What are the variable costs?
2. Plot your variable costs and identify your current profit margin.
3. Compare this against your fixed costs to ensure viability.

## Top-Down: Value-Based and Competitor-Based Pricing

### Customer Value-Based Pricing

Rather than starting from your costs, value-based pricing starts from what the customer is willing to pay. This approach considers the perceived value of your product or service in the market.

For a visual overview of value-based pricing strategies, see [this pricing strategies slide deck](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/robinchu_first-5-out-of-10-only-pricing-strategiesrobin-ugcPost-6564444065209872384-ndke/).

### Competitor-Based Pricing

This requires research: spend time finding out how much your competitors charge for similar or competing services. Once you have this information, decide how you will position yourself:

- Will you be a **budget option** that undercuts the market?
- Will you be a **premium or luxury offering**?

Once you have established your price point, the aim is to keep your variable costs below that price while maintaining a healthy profit margin — effectively the reverse of bottom-up pricing.

## Contrast Pricing

If you offer several packages or tiers, contrast pricing encourages customers to choose the option you want them to buy. Think of the small, medium, and large popcorn at the cinema — the medium looks like the best deal because of how it compares to the other two.

For more on contrast pricing, see [The Personal MBA: Contrast](https://personalmba.com/contrast/).

## Freemium

Freemium is a model in which you offer a basic plan, product, or service for free so that users can try it out. At the same time, you offer a more complete or advanced version for a fee — the premium option.

This strategy is common online because it keeps customer acquisition costs low while creating potential for sales growth over time. It works best for digital products and services where the marginal cost of each free user is very low.

For more on the freemium model, see [Business Model Analyst: Freemium](https://businessmodelanalyst.com/freemium-business-model/).

## Sliding-Scale (Robin Hood) Pricing

In this approach you charge customers who can afford to pay more a higher rate, while those who cannot afford your standard rate pay less or receive your service for free. This model is particularly popular among social enterprises because it allows your services to reach those who need them most.

It is a complex model to get right — you need to balance the higher-paying customers against the subsidised ones to remain financially sustainable — but it can be a powerful way to align your pricing with your social mission.
