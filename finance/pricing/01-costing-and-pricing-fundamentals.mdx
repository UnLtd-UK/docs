## Why Costing and Pricing Matter

Setting the right price for a new product or service can be one of the hardest tasks you face as a business owner. Working out your costs is relatively straightforward, but the price you charge is ultimately set by the marketplace.

Your price needs to cover all your costs and allow for a profit, but it must also take account of what competitors are charging and how much customers are prepared to pay. Setting too high a price can lead to lost sales. Setting too low a price will reduce your profits and may result in the business failing.

Understanding the impact of pricing on profitability helps you choose the best pricing strategy for your business.

## Key Terminology

### Costs

- **Direct costs (variable costs):** The cost incurred in producing and supplying your product or service. These vary in direct proportion to the number of units produced and include raw materials, bought-in components or goods, and direct wages (staff employed specifically to produce the product or service).
- **Fixed costs (overheads):** Costs incurred regardless of whether any products are produced or sold. These include the owner's salary or drawings, employee salaries, rent, rates, insurance, and depreciation of fixed assets such as machinery and equipment.

### Pricing Methods

- **Cost-plus pricing:** A traditional method often used for products. You apply a percentage markup on top of direct costs to cover fixed costs and make a profit.
- **Value-based pricing:** Setting a price based on what the market will bear. Factors such as fashion, convenience, and market share affect the price level you can achieve.

### Profit Measures

- **Breakeven point:** The point at which revenue from sales exactly equals all costs. Above this level you make a profit; below it you make a loss.
- **Gross profit:** Selling price minus the direct costs of making a product or delivering a service.
- **Operating profit:** Gross profit minus fixed costs.
- **Contribution:** As long as you sell a product for more than its direct cost, each sale contributes towards covering your fixed costs and then towards operating profit. If you sell a range of products, compare the gross profit each generates — the highest-volume product may not have the highest contribution, but it could still help attract and retain customers.

## Understanding Your Costs

In essence, costing and pricing is simple: work out all your costs and charge a price higher than those costs. In practice, it can be harder to identify exactly which costs are variable and which are fixed.

All costs ultimately need to be covered by your sales. The more you sell, the less contribution is needed from each unit to cover overheads. Costing therefore depends on estimating how many products or hours you can sell.

### Costs for a Production Business

You need to know your direct costs per unit and your actual or expected fixed costs. Divide your total fixed costs by the number of units you expect to produce, then add the direct cost per unit to get your total cost per unit.

**Example — Cabinet maker producing desks to order:**

The business expects to make and sell 100 desks per year. Direct material costs (wood, glue, screws, packaging) are £50 per desk. Total annual fixed costs (including the owner's time) are £30,000, giving a fixed cost of £300 per desk and a total cost of £350 per desk.

If the business makes more desks, the direct cost stays the same but the fixed cost per desk falls:

| Units Made | Direct Cost per Unit | Fixed Cost per Unit | Total Cost per Unit |
|---|---|---|---|
| 100 | £50 | £300 | £350 |
| 150 | £50 | £200 | £250 |
| 200 | £50 | £150 | £200 |

The business could sell desks at a lower price using cost-plus pricing, or maintain the price and achieve a much higher profit using value-based pricing.

### Costs for a Service Business

A service business needs to calculate its average daily or hourly cost. Not all working hours are productive — you need to allow time for marketing, administration, holidays, and illness.

**Example — Photographer:**

Total annual overheads are £30,000. After allowing for holidays, 200 days will be sold each year at an average of five chargeable hours per day (1,000 productive hours per year). This gives a daily cost of £150 and an hourly cost of £30. Add direct costs (travel and printing) and a profit margin to determine a selling price.

## Setting Your Prices

Follow two main steps:

1. Determine the costs of producing your product or delivering your service.
2. Set a price high enough to cover costs, but low enough to be competitive.

**Example — Photographer (cost-plus approach) for a one-day assignment (7.5 hours):**

| Component | Amount |
|---|---|
| Time (7.5 × £30) | £225 |
| Direct costs (travel + printing) | £40 |
| Profit margin (20%) | £53 |
| VAT (20%) | £63.60 |
| **Total price** | **£381.60** |

If market research shows the going day rate for photographers in the area is £500, a value-based approach would earn considerably more.

Research your market carefully to determine the price range you can charge. Once your business is established, you can adjust prices and review the effect on demand. Ultimately, the price you charge depends on how much the customer is prepared to pay.

## How Pricing Affects Profit

Small changes in price can have a dramatic effect on profitability. The table below shows the impact at the same sales volume:

| Selling Price | Direct Cost | Gross Profit per Unit | Units Sold | Total Gross Profit | Fixed Costs | Operating Profit |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| £45 | £10 | £35 | 1,000 | £35,000 | £35,000 | £0 (breakeven) |
| £50 | £10 | £40 | 1,000 | £40,000 | £35,000 | £5,000 |
| £55 | £10 | £45 | 1,000 | £45,000 | £35,000 | £10,000 |

Raising the price from £50 to £55 doubles the operating profit.

## Pricing and Sales Volumes

Selling more units does not automatically mean higher profit. The table below shows how many units you need to sell at different prices to achieve the same operating profit of £10,000:

| Selling Price | Direct Cost | Gross Profit per Unit | Units Required | Total Gross Profit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| £45 | £10 | £35 | 1,286 | £45,000 |
| £50 | £10 | £40 | 1,125 | £45,000 |
| £55 | £10 | £45 | 1,000 | £45,000 |

At £45, you need to sell 40% more units than at £55 to achieve the same profit. This matters particularly if you have limited production capacity.

## Pricing Against Competitors

Many start-ups let their competitors set the price, thinking that undercutting will bring success. This approach often fails because a new business lacks the economies of scale to make its price truly competitive, and can end up unable to make a profit.

Price is important to customers, but products and services can be differentiated in many other ways — including pre-sales service and post-sale support.

## Other Pricing Considerations

### Flexible Pricing

You might offer special prices across a range of products at different times of the year.

### Volume Discounts

Offering price discounts for bulk purchases can bring several advantages:

- The lower gross profit per unit is offset by higher total gross profit from the bulk order.
- There are lower costs associated with supplying one larger order to one customer.
- Higher total sales can lead to lower unit costs through increased productivity and buying power, allowing you to increase gross profit even at a lower average selling price.

### Total Customer Value

By understanding the total potential value of an individual customer, you may achieve higher gross profit by selling a range of products to that customer at lower individual prices. You could negotiate fixed prices based on the customer committing to a minimum volume over an agreed term as your sole or preferred supplier. Although individual product margins are lower, total gross profit from the full range is greater.

### Variable Pricing

You might charge different prices for premium services at unsociable hours, or charge variable rates depending on the season — for example, if your business is in the tourism sector.
