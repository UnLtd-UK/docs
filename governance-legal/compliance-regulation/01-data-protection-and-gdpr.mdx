## What the GDPR Means for Your Business

The General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) was originally introduced as part of European law and was retained as the UK GDPR when the UK left the EU. It requires your organisation to comply with data protection principles that give individuals greater control over their personal data.

If your business collects, stores or uses any information about identifiable people, the GDPR applies to you.

## Understanding Personal Data

"Personal data" means any information relating to an identified or identifiable person. Even where your records do not include names, the data is still personal data if it could be used to work out someone's identity.

Examples of information that might identify an individual include:

- Date of birth
- Postal address
- IP address
- Computer cookies
- Any anonymised identifier that can be traced back to a person, such as an account code or online username

## The Six Lawful Bases for Processing

"Processing" personal data refers to any operation carried out on the data, including collecting, recording, organising, storing, altering, accessing, using, sharing or destroying it.

Under the GDPR, you can only process personal data if you have one or more of six lawful bases:

| Lawful Basis | When It Applies |
|---|---|
| **Consent** | An individual has explicitly agreed to your request to process their data for a specific purpose |
| **Contract** | You have a contract with the individual and need to process their data to fulfil it |
| **Legal obligation** | You are legally required to process the data (e.g. employee records for taxation) |
| **Legitimate interests** | You or a third party has a legitimate interest that makes processing necessary (e.g. fraud prevention, IT security) |
| **Vital interests** | Processing is necessary to protect someone's life |
| **Public task** | You are exercising official authority or carrying out a task in the public interest |

### Getting Consent Right

Consent is only one of six lawful bases, so it is not always required. It is more likely to be needed when you process sensitive data such as religious or political beliefs, ethnicity, sexual orientation or medical information.

When consent is required:

- You must obtain a positive opt-in action from the individual (pre-ticked boxes are prohibited)
- You must clearly explain the data processing you intend to carry out
- If you process data for more than one purpose, you must explain and obtain consent for each purpose separately

## Data Protection Principles

When processing personal data, you must comply with these principles:

- **Transparency, fairness and lawfulness** in your handling and use of personal data
- **Purpose limitation** --- only process data for the purposes you have stated
- **Data minimisation** --- ensure data is adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary
- **Accuracy** --- keep personal data accurate and up to date
- **Storage limitation** --- retain data only for as long as necessary for its original purpose
- **Security** --- ensure the security of personal data
- **Accountability** --- keep records that demonstrate your compliance with all of the above

## Individuals' Rights

The GDPR gives individuals several rights regarding their personal data:

- The right to be informed about data you hold about them and to access it
- The right to have inaccurate or incomplete data corrected
- The right to have their data erased in certain circumstances
- The right to withdraw consent for processing

## Putting Compliance into Practice

The GDPR does not prescribe a specific list of measures. Instead, it requires you to take measures that are "risk-based and proportionate." A business processing large amounts of sensitive data needs a more extensive framework than a small organisation processing minimal personal data.

Practical compliance measures include:

- Having a written data protection policy
- Displaying a clear privacy notice explaining your data processing activities
- Regularly reviewing and updating your data security measures
- Carrying out a data protection impact assessment before starting new data-processing activities
- Fully documenting all data-processing activities, reviews and decisions

## Handling Data Breaches

A personal data breach is any security breach that leads to personal data being lost, destroyed, corrupted or disclosed. You must:

- Record all personal data breaches, including their details, effects and your response
- Report serious breaches to the [Information Commissioner's Office (ICO)](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/report-a-breach/personal-data-breach/personal-data-breaches-a-guide) within 72 hours
