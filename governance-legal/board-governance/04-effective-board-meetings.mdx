## Getting the Most from Your Board Meetings

Board meetings are where governance happens in practice. Well-run meetings lead to better decisions, stronger accountability and a more engaged board. Poorly run meetings waste everyone's time and can lead to poor governance outcomes.

## Before the Meeting

### Check Your Articles

Your company's Articles of Association set the rules for board meetings, including:

- Minimum and maximum number of directors
- Quorum (minimum number of directors required to make decisions)
- Whether meetings can be held by video conference
- The chair's casting vote provisions
- How resolutions are passed

Always refer to your Articles when planning meetings.

### Set a Clear Agenda

A well-structured agenda keeps meetings focused and productive. A standard board meeting agenda should include:

1. **Welcome and apologies** --- note who is present and absent
2. **Declarations of interest** --- any conflicts relevant to agenda items
3. **Approval of previous minutes** --- review and formally sign off
4. **Matters arising** --- follow up on actions from the previous meeting
5. **Company reports** --- covering financial performance, social impact and other key areas
6. **Strategic items** --- substantive topics requiring board discussion or decision
7. **Any other business** --- items not on the formal agenda
8. **Date of next meeting**

### Distribute Papers in Advance

Send board papers at least one week before the meeting. Directors should read and prepare questions in advance --- this makes the meeting itself much more productive.

## During the Meeting

### Chairing Effectively

The chair is responsible for:

- Ensuring all directors have the opportunity to contribute
- Keeping discussion focused and on track
- Managing time so all agenda items are covered
- Summarising decisions and actions clearly
- Using a casting vote if necessary (where permitted by the Articles)

### Making Decisions

Boards make decisions through **resolutions**:

| Type | When Used | Requirement |
|---|---|---|
| **Ordinary resolution** | Routine decisions (e.g. appointing directors, approving accounts) | Simple majority of those voting |
| **Special resolution** | Major changes (e.g. changing Articles, changing company name) | 75% or more of those voting |
| **Written resolution** | When a formal meeting is not practical | Circulated to all eligible directors for signature |

## Taking Minutes

Minutes are the formal record of board decisions and are a legal requirement. Good minutes should include:

### What to Record

- Date, time and location of the meeting
- Names of those present and any apologies
- Declarations of interest
- Key discussion points (not a verbatim transcript)
- Decisions made and the rationale behind them
- Actions agreed, with the responsible person and deadline
- Any dissenting views, if a director wishes to have their disagreement recorded

### Practical Tips

- **Assign a minute-taker** --- this can be a company secretary, board member or external person
- **Include actions and deadlines** --- use footnotes or a separate action tracker
- **Be concise but complete** --- record enough to demonstrate that directors fulfilled their duties
- **Circulate promptly** --- send draft minutes within a week of the meeting
- **Approve formally** --- minutes should be approved at the next board meeting

## Meeting Frequency and Format

There is no single right answer for how often your board should meet. Consider:

- **Quarterly meetings** are common for established organisations
- **Monthly meetings** may be appropriate for early-stage or fast-growing organisations
- **Ad hoc meetings** can be called for urgent matters
- **Video conferencing** can be used if your Articles allow it, making it easier for directors to attend

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

- **Lack of preparation** --- directors arriving without reading the papers
- **Dominated discussions** --- one or two people doing all the talking
- **Missing quorum** --- making decisions without enough directors present (these decisions are invalid)
- **No follow-up** --- actions agreed but never tracked or completed
- **Rubber-stamping** --- approving everything without genuine discussion or challenge
- **Avoiding difficult conversations** --- failing to address poor performance or strategic concerns
