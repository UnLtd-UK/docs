## Planning Your Workshop

A well-planned workshop creates the conditions for productive, creative collaboration. Before you begin designing the agenda, establish the fundamentals.

### Team Size and Structure

Design workshops work best with small, focused teams:

- **4 to 6 participants per team** — large enough for diverse perspectives, small enough for everyone to contribute
- **3 to 4 teams per workshop** — enough groups to generate varied ideas and enable cross-team sharing
- **20 to 24 participants in total** — the practical upper limit for a single facilitator to manage effectively

Arrange participants into team-based working tables. Where possible, mix roles, backgrounds, and levels of experience within each team to maximise diversity of thinking.

### Workshop Layout

Set up the room for active, collaborative work:

- Team-based working tables with enough space for spreading out materials
- Sufficient wall space for teams to hang work in progress and display large-format design tools
- A central area or screen for facilitator presentations and group discussions

## Materials and Equipment

Provide the following materials for each team:

- **Design tool templates** — printed at A0 or A1 size where applicable
- **Flipcharts and easels** — for capturing group discussions and ideas
- **Post-it sticky notes** — in various colours (yellow, pink, green, orange)
- **Marker pens, Sharpies, and pencils** — bold markers are easier to read from a distance
- **Sheets of paper and card** — in different colours for sketching and visual work
- **Tape and adhesive putty** — for hanging tools and work in progress on walls
- **Prototyping materials** (for two-day workshops) — cardboard, foam, scissors, glue, string, modelling clay, fabric scraps, and any other low-cost craft materials that help teams build rough models of their ideas

## Facilitator Tips

### Be Flexible with Timing

Different groups work at different paces. Some teams will take longer than planned on certain exercises but move quickly through others. Monitor progress across all teams and adjust exercise timings to maintain the overall workshop schedule. The agenda is a guide, not a rigid script.

### Encourage Experimentation

Design thinking is a creative activity. Encourage participants to try things that feel unfamiliar. Remind them that there are no wrong answers during ideation, and that rough, imperfect work is more valuable than polished work that never gets tested.

### Build Confidence Through Structure

While flexibility is important, clear structure gives participants confidence. Introduce each exercise with a brief explanation of what it is, why you are doing it, and how long they have. Bring the group back together at key moments to share progress and insights across teams.

### Know When to Step In

As a facilitator, your role is to guide the process, not to contribute ideas. Step in when a team is stuck or when a dominant personality is preventing others from contributing. Step back when creative energy is flowing.

## The "I Liked / I Learned / I Wish" Reflection Method

Individual and group reflection at the end of each workshop is an essential part of the learning process. The most effective method uses Post-it notes on three reflection charts rather than individual assessment questionnaires.

### How to Run It

1. Place three large sheets on the wall, each labelled with one prompt:
   - **I Liked...** — what participants enjoyed or found valuable
   - **I Learned...** — new insights, skills, or knowledge gained
   - **I Wish...** — what participants would change or do differently next time
2. Give each participant a stack of Post-it notes and a few minutes of quiet writing time.
3. Ask participants to place their completed notes on the appropriate chart.
4. Read through the posted comments together as a group, discussing themes and patterns.

This method surfaces honest, specific feedback and gives you actionable insights for improving future workshops. It also gives participants a sense of closure and shared ownership of the learning experience.

## Framing "How Might We" Statements

Before diving into design activities, you need a clear challenge to work on. "How Might We" (HMW) statements are one of the most effective ways to frame a design challenge.

### Why This Format Works

Every problem is an opportunity for design. By framing your challenge as a "How Might We" question, you set yourself up for innovative solutions. The format works because:

- **"How"** suggests that a solution is possible
- **"Might"** gives you permission to explore broadly without committing to a single answer
- **"We"** establishes that this is a collaborative effort

### Tips for Writing Good HMW Statements

- **Avoid being too broad.** "How might we fix everything?" gives you no direction. "How might we make it easier for first-time users to find our service?" gives your team a clear focus.
- **Avoid embedding solutions.** "How might we build an app?" assumes the answer. "How might we help people access our service when they need it most?" opens up possibilities.
- **Frame around people.** The best HMW statements centre the people you are designing for, not the organisation's internal needs.

A well-framed HMW statement becomes the anchor for your entire workshop, guiding every activity from stakeholder mapping through to prototyping and prioritisation.
