## Taking Design From Theory to Practice

Design thinking frameworks only create value when they are applied to real problems with real people. This guide covers two essential dimensions of practical design work: using design tools to understand and improve customer experiences, and collaborating effectively with the stakeholders who are affected by your designs.

Whether you are mapping a customer journey for a social enterprise or co-designing a service improvement with the people who use it, the principles are the same: start with empathy, involve the right people, and iterate based on what you learn.

## Case Study: Customer Journey Mapping for a Social Enterprise

### The Moments Cafe Project

Moments Cafe, a social enterprise, worked through a multi-session design process to develop their sales approach and business development portfolio. The project followed a structured sequence of sessions, each building on the last.

### Project Structure

| Session | Focus | Activities |
|---------|-------|-----------|
| **Session 1: Kick-Off** | Define the challenge and map current state | Develop a "How Might We" statement, establish existing materials, identify the customer journey |
| **Session 2: Stakeholder Mapping** | Understand the customer | Develop personas for five typical customers, considering sentiment, buying power, motivation, and buy-in |
| **Session 3: Prototyping** | Design new resources | Using knowledge from sessions 1 and 2, prototype resources for each stage of the customer journey |
| **Build Phase** | Create and adapt | Build new resources, adapt existing ones, establish a test-and-learn approach |
| **Delivery and Testing** | Implement and iterate | Trial the new approach with real customers and refine based on feedback |

### Developing the HMW Statement

The team developed their "How Might We" statement in the kick-off session:

> *"How might we take what we've got, adapt it and grow our offer to increase our sales and build the right tool for our customers and ourselves?"*

This statement acknowledged a crucial insight: the organisation already had most of what it needed. The challenge was not to start from scratch but to adapt and deploy existing resources more effectively.

### Mapping the Customer Journey

The team identified seven types of resources that Moments Cafe already had:

1. **Traditional marketing** -- used prominently at the start of the process (pre-interest and interest stages)
2. **Market knowledge** -- understanding of the sector and its dynamics
3. **Sector support and relationships** -- connections with partners and stakeholders
4. **Business systems and processes** -- operational infrastructure
5. **Demonstrating the "why"** -- the social mission and evidence behind it
6. **Sales approach** -- how the team engaged with potential customers
7. **Impact data** -- collected throughout the process but not used to its full effect

By mapping these resources against the customer journey, the team could see where resources were concentrated and where gaps existed.

### Rose, Thorn, and Bud Analysis

At each stage of the customer journey, the team used the Rose/Thorn/Bud framework to identify what was working, what was not, and what had potential.

#### Pre-Interest Stage

| Type | Finding |
|------|---------|
| **Roses** (working well) | Getting in front of the right people to share ideas; showcasing existing strengths |
| **Thorns** (pain points) | Time it takes to research interest; the process may be too passive at the start |
| **Buds** (opportunities) | Lots of resources and mechanisms available to generate interest |

#### Interest Stage

| Type | Finding |
|------|---------|
| **Roses** | Collecting lots of useable data; getting customers to ask the right questions |
| **Thorns** | Institutional knowledge not systematised; social impact packaging needs work; evidence for the "why" is scattered |
| **Buds** | Need to learn how to pre-empt customer questions and keep them empowered |

#### Sign-Up Stage

| Type | Finding |
|------|---------|
| **Thorns** | Information sharing feels disjointed rather than planned; no clear picture of likely timeframes to convert |

#### Come Back Stage

| Type | Finding |
|------|---------|
| **Thorns** | Support from the broader network is underutilised |

#### Overarching Findings

| Type | Finding |
|------|---------|
| **Roses** | "We have 90% of what we need"; proven delivery model backed by research |
| **Thorns** | Lack of stakeholder nurturing due to busyness; not enough directionality in current approaches |
| **Buds** | Better delegation and team empowerment; ability to shape offerings against customer needs; need for a lead-qualifying tool; creating versatile impact reports linked to customer-specific data |

### Key Insights and Outcomes

The customer journey mapping process produced several actionable insights:

- **Adapt, do not reinvent.** The majority of existing tools simply needed to be used in the right way at the right time.
- **Create versatile impact reports.** Demonstrating impact data against area-specific health data would be a powerful sales tool.
- **Delegate to the team.** Better delegation could free up leadership capacity for the sales pipeline.
- **Evidence of investment matters.** Customers need to see that the organisation invests in quality.
- **Establish a service design agreement.** A clear process statement communicates confidence and professionalism.
- **Guide the customer's questions.** Help customers ask "What difference will this make for us?" rather than "Is this for us?"

## Collaborative Design with Stakeholders

### The Coproduction Ladder

Effective collaboration exists on a spectrum. At the lower end, stakeholders are informed or consulted. At the higher end, they are genuine partners in design and decision-making. The coproduction ladder describes increasing levels of involvement and empowerment.

As you move up the ladder, collaboration becomes more meaningful. Both parties bring something to the table to achieve a shared goal. The aim is to move toward higher levels of involvement wherever possible, recognising that meaningful collaboration leads to better outcomes for everyone.

### Three Quick Ways to Collaborate

If you are short on time but want to start involving stakeholders, try these approaches:

**1. Phone a stakeholder.**
Before you develop an idea, run it past someone who will be affected by it. A quick phone call can save weeks of work on a solution that misses the mark.

**2. Invite stakeholders to the planning stage.**
Before you write the script, design the service, or finalise the approach, invite one or two stakeholders to be part of the initial idea-planning session. Early involvement shapes better outcomes.

**3. Form a working group.**
If you keep hearing the same feedback, bring those voices together. A small working group of people with lived experience can guide projects and connect you with others who share their interests and skills.

### Defining Roles and Responsibilities

Clearly defined roles promote more meaningful collaboration. When people know what is expected of them, they can contribute with confidence.

For each participant in a collaborative project, establish:

- **What they are responsible for** -- specific duties and contributions
- **Who they report to** -- the project lead or team they work with
- **How much time is involved** -- so expectations are realistic
- **What support they will receive** -- training, supervision, expenses, and ongoing guidance
- **How their input will be used** -- so they know their contribution matters

### Tips for Keeping Conversations Productive

Collaborative sessions can go off track, especially when participants bring strong personal experiences. Use these techniques to keep things focused:

**The car park.** Set up a flipchart labelled "Car Park" where participants can write down thoughts and insights that are important but not directly relevant to the current discussion. This acknowledges their input without derailing the session.

**Designated signpost.** Assign a person to signal when the conversation drifts off topic. They might use a hand signal or a gentle verbal cue. Establish this role transparently at the start of the session: "I am the person who will give this signal if we are going off track. When I do, let us get back on topic."

**Post-it notes for stray ideas.** Encourage participants to write down any ideas or thoughts that are not related to the current exercise. These can be reviewed later and may become valuable inputs for future work.

### Using Personas in Collaborative Settings

Personas are fictional but research-based profiles that represent the range of people affected by your product or service. They bring stakeholder needs into the room in a tangible way.

**How to build personas:**

1. Run focus groups or conduct interviews with a range of stakeholders about the experience you want to understand.
2. Look for common themes across what you hear.
3. Create a composite profile with a name, background, feelings and motivations, needs, and a representative quote.
4. Bring personas into planning and solution-focused sessions as reference points.
5. Always make clear that personas are fictional composites and can never be tied back to any real person.

Personas work especially well when presented alongside case studies and journey maps, grounding abstract discussions in specific human experiences.

### Using Journey Mapping in Collaborative Settings

Journey mapping is a powerful way to understand stakeholder experiences across time. Unlike surveys, which often produce closed responses with limited nuance, journey maps capture both practical and emotional experiences at each stage of a process.

**Journey map structure:**

| Element | Description |
|---------|------------|
| **Horizontal headings** | Chronological steps in the process or journey (e.g., Before, Begin, During, After) |
| **Vertical headings** | Points of feedback to capture at each step (e.g., What happened? How did you feel? What were the barriers? What would help?) |

**At each stage, ask participants:**

- What was the main point of contact with services?
- What happened for you? What did you do?
- What were your thoughts when this happened? What was helpful and what was not?
- How did you feel at this time? Was the overall experience positive, neutral, or negative?
- What were the barriers to getting your needs met?
- How would you solve the problem? What would help fix this?

Journey maps can be completed individually on printed A3 sheets or collaboratively on a large board where participants place post-it notes. The group can then visually map where the main issues cluster and use dot-voting to prioritise which areas to address first.

### Getting-to-Know-You and Reflective Exercises

Starting collaborative sessions with a brief icebreaker builds trust and puts participants at ease. These do not need to be complex:

- Ask each person to share their name, where they travelled from, and a light personal detail (such as the last good programme they watched)
- Use a "Yes and..." exercise: in pairs, one person starts a story and their partner builds on it using "Yes and..." to practise collaborative thinking
- Use reflective prompt cards that let participants choose which questions to answer, rather than working through a checklist

At the end of sessions, reflective exercises help capture meaningful feedback. Ask participants to share their overall experience, any concerns, and their hopes for what happens next.

### Ensuring Inclusion

Inclusive design is not optional. When you involve stakeholders, you must actively consider who is in the room and who is missing.

**Principles for inclusion:**

- **Flexibility first.** Offer different ways to participate -- in person, by phone, in writing. Not everyone can attend daytime meetings or read lengthy documents.
- **Accessibility.** Check that your materials, language, and venue are accessible to people with different abilities, literacy levels, and communication preferences.
- **Diverse perspectives.** Actively seek out participants from underrepresented groups. If your collaborative sessions always attract the same types of people, you are missing important perspectives.
- **Open conversations.** Ask participants what they need to participate fully. Start by opening up the conversation rather than making assumptions.
- **Safe spaces.** Establish ways of working at the start of each session. Provide directions to a quiet space. Acknowledge that participating may bring up difficult feelings and that it is always acceptable to take a break.

### Planning Collaborative Sessions

A well-planned session follows a clear arc from welcome to close. Here is a general structure you can adapt.

| Time | Activity | Purpose |
|------|----------|---------|
| **15 min** | Arrivals and sign-in | Allow participants to settle, write name badges, get refreshments |
| **10-15 min** | Welcome and icebreaker | Introductions, housekeeping, establish ways of working and car park |
| **10-15 min** | Introduction and context | Share the aims of the session, present background research, explain the framework |
| **20-30 min** | Individual or small-group work | Journey mapping, persona development, idea generation using structured prompts |
| **15 min** | Break | Take photos of work produced, prepare for next exercise |
| **20-30 min** | Sharing and discussion | Bring the group together, share individual work, identify themes, dot-vote on priorities |
| **20-30 min** | Identifying next actions | Brainstorm solutions using idea sheets, sketch ideas, role-play scenarios |
| **15 min** | Close and feedback | Summarise actions, confirm next steps, collect evaluation feedback |

**Materials typically needed:**
- Sign-in sheets and name stickers
- Consent forms (if recording or using quotes)
- Post-it notes in multiple colours
- Flipchart paper and marker pens
- Printed templates (journey maps, idea sheets, persona templates)
- Dot-vote stickers
- "Car park" flipchart
- "Ways of working" flipchart

For multi-session projects, display the work from previous sessions on the walls so participants can do a "walking tour" to reconnect with earlier insights before building on them.

## Bringing It All Together

Collaborative design in practice requires three things: a structured process, genuine involvement of the people affected by your work, and a willingness to adapt based on what you learn. Whether you are mapping a customer journey for a social enterprise or co-designing a service with the people who use it, the same principles apply.

Start with a clear "How Might We" question. Involve stakeholders early and meaningfully. Use frameworks like journey mapping, personas, and Rose/Thorn/Bud analysis to structure your exploration. Define roles clearly. Keep sessions focused and inclusive. And always close the loop by sharing what you learned and what you plan to do about it.
