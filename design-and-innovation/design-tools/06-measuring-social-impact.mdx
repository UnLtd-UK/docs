## Why Measuring Social Impact Matters

If you run a social enterprise, charity, or purpose-driven organisation, your impact is your reason for existing. But impact that is not measured cannot be communicated, improved, or proven. Whether you need to report to funders, attract new partners, or simply understand whether your work is making a difference, you need a structured approach to social impact measurement.

Measuring impact is not just about accountability. It is about learning. When you understand what is working and what is not, you can adapt, improve, and make better decisions about where to invest your time and resources.

## The Social Impact Management Cycle

Social impact management is not a one-off exercise. It follows an ongoing cycle with three phases:

| Phase | Description |
|-------|------------|
| **Design** | Define your value proposition, build your Theory of Change, and plan how you will measure impact. |
| **Do** | Deliver your core activities and collect data as you go. |
| **Learn and Adapt** | Analyse your data, draw insights, communicate findings, and refine your approach. |

This cycle sits alongside your core business activities -- sales, marketing, training, investment -- and informs your strategy at every stage.

## Stakeholder Analysis

Before you define what to measure, you need to understand who cares about your impact and why. A stakeholder analysis maps the people and organisations connected to your work into three groups.

### Who Benefits

These are your primary beneficiaries -- the people whose lives your work is designed to improve. They might be your customers, service users, or the communities you serve.

### Who Pays

These are your funders, investors, commissioners, or paying customers. They need to understand the return on their investment, whether financial or social.

### Who Else Cares

These are secondary stakeholders -- regulators, partners, local government, media, other organisations in your sector. They may have influence over your work or be affected by it indirectly.

For each stakeholder group, ask yourself:

- How do you communicate with them?
- What do they want to know and why?
- What do you want them to do with the information?
- Is it worth your time and resource to collect and share this data?

## Defining the Problem

Before you can measure your impact, you need to be clear about the problem you are solving. Use a problem definition framework to articulate the social issue your organisation addresses.

### Key Questions

1. What is the key issue you are trying to address?
2. Why is it important?
3. Who is it a problem for?
4. What **PESTLE factors** shape this problem? (Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Legal, Environmental)
5. What evidence do you have that this is worth the investment?
6. What are the alternative solutions currently being used?
7. What are the disadvantages of those alternatives?

Consider whether the problem you have identified is one that is recognised by others. Aligning your problem definition with established frameworks -- such as the UN Sustainable Development Goals or national policy priorities -- can strengthen your case and open doors to funding.

## Developing Your Value Proposition

Your value proposition explains how your organisation solves the problem you have defined. There are several frameworks that can help you articulate this clearly.

### Steve Blank's XYZ Formula

A simple, powerful structure:

> **"We help X do Y by doing Z."**

Fill in the blanks: Who do you help? What do you help them achieve? How do you do it? This forces you to be specific and concise.

### Geoff Moore's Value Proposition Statement

A more detailed template:

> For **(target customer)**
> who **(statement of the need or opportunity)**
> our **(product/service name)** is **(product category)**
> that **(statement of benefit)**.

This format works well for investor pitches and grant applications because it clearly links your offering to a specific customer need.

### Simon Sinek's Golden Circle

Simon Sinek's framework starts with purpose and works outward:

- **Why:** Your purpose. Why does your organisation exist?
- **How:** Your process. How do you deliver on your purpose?
- **What:** Your offering. What products or services do you provide?

As Sinek argues: "People don't buy what you do; people buy why you do it."

**Example (Apple):**
- **Why:** We believe in challenging the status quo and doing things differently.
- **How:** Our products are beautifully designed and easy to use.
- **What:** We make computers.

For a social enterprise, leading with your "why" is especially powerful because your purpose is often your greatest differentiator.

### Value Proposition Canvas Questions

To develop your proposition further, work through these questions:

| Area | Questions |
|------|----------|
| **Customer** | What benefits do your customers want? What do they currently like about other solutions? What are they trying to achieve? What pains do they want to avoid? |
| **Value Map** | What does your product or service help customers achieve? How does it deliver gains? How does it fix their pains? |

## Building a Theory of Change

A Theory of Change is your roadmap from activities to impact. It maps the logical chain of cause and effect between what you do and the difference it makes.

### The Chain of Change

| Stage | Description | Guiding Question |
|-------|------------|-----------------|
| **Resources** | What your enterprise has (staff, funding, equipment, partnerships) | If my enterprise has these... |
| **Activities** | The services and products you deliver | If my enterprise does these... |
| **Outputs** | The direct, countable products of your activities (goods and services delivered) | If people access these... |
| **Sub-Outcomes** | Changes in the behaviour or practice of your users | If people use the goods/services, then... |
| **Outcomes** | The meaningful changes in people's lives that result from changed behaviour | If people change their behaviour, then... |
| **Impact** | The broader, lasting change for a group of people, the environment, the economy, or society | What is the meaningful change? |

### The Role of Assumptions

At every stage of the chain, you are making assumptions. Between resources and activities, you assume your staff have the right skills. Between activities and outputs, you assume people will access your services. Between outputs and outcomes, you assume that accessing the service will lead to behaviour change.

Write down your assumptions explicitly. They are the places where your Theory of Change is most vulnerable, and testing them is how you learn and improve.

## KPI Development

Once you have a Theory of Change, you need Key Performance Indicators (KPIs) to track whether your theory is playing out in practice. Use the **SMART** framework to define them.

| Letter | Meaning | Description |
|--------|---------|------------|
| **S** | Specific | What exactly are you measuring? |
| **M** | Measurable | Can you count it or observe it reliably? |
| **A** | Attainable | Is the target realistic given your resources? |
| **R** | Relevant | Does this indicator connect to your outcomes? |
| **T** | Timebound | By when do you expect to see this result? |

**Example:** "By the end of the year, 100 young people will have developed new skills and feel more confident and employable as a result of the project."

From this outcome statement, you can extract:
- **Indicator:** Number of young people with new skills and increased confidence
- **Target:** 100
- **Timeframe:** End of year

## Types of Data

Your KPIs will require two types of data.

| Type | What It Captures | Example |
|------|-----------------|---------|
| **Quantitative** | Quantities and amounts. Numbers you can count. | Number of customers reporting increased income as a result of training. |
| **Qualitative** | Judgement, perception, emotion. The richness behind the numbers. | Evidence that beneficiaries' wellbeing has increased as a result of support. |

The most robust impact measurement uses both types, cross-referencing quantitative trends with qualitative stories to build a complete picture.

## Data Collection Approaches

Choose data collection methods that are appropriate for your resources, your stakeholders, and the type of data you need.

| Method | Best For |
|--------|----------|
| **Interviews** | Deep, qualitative insight from individuals. Good for understanding experiences and motivations. |
| **Surveys and forms** | Quantitative data at scale. Good for tracking trends across large groups. |
| **Focus groups** | Exploring shared experiences and generating ideas. Good for understanding group dynamics. |
| **Diaries** | Capturing ongoing experiences over time. Good for longitudinal insights. |
| **Beneficiary photography** | Visual storytelling. Good for engaging stakeholders emotionally. |
| **Social media** | Real-time sentiment and engagement data. Good for understanding reach and perception. |
| **Art and creative approaches** | Accessing experiences that are hard to articulate in words. Good for working with diverse groups. |

You can also draw on pre-existing tools and methodologies such as Lean Data (Acumen), Most Significant Change, or the Wellbeing Index, or develop bespoke tools tailored to your specific context.

### Data Collection Planning

Create a data collection plan that answers these questions for each indicator:

| Column | Question |
|--------|----------|
| **Indicator** | What are you measuring? |
| **Method** | How will you collect the data? |
| **Milestone** | What intermediate targets will you track? |
| **Target** | What is the overall goal? |
| **When** | When will data be collected? |
| **Duration** | How long will collection take? |
| **Responsibility** | Who is responsible? |
| **Cost** | What will it cost? |

## Case Studies

### Exceptional Individuals: Theory of Change for Neurodivergent Employment

Exceptional Individuals developed a Theory of Change focused on promoting an inclusive working culture for neurodivergent (ND) people, allowing them to achieve their full potential.

**Activities included:**
- Academy programmes and work placements
- Workplace audits to ensure ND-friendly environments
- Workshops and one-to-one support for ND candidates
- Continued in-work support for ND employees and businesses
- Consulting, advocacy, and education initiatives
- Speaking events sharing education around ND needs in the workplace
- Guides and information for businesses recruiting ND employees
- Workplace needs assessments

**Target outcomes:**
- ND people are more aware of their career potential and more confident to achieve goals
- More businesses employ ND people into inclusive working cultures
- Recruitment barriers for ND candidates are reduced
- Skills and confidence of ND employees are improved
- Corporates and SMEs change policies and procedures to attract and maintain ND workers
- Wider society understands the benefits of an ND workforce

**Data points tracked:** Number of workshops held, knowledge levels before and after, time spent in new job roles (six months or more is ideal), number of corporates worked with, number who would recommend the service, number of work placements, social media engagement, one-to-one survey data, references in publications, and funding drawn from access-to-work schemes.

### Sofab Sports: Theory of Change for Disability Sports and Employment

Sofab Sports built a Theory of Change aimed at decreasing the percentage of disabled people in long-term unemployment.

**Activities included:**
- Paid employment and volunteer opportunities for young adults with physical and learning disabilities
- Skills development through a "skills matrix" approach for the retail industry
- Developing soft skills such as communication and teamwork
- Physical exercise and training for people who struggle to access alternative clubs
- New socialisation opportunities through sports teams

**Target outcomes:**
- Employees and volunteers improve employability skills (soft and hard skills)
- Employees, volunteers, and academy participants improve confidence and wellbeing
- Employees and volunteers have more agency in decision-making around employment and life choices
- Employees and volunteers increase job experience

**Recommended next steps followed a six-stage process:** stakeholder review, impact framework draft, impact beta sign-off with key stakeholders, testing data collection on a small group, finalising the framework and tools, and planning future impact management including data collection, analysis, insights, and communication.

### Peckham Pantry: Social Impact Workshop Walkthrough

The Peckham Pantry social impact workshop walked a social enterprise through the complete impact management process in a single facilitated session. The agenda covered:

1. **Stakeholder analysis** -- identifying who benefits, who pays, and who else cares
2. **Problem definition** -- using the DIY Toolkit Problem Definition Tool to articulate the social issue
3. **Value proposition development** -- working through Steve Blank's XYZ, Geoff Moore's statement, and Sinek's Golden Circle
4. **Theory of Change development** -- mapping the chain from resources through to impact
5. **KPI development** -- defining SMART indicators and targets
6. **Data collection planning** -- choosing methods and assigning responsibilities

This structured, single-session approach demonstrates that impact measurement does not have to be overwhelming. By working through the process step by step, even early-stage social enterprises can build a credible and useful impact framework.
