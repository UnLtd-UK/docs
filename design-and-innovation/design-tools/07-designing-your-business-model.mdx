## What a Business Model Really Is

At its core, a business model is a set of **choices** and the **consequences** of those choices. It describes how your organisation creates and captures value -- the logic of how your enterprise works.

This framing, developed by Ramon Casadesus-Masanell and Joan E. Ricart in their research on competitive strategy, moves beyond vague descriptions and gives you something concrete to design. Your business model is not your strategy. It is the machine that your strategy builds. Understanding this distinction is essential.

## Three Types of Choices

When you create a business model, you make three types of choices.

| Choice Type | Description | Examples |
|-------------|------------|---------|
| **Policy choices** | The actions your organisation takes across all its operations. | Using non-union workers, locating facilities in rural areas, encouraging employees to fly economy class, offering no-frills service. |
| **Asset choices** | The tangible resources you deploy. | Manufacturing facilities, technology platforms, fleet of vehicles, satellite systems. |
| **Governance choices** | How you arrange decision-making rights over policies and assets. | Should you own or lease equipment? Should decisions be centralised or distributed? |

Seemingly small differences in governance can dramatically influence the effectiveness of your policies and assets. Two organisations can make identical policy and asset choices but achieve very different results depending on how decision-making is structured.

## Consequences: Flexible and Rigid

Every choice produces consequences, and these fall into two categories.

**Flexible consequences** respond quickly when the underlying choice changes. If you raise prices, volumes drop almost immediately. If you launch a promotion, demand responds within days.

**Rigid consequences** take time to build and are slow to disappear even if the choices that created them change. A culture of frugality, built over years through policies requiring economy travel and modest offices, does not vanish overnight when those policies are relaxed. A reputation for quality or innovation, once established, persists.

Rigid consequences are your source of competitive advantage because they are difficult for competitors to imitate. They take time to build, and that time is your moat.

## Three Characteristics of a Good Business Model

How can you tell if a business model will be effective? Evaluate it against three criteria.

### 1. Aligned with Your Goals

The choices in your business model should produce consequences that enable your organisation to achieve its goals. This sounds obvious, but misalignment is common. Consider the cautionary example of Xerox PARC, which spawned transformative innovations -- laser printing, Ethernet, the graphical user interface -- but was notoriously unable to capture value from those innovations for the parent company. The business model was not aligned with Xerox's goals.

### 2. Self-Reinforcing

Your choices should complement one another. There must be internal consistency. If a low-cost carrier decided to offer the comfort level of a full-fare airline -- fewer seats, complimentary meals, wider legroom -- those choices would undermine its low-cost structure and destroy profitability. When you spot a lack of reinforcement, it is a signal to rethink some choices and make new ones.

### 3. Robust

Your business model should be able to sustain its effectiveness over time by fending off four threats:

| Threat | Question |
|--------|----------|
| **Imitation** | Can competitors replicate your business model? |
| **Holdup** | Can customers, suppliers, or other players capture the value you create through bargaining power? |
| **Slack** | Is organisational complacency creeping in? |
| **Substitution** | Can new products decrease the value customers perceive in yours? |

## How Business Models Create Virtuous Cycles

The most powerful and often overlooked aspect of business models is their ability to generate **virtuous cycles** -- feedback loops that reinforce themselves over time.

Your choices produce consequences. Those consequences enable further choices, which produce further consequences. As these cycles spin, your key assets and resources grow, and your competitive advantage deepens. This is similar to network effects, but it is not an inherent feature of your technology. It is something you design through the choices you make.

### Example: Ryanair's Virtuous Cycles

When Ryanair switched from a traditional model to a low-cost model in the early 1990s, it made a series of interconnected choices: low fares, secondary airports only, one class of passenger, charges for all extras, no meals, short-haul flights only, a standardised fleet, non-unionised workforce, high-powered employee incentives, and lean headquarters.

These choices generated several reinforcing cycles:

| Cycle | How It Works |
|-------|-------------|
| **Volume and bargaining power** | Low fares lead to high volumes, which give greater bargaining power with suppliers, which lower fixed costs, which enable even lower fares. |
| **Aircraft utilisation** | Low fares lead to high volumes, which increase aircraft utilisation, which lower fixed cost per passenger, which enable even lower fares. |
| **Service expectations** | Low fares set expectations of no-frills service, so no meals are expected, which keeps variable costs low, which enables even lower fares. |

As long as these cycles continue to spin, competitive advantage keeps growing. But they are not invincible. If employees unionised and demanded higher wages, forcing fare increases, volumes would fall, aircraft utilisation would drop, and the cycles would reverse.

### Virtuous Cycles for Differentiators

Virtuous cycles are not just for low-cost players. Irizar, a Spanish cooperative manufacturing luxury motor coach bodies, built a radically different business model centred on shared ownership, flat hierarchy, decentralised decision-making, and team-based work. The rigid consequences -- a deep sense of employee ownership, feelings of accomplishment, and trust -- generated a virtuous cycle connecting customer willingness to pay with relatively low costs, high profits, and reinvestment in innovation, service, and quality. This model produced a 23.9% compound annual growth rate over 14 years.

## Competing Through Business Models

Most organisations design and evaluate their business models in isolation. But the success or failure of your model depends significantly on how it interacts with the models of other players in your industry.

### Strategy 1: Strengthen Your Own Virtuous Cycle

You can modify your business model to generate new virtuous cycles that make you more competitive. The key is to identify where a new cycle would reinforce existing ones.

Airbus did this when it launched the A380 to compete with Boeing's monopoly in the very large commercial transport segment. The 747 had been contributing an estimated 70 cents of every dollar of Boeing's profits, and those profits were reinvested to strengthen Boeing's position in other segments. By entering the same segment, Airbus strengthened its own cycle while simultaneously weakening Boeing's most profitable one.

### Strategy 2: Weaken Competitors' Cycles

Rather than only strengthening your own position, you can use the rigid consequences of your choices to weaken a competitor's virtuous cycle. The goal is to disrupt the feedback loops that sustain their advantage.

Microsoft used this approach against Linux. While Linux's virtuous cycle was powered by being free and allowing user-contributed code improvements, Microsoft used its relationships with hardware manufacturers to have Windows pre-installed on PCs and laptops, preventing Linux from growing its customer base and disrupting its key cycle.

### Strategy 3: Turn Competitors Into Complementors

Rivals with different business models can sometimes become partners in value creation. When Betfair, an online betting exchange, entered the British market, it offered a two-sided platform where people could both place bets and offer odds to others. While this initially hurt traditional bookmakers, the better odds Betfair offered meant that gamblers lost less money, placed more wagers, and fed a virtuous cycle that expanded the entire market. Traditional bookmakers also used Betfair to gauge market sentiment more accurately and hedge their exposures at lower cost. What started as a competitive threat became a complementary relationship.

## Business Model, Strategy, and Tactics

These three concepts are distinct and should not be used interchangeably.

| Concept | Definition | Analogy |
|---------|-----------|---------|
| **Business Model** | The logic of how your company operates -- how it creates and captures value. The system of choices and consequences. | The car itself. |
| **Strategy** | The contingent plan about which business model to use. It includes provisions for different competitive scenarios and environmental changes. | Designing and building the car. |
| **Tactics** | The residual choices available to you within your current business model. These are comparatively easy and inexpensive to deploy. | How you drive the car. |

Your business model determines which tactics are available to you. A free newspaper cannot use price as a tactic. A no-frills airline cannot use in-flight luxury as a differentiator. Understanding these constraints helps you make better decisions about where you can and cannot compete.

## Applying This to Your Organisation

### For Social Enterprises and Cooperatives

The choices-and-consequences framework is particularly useful for social enterprises, where the business model must serve both social and financial goals simultaneously. Consider:

- **Policy choices** that reinforce your social mission while generating revenue
- **Asset choices** that serve your beneficiaries and your customers
- **Governance choices** that reflect your values (cooperatives, community ownership, stakeholder boards)

### For Platform Models

If your organisation connects two or more groups (buyers and sellers, service providers and users), your business model involves managing multiple sides of a market. Your virtuous cycles will depend on growing both sides in balance.

### Building Your Own Model

Start by mapping your current choices across all three types (policy, asset, governance). Identify the consequences of each choice. Look for where consequences reinforce each other -- those are your existing virtuous cycles. Then ask: Where could a new choice create a new cycle? Where is a cycle vulnerable to disruption?
