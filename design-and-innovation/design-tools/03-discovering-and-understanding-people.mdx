## Challenge Statements and How Might We Framing

Every design process begins with a clear challenge. Challenge Statements are collaborative and consensus-building tools that help you explore and test the validity of a problem before jumping to solutions.

### How to Create a Challenge Statement

1. Place a pre-prepared challenge statement in the left-hand area of a large template or poster.
2. Discuss **why** you need to address this challenge. Write each reason on a separate Post-it note and place more strategic reasons at the top, more operational ones at the bottom.
3. Discuss **how** you might address the challenge. Again, write each idea on a Post-it, with strategic approaches at the top and granular, operational ones at the bottom.
4. Cluster the Post-its to identify common themes.
5. Revisit the original challenge statement — does it still accurately describe the problem based on the themes that have emerged?
6. Agree on short, medium, and long-term steps, framing each one as a "How Might We..." question.

### Tips for "How Might We" Framing

"How Might We" questions help you think divergently, creating opportunities rather than pre-determining solutions. Keep them focused enough to be actionable but open enough to allow multiple possible answers.

For example: *"How might we take what we have, adapt it, and grow our offer to increase sales while building the right tools for our customers and ourselves?"*

## Stakeholder Mapping and Analysis

A stakeholder list is a complete inventory of everyone involved in shaping, making, delivering, commissioning, and using whatever you are designing.

### How to Create a Stakeholder Map

1. Identify groups of people who might have a stake in your challenge.
2. List as many different groups as possible — think broadly. If your stakeholder group is a GP surgery, the "who" might include the GP, nurse, receptionist, patient, cleaner, and more.
3. For each group, list specific individuals or roles connected to it.
4. Complete the Extreme Users column (see below) for each group.

A stakeholder list is a useful precursor to deeper research. It ensures you consider and design with everyone's needs in mind rather than focusing only on the most obvious users.

## Understanding People and Extreme Users

Understanding people is a method that helps you identify the full range of users of a product, service, or system. Rather than designing only for the "average" user, you deliberately consider the extremes.

### Why Extreme Users Matter

Extreme users sit at the edges of your user spectrum. By understanding their needs, you design solutions that work for everyone in between. Common ranges to consider include:

- First-time users versus frequent users
- Early adopters versus those who are last to engage
- Individuals versus people with dependents
- Youngest users versus oldest users

When you design for the extremes, you create more inclusive and universally useful solutions. This can lead to greater uptake and deeper relevance of your product or service.

## Research Methods

### Interviewing: In Context and Out of Context

Interviewing is one of the most direct ways to gather insights from the people you are designing for.

**Interviewing in context** means recording what people say they do and how they do it within the actual environment relevant to your challenge. This creates opportunities to gain insight into what people actually think, feel, and do. It helps you identify hidden data and build empathy.

**Interviewing out of context** means gathering data from people outside their usual environment. It is more economical and can be done remotely, but you may miss the environmental cues that in-context interviews reveal.

#### Interview Protocol

Structure your interviews around these prompts:

1. **I am...** — introduce yourself and the purpose of the interview
2. **You are...** — confirm who they are and their relationship to the service or challenge
3. **Tell me...** — open-ended questions about their experience
4. **Tell me more...** — probe deeper into specific points
5. **Show me...** — ask them to demonstrate or walk you through their experience
6. Ask what they **like**, what they **do not like**, and what **ideas or suggestions** they have
7. Ask if they can **suggest anyone else** you should talk to

> "What people say they do, think they do, and what they actually do, are entirely different things." — Margaret Mead, Cultural Anthropologist

### Be-the-User

Be-the-User helps you gain first-hand experience of using products and services as others do. You adopt the mindset of a target user or customer and go through the actual experience.

#### How to Do It

1. Put yourself into the role of someone using the product or service.
2. Take notes, sketches, and photos to capture aspects of the experience. Think about what happens **before**, **during**, and **after** using the service.
3. Pay attention to the people involved, the space the service takes place in, the information available, and how other people contribute to the experience.

This method is particularly effective when done by different groups or teams from an organisation. It often reveals significant strengths or weaknesses that you would never have noticed otherwise.

### Journaling

Journaling is the documenting of an activity over an extended period. Participants create journals based on the context of your project, telling their own stories in their own time over days, weeks, or months.

Journals can take many formats — notebooks, photographs, blogs, or any resource that can be interpreted when the activity ends. This method captures far more data than interviews alone and avoids the opportunity cost of deploying a researcher for extended periods.

### Fly on the Wall Observation

Fly on the Wall helps you understand how people interact with the world around them by observing directly while being as unobtrusive as possible.

#### How to Do It

1. Identify a relevant context or location to observe and explain why it is relevant to your challenge.
2. Prepare a guide outlining what you want to learn and the materials needed to capture observations.
3. Blend into the background and take on the role of an objective bystander.
4. Observe from different vantage points.
5. Record findings through notes, photos, and video (following data protection and safeguarding policies).

Fly on the Wall often reveals what people actually do, which frequently differs from what they say they do in interviews. Spending time immersed in a person's environment helps you identify barriers and opportunities they may not even be aware of themselves.

## Experience Diagrams

An Experience Diagram helps you analyse feedback on a service across its stages: before, during, and after. It is often a follow-on activity to Be-the-User research.

### How to Create One

1. Break down the experience into 8 to 10 steps.
2. Transfer each step onto a Post-it note (one step per note).
3. Plot each step against a scale from "better than expected" to "as expected" to "worse than expected."
4. Think broadly — when did you first become aware of the service? What happened after the core experience?
5. Discuss and agree on the key moments that need improvement.

Experience Diagrams ground your analysis in real user experiences rather than abstract notions of how a service should work. They make the highs and lows visible, helping you focus your design efforts where they will have the most impact.

## Personas and Pen Portraits

A persona (also called a pen portrait) is an informal description of a fictional person that represents one of your key user types. Personas cover a wide range of variables and focus on attitudes, needs, goals, and lifestyle.

### Why Personas Matter

Personas help you step out of your own perspective. They bring to life the concerns, needs, and wants of your target audience, helping your entire team align around a shared understanding of who you are designing for. They are especially valuable when designing for marginalised groups or end users whose lived experience differs significantly from your own.

### How to Create Personas

1. Following your user research, identify the stakeholders critical to success.
2. Complete a persona template noting the persona's name, age, location, occupation, needs, goals, and aspirations.
3. Include a sketch or image and a characteristic quote to bring the persona to life.
4. Limit yourself to 3 or 4 personas per project. Too many dilute your focus.

### Tips

- Personas are only as good as the research behind them. Primary research is always best.
- Do not base personas on known individuals, as this can bias your understanding.
- Try putting a face to each persona — it makes insights visible and memorable.
- Personas work well as standalone exercises but are even more powerful when incorporated into other design activities like ideation and customer journey mapping.

## Customer Journey Mapping

A customer journey map describes step by step how a user interacts with your service. The process is mapped from the user's perspective, describing what happens at each stage, what touchpoints are involved, and what obstacles or barriers they may encounter.

### The Basic Framework

1. Identify the key stages of your customer's journey (for example: pre-interest, interest, sign-up, experience, return).
2. For each stage, map what the customer is **doing**, **thinking**, **feeling**, and **experiencing**.
3. Identify the touchpoints — the people, information, products, and spaces they encounter.
4. Note what happens **before** and **after** the core experience, even when it involves other services or providers.

### The Theatre Model

The theatre model is an alternative customer journey format that maps the experience using a theatrical analogy:

- **Audience** — the users your organisation is trying to reach
- **Actor** — the voice of your organisation and the people delivering the message
- **Script** — what they are saying and the content being shared
- **Stage** — where they are saying it (online, presentations, conferences, in person)
- **Backstage** — everything that needs to happen behind the scenes to make it work (CRM systems, policies, documentation, processes)

### Emotion Tracking

The journey map is often integrated with an additional layer representing the level of positive or negative emotions experienced throughout the interaction. Plot the highs and lows of each step as a simple line graph to make emotional patterns visible. This helps you identify where your service delights users and where it causes frustration.

## Case Example: Moments Cafe

Moments Cafe is a social enterprise that used design thinking methods to improve its sales approach and business development portfolio. The project illustrates how multiple design tools can work together across a structured series of sessions.

### The Process

The team framed their work with a "How Might We" statement: *"How might we take what we have, adapt it, and grow our offer to increase our sales and build the right tools for our customers and ourselves?"*

They then worked through the following sessions:

1. **Kick-off** — defined the HMW statement and vision, identified existing resources and how they were used, then mapped the customer journey tracking what the customer was doing, thinking, feeling, and experiencing at each stage
2. **Stakeholder Mapping** — developed personas for five potential typical customers, considering sentiment, buying power, motivation, and buy-in
3. **Prototyping** — used knowledge from the first two sessions to prototype resources for each stage of the journey
4. **Build Phase** — built new resources, adapted existing ones, and created a test-and-learn approach

### What the Journey Map Revealed

By applying the customer journey mapping approach with Rose, Thorn, Bud analysis at each stage, the team uncovered specific insights:

- **Pre-interest:** The process was too passive at the start, taking too long to generate interest, though they had strong resources available once they got in front of the right people
- **Interest:** Customer knowledge was siloed in one person's head rather than systematised, and social impact data was not packaged effectively for customers
- **Sign-up:** Information sharing felt disjointed rather than planned, with no clear sense of likely timeframes to conversion
- **Overarching:** They discovered they already had 90% of what they needed — the challenge was adapting existing tools and using them more effectively against customer needs

This case demonstrates how combining several design methods — HMW framing, customer journey mapping, personas, Rose/Thorn/Bud analysis, and prototyping — can surface practical, actionable insights for a social enterprise.
