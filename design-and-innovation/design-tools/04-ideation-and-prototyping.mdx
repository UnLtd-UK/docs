## Generating Ideas: The Develop Phase

Once you have defined your challenge and understood your users, the Develop phase is where you generate as many ideas as possible. The goal is volume and variety. You are looking for unexpected connections, creative leaps, and diverse perspectives. Judgement comes later.

## Creative Matrix

The Creative Matrix is a time-bound, structured approach to brainstorming that increases the volume, focus, and usefulness of your outputs. It mitigates the pitfalls of basic brainstorming — lack of focus and the dominance of strong personalities.

### How to Use It

1. **Prepare the template.** Along the top of the matrix, place three "How Might We" statements drawn from your challenge work. Down the left column, place three important stakeholders from your stakeholder mapping.
2. **Generate ideas.** At the intersection of each row and column, think of an idea that might address the challenge while meeting the needs of that stakeholder. Write one idea per Post-it note.
3. **Cross-pollinate.** Ask other people or teams to contribute ideas to the matrix using different colour Post-its to show how many additional ideas have been added.

### Rules of Brainstorming

Apply these rules during the Creative Matrix exercise:

- Withhold criticism and judgement
- Encourage wild ideas
- Prioritise quantity over quality
- Build on the ideas of others
- One conversation at a time
- Be visual

### Tips

- Give a time limit — you can generate dozens of ideas in 15 minutes.
- Before discussing as a group, generate ideas individually to avoid being influenced by others. This ensures all unique ideas are captured.
- Encourage participants to put at least one idea in each box.

## Sketch Sheets for Rapid Idea Capture

Sketch Sheets provide different settings for ideas to be mapped out, recorded, and given brief descriptions. They are a flexible, visual way of capturing ideas at any stage of the process.

### When to Use Them

Use Sketch Sheets whenever you have ideas forming that need to be recorded quickly. They work well during ideation sessions but are also valuable tools to keep returning to throughout the design process. Each sheet provides structured spaces for quick drawings and short written descriptions, helping you communicate concepts visually even if you are not confident in your drawing skills.

## Cover Story Mock-Up

The Cover Story Mock-Up is where you present a story on a newspaper's front page describing the successful implementation of your idea and its future impact.

### How to Create One

1. Think of a catchy three-word title for the idea and write an attention-grabbing headline.
2. Draw an image illustrating the vision — how success will be reported in five to ten years.
3. Describe the challenge that has been overcome, the solution adopted, and the impact achieved. Include quotes or testimonials.

### Why It Works

This method builds engagement by explaining the need and benefit of an idea in a vivid, relatable format. It helps build consensus and shared understanding of what success looks like, and it is also useful as a prototyping method to test with stakeholders and gather feedback.

Choose a newspaper or publication that is relevant to your audience — one that key stakeholders are likely to read or relate to.

## Alternative Worlds

Alternative Worlds is a method that draws inspiration from organisations, products, and services in other sectors where similar problems may already have been solved.

### How to Use It

1. Think about how organisations in other sectors or domains might approach your challenge.
2. Ask the question: "How would [organisation X] solve this?"
3. Come up with as many ideas as possible from multiple alternative worlds.
4. Remember that all ideas are valid at this stage. The goal is to be as creative as possible.

### Why It Helps

This method pushes you beyond the usual boundaries of your sector. By looking at how other organisations, individuals, or initiatives normally approach a given issue, you identify common principles that may still apply when you return to your own context. Think of organisations that hold similar principles or deal with similar pressures but work in entirely different fields.

## Clustering and Theming

After generating a large volume of ideas, you need to make sense of them. Clustering helps you group ideas under categories. Theming assigns the right title or label to each cluster.

### How to Do It

1. Lay out all of the captured ideas.
2. Pick one idea to start with and look for others that are related.
3. Sort all ideas into clusters.
4. Break large clusters into sub-groups — avoid more than six Post-its per cluster.
5. Label each final cluster with a descriptive theme heading that includes a verb and "speaks for itself."
6. Delineate the clusters and highlight connections between them.

### Tips

- Group by theme or idea, not by colour.
- The theme heading for each cluster should be clear enough that someone who was not in the room can understand it.
- This method works best as a group activity to build shared understanding and surface emerging opportunity areas.

## Round Robin

Round Robin is a collaborative method where you critique and evolve ideas by passing them from person to person. It ensures everyone participates and prevents strong characters from dominating the conversation.

### How to Use It

1. In groups of at least three, each person takes a Round Robin template.
2. **Box 1:** Write the challenge you want to address.
3. **Box 2:** Write or draw an innovative solution to this challenge.
4. Pass the template to the next person, who writes or draws **three reasons why the idea will fail.**
5. Pass again to a third person, who writes or draws **the most creative way to address those failures.**
6. Gather, collate, and discuss all the ideas as a group.

The purpose is to encourage creative and collaborative thinking. Be as creative and as critical as possible — the method is designed to stress-test ideas and make them stronger.

## Purpose Primer Design Square

The Purpose Primer is a tool for aligning your team around a shared ambition before diving into design work. It provides a structured space for everyone to contribute their thoughts.

### How to Use It

1. Start with the **central box**, which holds the ambition — the core purpose of your design work. Broadly define this together before moving to the outer boxes.
2. Fill in the four surrounding boxes:
   - **Vision** — the context in which you want the ambition to be achieved, along with some of the "hows"
   - **Impact** — what completing this work will achieve
   - **Challenges** — obstacles and difficulties you expect to face
   - **People** — everyone involved or impacted, including staff, end users, other organisations, businesses, and stakeholder groups
3. The initial version does not need to be final. Revisit and refine it as your understanding deepens.

## Prototyping Methods

Prototyping allows you to test ideas and gather feedback on potential solutions before committing significant resources. The goal is to prototype and test early and often — the more cycles of "make, test, learn" you go through, the more you improve your design.

### Rough-and-Ready Prototypes

A rapidly built model of a new idea to gather early feedback. Use low-cost materials — cardboard, paper, foam — to mock things up quickly. It is better to be quick, rough, and early than slow, perfect, and late.

**Tips:**
- Identify which part of the product or service you are prototyping
- Use "just enough" technology to get going (for example, PowerPoint to mock up software, cardboard to mock up interiors)
- Test where people will actually use the product or service

### Service Blueprints

A service blueprint visually maps people's experiences and touchpoints alongside your organisation's behind-the-scenes inputs such as employee activity and processes.

Think of it as having a **front stage** (the user's experience and interactions) and a **backstage** (the staffing, infrastructure, and technology needed to make things happen). The blueprint helps you see the interaction between front and back, making links and dependencies visible.

### Roleplay

Roleplay is a simulation technique that helps you see through the eyes of people using your product or service. Give people roles ahead of the session, decide on scenes and storylines, and use storyboarding to capture scenes and the lessons you learn. Create props or use objects around you to make the simulation tangible.

### Storyboards

Storyboarding allows you to visualise a person's experience of a service or product through a sequence of images and captions. You show the key interactions and scenarios that will be part of the future process. Drawings do not need to be perfect — the point is to create a work in progress that others can respond to and build on.

### Product and Service Collateral

Create mock-ups of the items that will exist alongside your new idea: packaging, web pages, instructions, or a leaflet that explains your product or service. This helps you design, test, and iterate the collateral before production.

### Tips for All Prototyping

1. Do not be afraid of getting things wrong or receiving negative feedback. It is all part of the learning process and can help you reach the right solution faster.
2. Be clear about what you need to test and how you are going to test it.
3. Document your work through video or photos to communicate insights to stakeholders who were not present.
4. Test with potential users of the service whenever possible.

## Evaluation and Prioritisation

After generating and prototyping ideas, you need methods to evaluate them and decide what to take forward.

### Rose, Thorn, Bud

Rose, Thorn, Bud is a method for quickly categorising data or feedback as positive, negative, or having potential.

- **Rose (pink Post-its):** Highlights, successes, quick wins, compelling elements
- **Thorn (blue Post-its):** Challenges, difficulties, concerns, risks
- **Bud (green Post-its):** New ideas that could blossom with support

#### How to Use It

1. Write a single important point on a colour-coded Post-it note.
2. Bring all feedback together and cluster by theme to draw out new insights.
3. Focus on gathering specific feedback rather than jumping to solutions.

This method is effective for quickly identifying patterns and can be used as a group exercise to build common understanding before collaboratively developing solutions.

### Importance/Difficulty Matrix

The Importance/Difficulty Matrix helps you review actions or outcomes by plotting them on a 2-by-2 grid with importance along one axis and difficulty along the other. This gives you a clear visual indication of what to focus on in the short, medium, and long term.

#### How to Use It

1. List all the actions or ideas that emerged from your design work.
2. Rank the difficulty of each task first, placing them along the horizontal axis.
3. Plot each task along the vertical axis based on importance.
4. The resulting quadrants show you:
   - **High importance, low difficulty** — do these first (quick wins)
   - **High importance, high difficulty** — plan these for the medium term
   - **Low importance, low difficulty** — do these if time allows
   - **Low importance, high difficulty** — deprioritise or drop these

You can group sub-actions together into larger themes and draw arcing lines to determine the order in which clusters should be tackled, starting with the easiest high-impact group.

### Dotmocracy

Dotmocracy is a simple, democratic voting method for deciding which ideas to take forward when you have more options than you can pursue.

#### How to Use It

1. Display all ideas or concepts visibly (on a wall, board, or table).
2. Give each participant approximately three votes (dot stickers or marker dots).
3. Participants cast their votes by placing dots on their preferred ideas. You can put all your votes on one idea or spread them across several.
4. Count the dots. The ideas with the most votes indicate the group's priorities.

Dotmocracy works well as a closing activity because it gives every participant an equal voice and produces a clear, visual result that the whole group can see and discuss.
