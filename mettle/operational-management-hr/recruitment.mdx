INTRODUCTION TO THE ROOTS HR RECRUITMENT TOOLKIT
The overarching aim of an organisation’s recruitment process is to recruit motivated
and high performing people who share the organisation’s values and who wish to
contribute to its aims and objectives.
The aim of this Toolkit is to support organisations in understanding and being self-
sufficient in applying the processes that can contribute to developing and
implementing a legally compliant, good practice, effective employee recruitment
process. The Toolkit links to template documents that can be used as provided or
adapted to meet the needs of the particular organisation. This Toolkit is also supported
by our Selection Toolkit (due to be released in August 2021).
This Toolkit is aimed at helping small social sector organisations to:
• Understand and meet minimum legislative requirements in recruiting
employees
• Apply good practice whilst enabling an appropriate level of flexibility in their
approach to recruitment
• Respond effectively, strategically and operationally, to internal and external
change
• Attract and appoint the best possible candidates
• Treat applicants fairly in line with equality of opportunity, the principles of
diversity and inclusion and with transparency and accountability
• Maintain the minimum possible cost and administrative burden balanced
against the direct and indirect financial costs of getting the recruitment
process wrong.
The recruitment process in this Toolkit starts with the identification of a vacancy and
ends after shortlisting.
The Toolkit is not intended to be prescriptive and decisions taken throughout the
recruitment process should be in the context of, and proportionate to the scale of, the
individual organisation and managing and mitigating against its risks.
The Toolkit is not written for the purpose of recruiting volunteers, Trustees or service
users who are being supported within employment although many of the principles
and practices applied within it may be useful in those scenarios.
Roots HR can provide additional consultancy advice and support on any of the areas
outlined in this Toolkit upon request.

1. Introduction
1.1 These guidance notes cover the following areas:
• Recruitment principles
• Equality, diversity and inclusion in the recruitment process
• Job analysis
• Job description and person specification
• Recruitment process, timescales and panel
• Application process
• Recruitment information pack
• Advertising
• Shortlisting
• Candidate communications.
2. Recruitment Principles
2.1 Everyone should have a right to equal access to employment and, when
employed, should have equal pay and equal access to training and
development.
2.2 The overall aim of a recruitment and selection process is to appoint into a
vacancy the candidate in the employment market at that time who best meets
your selection criteria and is willing to work for your organisation at the salary
offered. Good recruitment and selection processes therefore focus on obtaining
as much relevant evidence about potential candidates as is possible and using
this to make a decision based on merit about the best candidate who will be
offered the role.
2.3 All recruitment should be managed in a way that reflects, as well as supports,
the organisation in being a good employer and complying with statutory and
regulatory requirements.
2.4 Recruitment is a two-way process and is not just about selecting a candidate
but is also an opportunity for candidates to consider whether this is the role and
organisation for them. A change in job could be a significant lifestyle change for
the individual and their family. As such, every stage of the recruitment process
is an opportunity to show the organisation as an attractive employer.

2.5 All involved in recruitment are representing the organisation and should provide
positive but realistic impressions of the job and organisation, enabling
prospective employees to make an informed decision.
2.6 Recruitment processes should be efficient and cost effective, using the most
appropriate advertising and assessment for the role. The process should be
expedited promptly and within agreed and published timescales, to benefit
those involved as well as to provide a positive impression to candidates and of
course to avoid losing good candidates to other employers.
2.7 All recruitment activity should comply with data protection requirements and:
• have a clear, documented audit trail of decisions made throughout the
process
• treat all applications and personal data confidentially, securely storing
personal data and restricting access, with the need to print or transport
documents taken into account
• personal data held on unsuccessful candidates should be confidentially
destroyed at the appropriate time (usually 6 months from application unless
your organisation’s policy provides for an alternative period of time).
2.8 This Toolkit is applicable to internal and external recruitment. As such the same
considerations should be given to internal candidates, particularly at the
shortlist stage, as would be for external candidates.
2.9 It is the responsibility of the employer and the people involved in recruitment
decisions to ensure that they are appropriately competent and confident in their
role and to ensure that candidates are treated fairly and that decisions are made
objectively.
2.10 The guidance in this Toolkit should be considered in conjunction with any
policies your organisation may have in place on recruitment, data protection
and equality, diversity and inclusion and on the employment of ex-offenders
and safeguarding as relevant to the nature and scope of the organisation.
3. Equality, diversity and inclusion in the Recruitment Process
3.1 Equality of opportunity should be central to any recruitment process, not only
because it is unlawful to discriminate based on a protected characteristic, but
Page 2 of 18

RECRUITMENT TOOLKIT
also because discrimination, stereotyping and favouritism reduces the
opportunity to identify and select the best person for the job based on merit.
3.2 As well as getting the best person for the job, a fair and consistent recruitment
process will help minimise the risk of:
• Staff turnover
• Absenteeism
• Performance and/or disciplinary issues
• Tribunal claims and the related legal costs, management time and
reputational risk
3.3 Diversity recognises that, though people have things in common with each
other, they are also different in many ways. In recruitment, this can, consciously
or unconsciously, lead those responsible for designing the role and the process
and for shortlisting and selection to make decisions which create barriers or
result in excluding people in some way from the recruitment process, not
because they would be unable to perform the role but because they do not
conform, perhaps to the organisation’s previous norms or to perceptions held
by the shortlisting panel.
3.4 Inclusion is where the differences between people are seen as a benefit, and
where perspectives and differences are shared, leading to better decisions.
Organisations are increasingly committed to demonstrating their position on
inclusion, for reasons of business performance, reputation and ethics, and
recruitment is a key opportunity in the employee journey to do this. We have
seen the Black Lives Matter movement have a positive impact within the sector,
with many organisations actively taking lawful steps to increase ethnic diversity
within their workforce.
3.5 Candidates should therefore be selected solely on their ability to do the job
irrespective of age, disability, gender, gender reassignment, race and ethnicity,
religion or belief, sexual orientation, pregnancy or maternity and marriage or
civil partnership. These are known as protected characteristics.
3.6 Equality legislation covers direct and indirect discrimination, as well as
discrimination by association or perception, and the responsibility lies with the
employer to show that they have not unlawfully discriminated on the grounds of
the factors above. Further information on these definitions can be found here:

3.7 Individuals involved in recruitment who take discriminatory actions or decisions
are potentially accountable, as well as the organisation itself, unless the
organisation can demonstrate it took all reasonable steps to prevent the
individual from discriminating against someone.
3.8 This does not prevent an employer from taking positive action to address under-
representation of certain groups in its workforce. A way to do this is, for
example, to ensure the advert for a role reaches the under-represented group,
perhaps by placing it in a targeted form of media aiming to reach that
demographic.
3.9 Sector regulators, commissioners, grant-makers, funders, customers and
partners are also likely to expect an objective and fair recruitment process that
enables the organisation to identify and select the best candidate for the role.
They may also expect to see initiatives underway to address under-
representation and to promote diversity and inclusion within the workforce.
3.10 Ways in which objectivity can be demonstrated and discrimination avoided
generally include:
• Advertising vacancies and not just offering roles to family members, friends
or a current employee
• Reality-checking the job and person specification, selection criteria and
advert wording to ensure that they do not include any content that is
potentially discriminatory or unreasonably excludes certain groups without
justification
• Not making assumptions about candidates, for example based on name,
how long ago they were at school, whether they went to university if not
relevant to the role, family or caring responsibilities etc., but instead
focussing on assessing each candidate objectively on how well they meet
your selection criteria based on the evidence they provide to you
• Not making assumptions about the ability of internal people to do the job
based on what you know about them, but instead, providing them with the
same opportunity provided to external people to provide evidence (which

may include previous experience or voluntary work etc., that you are not
aware of) to demonstrate their suitability for the role
• Not including age-related criteria in job and person specifications, selection
criteria and advert wording and avoiding words like ‘young’, ‘recently
qualified’ and ‘mature’, as these could potentially be seen to be
discriminatory to older or younger workers
• Ensuring job and person specifications, selection criteria and advert wording
avoid language which is gender specific or gender coded
• Not requiring candidates to have experience or qualifications that cannot be
proven to be relevant to the role
• Not specifying a required length of experience. Using such a requirement,
particularly where it is for a lengthy period, could potentially be seen to be
discriminatory against both younger workers and women who have taken
career breaks when having children. Instead, focus on what type of
experience you are looking for rather than an arbitrary figure for how long
the person has being doing it for example, ‘significant experience of
providing a service in a busy, customer-focused environment’ rather
than ‘three years customer service experience’
• Not letting a protected characteristic such as a person’s age or ethnicity
consciously or unconsciously affect your judgment of their suitability
• Being prepared to make reasonable adjustments if you are informed by a
candidate that they have a disability and require adjustments to the
recruitment process
• People involved in making selection decisions in recruitment campaigns
declaring at the earliest possible stage if they know an external applicant.
Usually declaring and noting this at the shortlisting and interview stage, as
appropriate, will be sufficient

4. Job Analysis
4.1 Whether a new role is being considered or a vacancy arises from an employee
leaving, this provides an opportunity to consider the needs of the
team/organisation. The best way of handling a vacancy is often not to simply
recruit a new employee into the same role. Possible alternatives include:
• developing the role as an apprenticeship
• redistributing aspects of the role where there is capacity elsewhere and/or
to provide development opportunities for other staff
• restructuring within the team to create a different/new role
• using temporary staff or creating an internal secondment where the
requirements are short-term or for a specific project.
4.2 Even if this is an existing role, it is important to undertake a job analysis exercise
because it will ensure that the way in which the role is described reflects the
current and future needs of the organisation and takes account of anything that
may have changed in relation to the role or organisation since the role was
previously filled.

4.3 Job analysis is a process used to collect information about the job itself and
what is needed to do the job and may include discussion and decisions on the:
• job title
• job location
• duties and responsibilities
• working conditions and work environment
• machines, tools, equipment and materials needed to do the job
• possible hazards
• outcomes
• necessary qualifications, skills, knowledge, experience and personal
attributes.
4.4 These factors identify what a job demands, what an employee must possess to
perform the job and how much it should be paid to be attractive and competitive
in the labour market. This information is essential in developing or updating the
job description and person specification for the role and ensuring this is
accurate and unbiased and in setting the pay for the role.
4.5 Job analysis also helps to determine the relationship of the role to other roles
in the organisation, which in turn, helps in determining pay.
4.6 The person undertaking the job analysis will review available documents and
speak to key stakeholders including the outgoing postholder, their line manager
and their immediate team. The process may include the following activities:
• Reviewing the job responsibilities of current employees including,
where possible, speaking to existing employees doing the role, including
obtaining from departing employees and making sure they have described
their daily duties in sufficient detail so that you are able to recruit a new
employee who has the capacity and capability to do the job correctly.
• Doing internet research and viewing sample job descriptions online or
offline of similar jobs. While you would not want to copy another
organisation’s job description, looking at several can be helpful in writing
your own.
• Analysing the work duties, tasks, and responsibilities that the employee
filling the position needs to accomplish. Not every job within a company is
optimised. You may find duties that are undone or projects that you should
move from one department to another. You may identify tasks that another
Page 7 of 18

RECRUITMENT TOOLKIT
role would more successfully and easily accomplish. Look at the needs of
the organisation and at any unassigned or illogical responsibility.
• Researching and sharing with other organisations that have similar
jobs. Sometimes organisations will happily share information about their job
descriptions. There are also salary survey companies, where you can match
up your jobs to their descriptions and share salary information and also help
you figure out what to include in your own job descriptions and what to pay.
This is called salary benchmarking and is something that Roots HR can help
with.
• Articulate the most important outcomes or contributions needed from
the position. Sometimes you can get so caught up in the tasks that you
forget to look at the required outcomes. Make sure you are clear about the
most important elements of the role and those which will take the majority
of the postholder’s working time.
4.7 Sometimes, a job analysis exercise will highlight issues wider than relevant to
the role you are analysing. For examples, tasks that need to be done may not
be assigned to an employee, or one role has more tasks assigned than any one
person could realistically accomplish.
4.8 The more information you can gather, the easier you will find the decision to
either reallocate or address the work in other ways (see 4.1) or if you decide
there is a vacancy, with the writing of the job description and person
specification.
5. Job Description and Person Specification
5.1 The fundamental roles of the job description and person specification in the
recruitment process are to provide a clear and accurate portrayal of the job on
offer and to allow effective assessment of candidates’ skills and abilities to
perform that job. It is important to spend the time before initiating the
recruitment process to make sure that the job is described realistically as well
as attractively, and that the person specification genuinely reflects the attributes
required to perform effectively in the role. A standard template for a job
description and person specification is included with this Toolkit.
5.2 An accurate job description and person specification will allow candidates to
self-select into the recruitment process (or not) and help you to avoid the
possibility of recruiting someone who subsequently finds the job is not what
Page 8 of 18

RECRUITMENT TOOLKIT
they expected, as well as enable the selection panel to assess candidates’ skills
and abilities fairly and effectively against the criteria.
5.3 Both documents are contributory elements to identifying training needs for the
postholder and for the probation and performance reviews following
appointment, which adds to the importance that they accurately reflect the job
the successful candidate will be doing following appointment.
5.4 The job description does not need to be exhaustive, but it should include a clear
description of the purpose and key responsibilities/accountabilities of the role,
not just now, but in the future as the team/organisation works towards achieving
objectives and plans. While being relatively concise it should include enough
detail to provide clarity to someone unfamiliar with the role or the organisation.
5.5 The person specification should be drawn from the key responsibilities
described in the job description. It is not intended to be a picture of the ideal
staff member, but a representation of the Essential and Desirable
qualifications, skills, experience, knowledge and personal qualities needed to
perform effectively in the role. Listing unnecessary criteria under Essential may
deter competent candidates from applying and may even amount to indirect
discrimination so it is imperative to list here only those criteria that are truly
Essential for the role. If the role can be done without them, list them in Desirable
instead.
5.6 It is important that the job description and person specification is written clearly
and in plain English (or the relevant language for the role) and does not contain
jargon, abbreviations or acronyms that could be unfamiliar to potential
applicants. The key is a functional job description and person specification
more than anything else. Ask yourself, ‘If somebody from outside the
organisation read this, would they know what the person in this position actually
does?’
6. Selection Process, Panel and Timescales
6.1 Once a job description and person specification for the role have been agreed,
as well as the terms and conditions, you should decide on what the process will
look like along with appropriate timescales. There needs to be an appropriate
balance between not rushing the process, allowing sufficient time from start to
finish taking account of different aspects of the process, such as, shortlisting,
tests etc., and ensuring that the process is expedited so as not to lose the best
Page 9 of 18

RECRUITMENT TOOLKIT
candidates (since it is reasonable to expect that people interested in your role
will also be applying for other roles at the same time).
6.2 The recruitment timetable should be determined before the role is advertised,
so that panel members can be confirmed for shortlisting and interview and
prospective candidates are aware of the keys dates at the point of applying.
The timetable will also give you an indication of when a successful candidate is
likely to start, so that this and the successful candidate’s induction can be
planned for. For senior vacancies, consider the impact of possible longer notice
periods on the start dates of prospective candidates.
6.3 Consideration should also be given to the composition of the panel to ensure
members are, as a minimum, objective and are suitably competent. Depending
on the nature of the role, the panel should usually consist of at least two and
no more than four people, one being the direct line manager of the role.
6.4 The Roots HR Selection Process Toolkit (due to be released in August 2021)
provides in depth guidance in designing a selection process to test candidates
for a role. As summary guidance for the purposes of this Toolkit, it is most
common to test candidates through an interview process but additional insight
into their ability to meet the essential criteria for the role can be gained by
selection tests. When a series of tests are applied to candidates over the
course of a day, it may be referred to as an “assessment centre”. Any tests the
candidate undertakes should be directly relevant to the job and designed to
clearly supply evidence for one or more important criterion in the person
specification. Many of the most useful tests also require a significant time input
in design and assessment, so the value of the additional evidence needs to be
weighed up against that cost.
6.5 Types of assessment options include:
• Work sample tests i.e. drafting a letter, analysing financial information,
outlining a plan/approach
• Presentations on a subject relevant to the role, where presentation skills are
also a key requirement for the role
• Roleplay exercises
• Case studies
• In-tray exercises

6.6 Tests should be used in conjunction with other evidence available through the
recruitment process (i.e. application and interview) to support an evidence
based judgement on the suitability of a candidate for the role. They should not
be used in isolation to rule candidates out of the process.
6.7 Whatever the assessment process, it should be professionally planned and run.
This is particularly important, not only in terms of the impression that it gives
candidates of the organisation but also as candidates may spend a significant
amount time preparing for and participating in the process.
7. Application process
7.1 As part of the overall recruitment process, it is important to consider how you
wish to receive applications. Whatever approach you choose, it should be
stated in your advertising and consistent across all candidates as part of your
fair process.
7.2 Under the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), employers should
collect only the information required to process an application. Applicants
should be provided with your Job Applicant Privacy Notice setting out how the
data required of them will be processed.
7.3 Your application process will determine the quality and consistency of the
information you receive across candidates at the application stage. Consider
the “candidate journey” in terms of how long the application will take them, how
your competitors ask candidates to apply for jobs and how senior the role is.
Filling out a lengthy application form will invariably reduce the number of
candidates who apply but the quality of information is usually better; asking for
cvs only will usually generate more candidates but the information you receive
will be down to what they choose to provide.
7.4 Application forms (either in electronic or hard copy) are a traditional application
approach. A generic application form is included with this Toolkit. Once
completed, they contain a large amount of personal and sometimes “special
category” (more sensitive) data over which great care should be taken,
including information such as contact details, education, training and career
history, criminal record (depending upon the role applied for), referee contact
details and equality monitoring information. Typically, they contain a space for
the employee to compete their personal statement about how they match the
Page 11 of 18

RECRUITMENT TOOLKIT
person specification for the role and why they want the role. In the hope of
maximising applications, some employers now state that they will accept
application forms alongside cvs to cover off the information required on
education, training and career history whilst reducing application time.
7.5 Once used only by executives and professionals, most working people now
have a cv (a curriculum vitae). These are increasingly commonly accepted as
an application for a role, especially by recruitment agencies who seek to
generate as many candidates as possible. It takes considerably less time for a
prospective candidate to provide a cv than to complete an application form but
the layout and the amount of detail will vary between individuals which can lead
to inappropriate assumptions when shortlisting candidates.
7.6 Cover letters or emails are a good way to supplement and substantiate the
information provided on a cv and to ensure a candidate supplying a cv
demonstrates a commitment to the role and the future employer by a
reasonable investment of time at the application stage. Applicants are asked to
provide, for example, “no more than 400 words” or “1 side of A4” setting out
how they match the person specification for the role and why they want the role.
This can make shortlisting considerably easier.
7.7 It is usual to record received applications on an applicant tracking spreadsheet
or online portal, in order to ensure consistent control of the overall recruitment
process and to know which applicants have progressed within the process and
which have received specific communications. A suggested applicant tracking
spreadsheet is included with this Toolkit.
8. Recruitment Information Pack
8.1 A recruitment information pack is not essential, but it can be effective in
attracting candidates. It is a good way to showcase the role and the
organisation by pulling key documents together into one document. A
recruitment information pack can also help in providing additional information
about the role and organisation than can be provided in a job description and
advert and therefore reduce the number of potential enquiries from people
interested in the role.
8.2 The contents of the recruitment information pack will vary depending on the
organisation and role, but may include some or all of the following:
Page 12 of 18

RECRUITMENT TOOLKIT
• A cover letter from the line manager of the role which accurately represents
the role and the team and helps the candidate to get an understanding of
the challenges and opportunities
• Information about the organisation and details of how to find further
information such as the Corporate Plan or Annual Accounts, particularly for
senior roles
• A copy of the advert text
• The job description and person specification
• Team structure chart
• Information about the location of the organisation and commuting
• Information about employee benefits relevant to the role.
8.3 The information pack will usually be created as one document in pdf or word
format, which can be sent to potential candidates or downloaded from the
organisation’s website or if separate documents, created as a zipped file. This
should be on organisation headed paper and formatted in line with the
organisation’s branding guidelines.
9. Advertising
9.1 There is no requirement in law to advertise a role externally and in the event
you have a reasonable pool of candidates in your current workforce, you may
decide to advertise internally only. All posts that are advertised externally
should also be advertised internally. However, if you have a Recruitment Policy
which requires you to advertise all vacancies externally then you should comply
with this.
9.2 There are a number of options when deciding how and where to advertise a
position. The advertising options may include:
• Internal advertising
• Online advertising (i.e. general job boards, specialist sites, Indeed,
LinkedIn, etc.)
• Media advertising – national and/or local newspapers, specialist
publications
• Community networks and publications
• Job centres.
9.3 Using recruitment agencies is another option, but remember that agencies will
usually charge between 15-30+% of the annual salary for placing a permanent
Page 13 of 18

RECRUITMENT TOOLKIT
employee and as such, ensuring that you have the budget for this and aiming
to negotiate the fee down are essential requirements if you decide to go this
route. Bear in mind recruitment agencies may charge additional fees for
advertisements on top of the 15-30+%. Ensure you clarify what work they will
do within that fee, eg shortlisting, interviewing, taking up references etc.
9.4 Usually, an advert will be the first contact that an external candidate has with
the organisation, so it is essential that it attracts and retains the attention of
potentially suitable candidates, within constraints on space and any associated
advertising costs.
9.5 Writing a job advert is a relatively simple process, mainly because you are so
space-limited there is little opportunity to get creative. However, it is important
for that reason to make sure you are maximising the use of that space. That
doesn’t always mean cramming as much information as possible into very short
sentences; remember that the purpose of the advert is to drive as many people
as possible to the place where they can find further information to enable them
to decide whether they wish to apply.
9.6 It is best when writing an advert to keep everything bite-size. Bullet points
can work very well depending on the information you are setting out.
Concentrate on what a potential applicant will want to know; that means the
job they will be doing. Don’t waste half the advert writing about the work of the
team. Fundamentally, a potential applicant wants to know a little about the
organisation, a bit more than that about the job, and what their pay, location
and benefits will be! It is a good idea to include “No agencies please” to deter
unwelcome calls from agencies. There is no legal requirement to include
wording about equal opportunities (unless your organisation has a
Recruitment Policy which states that you must do this); however candidates
may have a more positive perception of you as an employer if a statement
setting out your principles around equality and diversity is included.
9.7 Decide in advance of advertising how long to advertise for, to provide enough
time for candidates to see the role, obtain further information where appropriate
and decide that they want to apply. Also consider whether there is a holiday
period or religious festival during the advertising period and whether it is
appropriate to advertise then, or whether it will be necessary to extend the
closing date to reflect this. The aim is to provide the maximum opportunity for
people to see the role, and make a decision to apply and then apply, so
Page 14 of 18

RECRUITMENT TOOLKIT
consider for example, is there any benefit to you of a closing date of 5pm on a
Friday, as opposed to 9 a.m. on a Monday?
9.8 All adverts should include details of the closing date for the advert and contact
details to obtain further information (it is best to add, “…after the applicant has
read the job description/Candidate Information Pack”) and how to apply. In the
interest of fairness to all applicants and potential applicants, applications should
not normally be accepted after the closing date. A template for an advert layout
is included in the Toolkit.
9.9 Sometimes an organisation may decide to close the advert ahead of the
advertised closing date, particularly where it is expected that there will be a high
volume of applications. In this case, it is important to let people know in the
advert that the job may close early where there is a high volume of applications,
so as not to disappoint those applicants that decided to wait until close to the
closing date to apply.
9.10 Equally, there may be occasions where it is necessary to extend the closing
date due to a low response rate/advertising over a holiday period etc. In this
case, make sure the advert closing date, job description/recruitment information
pack is updated accordingly, and consider letting candidates that have already
applied know, particularly if this will impact on the recruitment timescale
previously notified.
9.11 One of the best sources of marketing can come from existing employees.
People who enjoy the work they do and the organisation they work for often tell
their friends and associates, and staff that recommend their place of work to
other people rarely make recommendations to people that are not suitable
candidates for the role. As such, make sure every effort will be made to ensure
staff are aware of the vacancy, as if they are not interested in the role for
themselves, they may know someone who is.
10. Shortlisting
10.1 Shortlisting is the main process of assessing written applications for the position
and identifying those with the potential to undertake the role who should be
invited for interview (and testing if applicable).
10.2 Shortlisting will often be carried out by at least two members of the panel who
will perform the interview but equally may be carried out by an HR professional

or someone outside of the recruitment process who is trained to shortlist. It
does not have to wait until the closing date, particularly if you are receiving
applications electronically and expect to receive a high volume of applications.
The shortlisting criteria should therefore be discussed and agreed ahead of
advertising, to enable managers to begin the process on receipt of applications,
if they wish. Where a panel is shortlisting, the panel members should perform
the shortlisting individually and then discuss their results to compare and agree
the candidates to invite to the next stage of the process. Where opinions differ,
panel members should trust the process and take forward the candidates with
the highest overall scores from shortlisting.
10.3 A typical scoring system for shortlisting is as follows:
- Good evidence that the candidate meets the required criterion - 2 points
- Some evidence that the candidate meets the required criterion - 1 point
- No evidence that the candidate meets the required criterion - 0 points.
10.3 The shortlisting scores should be based only on the written information supplied
by candidates, ie the “evidence” provided against the person specification
criteria. It is rare that any candidate will be able to provide good evidence for
every criterion. Shortlisting is an exercise of judgement (which is why it is
usually done by more than one person) and panel members will need to take a
view based on the evidence for each section as a whole.
10.4 The shortlisting process should be seen as an individual assessment of the
extent to which each candidate meets the criteria, not a comparison of
candidates against each other.
10.5 No assumptions should be made about candidates’ abilities where they have
not provided evidence and no account should be taken of additional information
known about candidates. This is particularly important for internal candidates.
Any additional information known on internal candidates i.e. current
disciplinary/performance issues, can be taken up at the pre-employment
(reference) stage. The guidance in section 3 above, should also be taken into
account.
10.6 All shortlisting decisions should be recorded, and a record maintained in line
with your data protection retention schedule. A shortlisting template is included

Why is good practice in recruitment important?

Getting the right person first time is important. The sooner a new employee is on board and working for you, the sooner you feel the benefit of their knowledge and experience. This ups your productivity and takes the burden off of other people. 

It will inevitably take a certain amount of time to recruit – for example, to advertise, wait for applications and then to interview the candidates usually takes at least 1 month for a permanent role but then, assuming they are currently in work, there is the wait for them to join you once you have made them an offer, they have accepted it and given their notice to their current employer. That may be another month for many roles but a notice period can be 3 or even 6 months for a senior role. So you want to make the right decision first time.

The CIPD’s Resourcing and Talent Planning Survey Report 2020 puts the overall median cost of recruiting a senior role at £5,000 and the cost of other roles at £2,000. So you definitely want to make the right decision first time.

Getting it wrong is expensive. It impacts on your business adversely in terms of productivity and reputation. It will affect the bottom line, service delivery and performance as well as team morale. Getting it wrong will involve resources spent on managing underperformance and the risk of dismissing someone in the future. It is demoralising for the employee and frustrating for the manager. It can impact on your organisational reputation (in many ways, eg word of mouth about what you are like as an employer, service delivery, how it looks if you have to repeat advert again etc). There are also the practical costs of re-recruiting and covering the vacancy until a suitable candidate is found and inducted. 

One of the worst indicators of an unfair recruitment process is when there is no process  at all, ie when a decision is taken to appoint or promote someone either without giving other candidates an opportunity to apply or considering them for a role. Effectively that is favouritism and whilst it might shorten the time taken to recruit and reduce the amount of management resources involved in that process, it is no guarantee that the person appointed will be successful in the role, nor that they will stay in it. Nor will it look or feel fair to other employees who may have wanted to apply for that role or who don’t feel the individual is suitable for it. 

Savvy employers recognise that their recruitment process is a window into their organisation and is a way for their stakeholders to judge them – either positively or negatively. Those stakeholders might be your potential and actual candidates but they might also be your clients, service users and competitors who use your recruitment process to have a deeper look into your organisation than they might ordinarily be able to get. If you subscribe to that view, and let’s face it, it makes sense, then you’ll want to make sure that your recruitment process reflects your standards, your products and services and your ethics to very best effect. 

Organisations are increasingly committed to demonstrating their position on inclusion, for reasons of business performance reputation and ethics, and recruitment is a key opportunity in the employee journey to do this. We have seen the Black Lives Matter movement have a positive impact within the sector, with many organisations actively taking lawful steps to increase ethnic diversity within their workforce. 

This Toolkit is designed to help you to implement a merit-based recruitment process, ie one which enables you to appoint the best candidate for the detailed and very specific requirements of your role. The benefits of this speak for themselves.

One of the biggest myths we hear about recruitment is that every job has to be advertised – this is not the case. There is no law that says any job has to be advertised.

The main legislation relating to recruitment is the Equality Act 2010, which makes it unlawful to discriminate directly or indirectly, by association or by perception.

This is about treating people differently in a way that disadvantages them, for no justifiable reason. An advert is the way a job vacancy is communicated to the potential candidates, both internally and externally if you wish. The reason employers usually advertise a job, at least internally if not externally, is to demonstrate inclusion, a commitment to diversity and anti-discriminatory practice and to show that there is genuine equality of opportunity in their organisation, ie that applications are welcome from anyone (as long as they meet the criteria for the role).

There are anti discriminatory laws in employment for all of the reasons on this slide. In recruitment and selection, anti-discriminatory practice applies to documents such as job descriptions, person specifications and advertisements and to practices such as shortlisting and selection methods (such as interview questions, tests etc).

Your organisation’s recruitment process is also a great way for the organisation to demonstrate its commitment to diversity and inclusion. 

Definitions and examples:
Direct discrimination: treating a person less favourably than others are treated on grounds of gender, race, age etc. 
Eg: selecting a white or male applicant over a better qualified black or female applicant.

Indirect discrimination: applying a provision, criterion or practice equally but which disproportionately disadvantages a certain group without any justifiable reason. 
Eg applying a dress code which makes the role unattractive to certain ethnic groups when it isn’t an essential requirement for the role.

Discrimination by Association: treating a person in a particular way because of their relationship with someone else who has a protected characteristic.
Eg not making a job offer to someone because they have responsibility for caring for an elderly relative  

Discrimination by Perception: treating a person in a particular way because you assume they have a characteristic which may not in fact be the case.
Eg not making a job offer to a female candidate who you consider to have a masculine appearance, because you think she is a lesbian and assume she will not be well-received by the team.

There is also a legal responsibility to make reasonable adjustments to both the interview process and the job for disabled candidates.

We often find ourselves helping employers to reply to communications from candidates where they believe the recruitment process has discriminated against them. When a fair process has been followed, with a good audit trail to support it, it is easy to do this and to close off the problem at the earliest indication. 

And remember, it is not just about ensuring your organisation has a defence in the event of a legal challenge to your recruitment process; it is also about valuing diversity and being seen to be genuinely inclusive.

### Planning for recruitment

There is a considerable amount of planning to do, either ahead of advertising for your role or in tandem with it. 

Timeline
Generally speaking, a good way to work out a timeline is to determine the date you ideally want to make the offer to the successful candidate (bearing in mind they may have to give notice to their current employer) and to work backwards from there. For most permanent roles, you’d want to advertise for a minimum of 3-4 weeks and for temporary roles at least 2 weeks if time permits. You also need to build in time to invite candidates to interview, for them to prepare (and for the panel to prepare) and for the scoring and consideration of the candidates to take place prior to an offer being made. It’s not uncommon for employers to decide they want to put a second interview in for the final 1 or 2 candidates before they make the offer.

Candidate pack
From the moment your advert goes live, candidates can see it and may want to apply for it so ahead of that point, you will need to have, as a minimum:
Put together your job description and person specification for the role
Put together any other candidate information
Decided on your application process and made any application form available.

Job description / person specification
I can’t recommend highly enough that you spend time getting the job description and person specification right. I always see your person specification as your “shopping list” in the labour market. You‘ll be listing the Qualifications, Experience, Knowledge, Skills, Personal Attributes and anything else that is Essential or Desirable for the role. Too many Essential criteria will put candidates off applying. If it’s not truly Essential for the role, list it in Desirable instead. For example, does your management role really require a degree, perhaps because you are in a regulated environment where it is required? Or can the role be done equally well by someone with experience of management practice? If the latter, the degree goes in the Desirable column. This could also be potentially discriminatory if a degree is not essential.

Candidate journey
It is a really good idea when you think you have got your adverts up in various places such as your website or job boards, to go through the “applicant journey” and to test each of the adverts to make sure they read correctly and the hyperlinks work properly and they lead the applicant to the right website, to the candidate pack, the application form etc. 

Panel / shortlisting / interview and assessment process
Remember candidates will be looking at other roles as well so once the advert closes, it is in your interests to shortlist as quickly as possible so that the interviews can be held as quickly as possible and you can determine your best candidate to make your offer before they are snapped up by someone else. It is therefore a very good idea to decide early on in the process who is going to shortlist and who is going to be on the interview or selection panel so that those people have the dates protected in their diaries and the candidates can be told the interview dates in the advert. 

You will also need to work out at this stage ideally how many candidates you will shortlist (usually somewhere between 4-8), how you intend to test the shortlisted candidates against the person specification (as this will impact on the number of people required to assess tests etc), the length of time each candidate will be with you and how many candidates you can test in a day. The interview questions and tests and the scoring processes for these can all be designed while the application process is underway. See our Roots HR Selection Process Toolkit, due for release in August 2021, for more detailed guidance on this.

### The steps in a merit-based recruitment process

The example recruitment process in your Toolkit take you through the steps from advertising to receiving applications to carrying out the shortlisting. 

You can use our applicant tracking form to list your candidates as they submit their applications. It’s not a legal requirement but a good measure in terms of anti-discriminatory practice is to redact the information on a cv or application form that identified the candidate and where they live, plus any other irrelevant information that might, consciously or subconsciously, inform prejudice such as date of birth, dates they attended school or college etc. Give each candidate a number and refer to them and their application (and any covering letter or email) by number throughout the shortlisting process., until they are either declined or invited to interview.

In the course of handling the applications in this way, remove any equality monitoring forms and process these separately. They will be anonymous; do not use the candidate number on these. The data can be put into a spreadsheet to enable you to see how diverse your applicant pool is. This information can be used to help determine improvements to your recruitment advertising strategy with future vacancies. 

The shortlisting panel may ask for the applications in hard copy or electronically. Cut and paste the Essential and Desirable criteria from your person specification for the role into our shortlisting template and put the name of the candidates along the top of the columns. If more than one person is shortlisting, it can be helpful to add formulae to the template so that the scores from each person can be pulled through to a master sheet, to save the calculations afterwards.

Of course, the shortlisting panel must assess each application in a consistent manner. Our Guidance Notes suggest a method of scoring each application for the purposes of shortlisting – see 10.3 on page 16. ### Skills required in a recruitment process – procurement

Recognise that recruitment will usually involve external purchasing, typically advertising or the services of an agency. To get the best deals for your organisation, you will need to know how to negotiate and what to expect from certain types of supplier. 
 
It will usually be possible to achieve significant discounts - but only if you ask for them and only if you know what to ask for, of course. Don’t place adverts or instruct agencies without speaking to colleagues in the sector and to professionals such as ourselves to find out what you should be paying and then develop your negotiation skills to ask for similar rates and discounts.
 
Many online job boards are free but the ones you really want to be on for social sector roles - for example, the Guardian, Third Sector and Charity Jobs - are chargeable.
 
You will usually get a very good discount if you are able to commit to advertising more than one role at a time. You don’t usually have to be placing these at the same time; the unused adverts are held as credits for a specified period of time until you are ready to recruit again. 
 
You can also get great discounts through recruiters who offer multiple job board advertising services. For example, our social sector recruitment partner, Not For Profit People, will write and place your adverts for you on a wide range of online job boards and send you the applications, for an all-inclusive price, as of July 2021 of £749 + VAT! This reflects a huge saving on just the normal price of the Guardian, the Charity Jobs and the Third Sector job boards, not to mention the administration time of placing all the adverts. Roots HR clients receive a 15% discount and further discounts are available for purchasing credits to hold for future campaigns, often saving 10% and sometimes even 20% and more on the price per campaign. 
 
If you recruit via an agency, you will usually find they charge a fee based on a percentage of the first year salary, payable for the introduction to the candidate you appoint. The more senior or specialist the role, the greater the percentage. Senior roles will often be quoted at 30% or more of first year salary but you may be able to negotiate this down to around 20% and considerably lower for more junior roles. 

If you do use an agency, ask them what the rebate policy is for the service you are having. If the candidate you select doesn’t work out for any reason (either because they leave in the first few weeks or because their performance or conduct doesn’t meet your expectations), most agencies will offer a discount off the fee for replacing that candidate, depending on how long they have been in post. For example, if the candidate leaves in week 1, you may find you can get a replacement from the agency free of charge.  If they leave after 2 months, there may be a discount of 50% on the fee for the replacement candidate. Obviously you should make sure you are clear on terms like this at the outset and that the contract between your organisation and the agency reflects what has been agreed verbally.

### Skills required in a recruitment process – written skills

In the course of a recruitment process, a candidate has many examples to look at your organisation and to make judgments about it. As a minimum , they’ll see your advert, your website, probably your social media and they’ll receive any communications you send to them about the application. 

Whether you choose to acknowledge receipt of applications, to answer any questions the candidates may have about your role, to send declines at shortlisting stage or after interview, or not, (and many organisations choose not to these days, especially for junior roles), your decision will have consequences for the impression candidates develop towards your organisation. 

Whatever you decide to do, in recruitment as in many other ways, your organisation’s output is visible to the world and is a reflection of its standards.  

You may decide to have an out of office message as a reply on recruitment inbox emails to acknowledge all emails received and to negate the time associated with an individual acknowledgement – see our example in the template pack. 

You may have a standard decline email or letter you send to all candidates who are unsuccessful after shortlisting – again see our example in the template pack.

Whatever you decide to do, spelling mistakes, punctuation, poor grammar and poor document formatting all reflect badly on your organisation. 

You may decide to use our templates which have been pre-approved for spelling, grammar etc. If you  do your own thing and are uncertain about the standard of your written English, get it checked out by someone who is competent in this area and / or approved by a senior manager or the person in your marketing department (if you have one) who will usually be responsible for outward-facing communications.  

In particular, in the event you decide to offer feedback on the application in writing (which we don’t recommend, see the Guidance notes with this toolkit,) make sure it is factual, evidence-based, non-discriminatory and related to the content of the application and the scores achieved in shortlisting. Ensure it is well presented in terms of spelling and formatting and always keep a copy on the candidate’s file.

### Skills required in a recruitment process - assessment

Recruiting requires those involved to make judgments on behalf of the employer at the shortlisting stage and as I said earlier, not everyone is comfortable about it. The most important thing in shortlisting is to make no assumptions. You do of course have limited information at this stage.

You should have applications – either on your application form or from the alternative application process of a cv and a covering letter or email. So you will have the information the candidates have provided to you. 

If you have used our shortlisting template in the toolkit, you will have a list of the Essential and Desirable criteria down the left hand side and some sort of identifier for the candidates across the top – either their initials or a number.  Those shortlisting are required to allocate a score against each criterion by weighing up the evidence in the application against it. 

The scoring mechanism we recommend is as follows:

- Good evidence that the candidate meets the required criterion - 2 points
- Some evidence that the candidate meets the required criterion - 1 point
- No evidence that the candidate meets the required criterion - 0 points.

See the table on the slide for some perhaps surprising examples of how points should be allocated in a way that precludes assumptions. In this example, the Essential criteria is that the candidate has:

Experience of delivering support services competently and independently to people with a range of physical and mental disabilities.

Then you are given 3 examples of text from applications. At the shortlisting stage, this is exactly the sort of information the person or the panel doing the shortlisting will have.

In the first example, a lot of people would assume that because the individual has been a manager in an environment where the client group has mental disabilities, they have the required experience of delivering support services. However, the applicant does not say this and it should not be assumed. In fact, the applicant should score 0 in this example because they offer no demonstrable experience of delivering support services. They do not meet the criterion. 

In the second example, the applicant states that they have been delivering support services to people with physical disabilities for 3 years, which is long enough for them to reasonably be competent and to be able to work independently. However, they don’t mention experience of working with people with mental disabilities and the fact that they have been on a training course in this area of work should not be interpreted as them having the required experience in this area. The applicant should score 1 in this example because they offer demonstrable experience – but only of 50% of the criterion.

In the third example, the applicant gives a tangible example of being in a job where s/he was required to deliver support services to people with both physical and mental health disabilities, for a length of time in which they could be expected to reasonably be competent and s/he states that s/he as required to both plan and provide the services. The applicant should score 2 points here because they offer demonstrable experience to meet the full criterion.

Shortlisting involves a series of these judgments for each criterion, for each candidate. Scoring candidates in this way removes the subjectivity from the decision as far as possible – if the written evidence isn’t present in the application, then the assessor should not assume it and should award points solely on what is written. Sometimes applicants put forward weak applications and an assessor undertaking a shortlisting exercise may score thinking that based on the job that someone has had, they should have the experience required. It can be even more tempting to do this if you know the candidate or the role they have worked in before. But if it is not in the application, it should not be assumed; to do so makes for an unfair process for other candidates and means you may end up putting forward a candidate for interview who doesn’t in fact meet the minimum criteria required for the role. To help with inclusivity and to address this matter, some employers include guidance notes for applicants in completing a good application in their candidate pack.
