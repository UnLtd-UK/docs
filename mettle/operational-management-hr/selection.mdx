INTRODUCTION TO THE ROOTS HR SELECTION TOOLKIT
The aim of an organisation’s selection process is to enable its officers to objectively
and robustly assess potential candidates for a post against the requirements of the
role, in order to identify not only those candidate(s) who will be able to satisfactorily
perform the role but the one who will, on merit, be the most suitable for the role and
perform the best in the role.
The aim of this Toolkit is to support organisations in understanding and being self-
sufficient in applying the processes that can contribute to developing and
implementing a legally compliant, good practice, effective candidate selection process.
The Toolkit links to template documents that can be used as provided or adapted to
meet the needs of the particular organisation. This Toolkit follows on from, and links
to, our Recruitment Toolkit (released in July 2021).
This Toolkit is aimed at helping small social sector organisations to:
 Understand and meet minimum legislative requirements in selecting
candidates from a shortlist to become employees
 Develop interviews and selection tests to assess and appoint the best
possible candidates
 Make compelling and professional offers of employment to successful
candidates
 Apply good practice whilst enabling an appropriate level of flexibility in their
approach to selection
 Respond effectively, strategically and operationally, to internal and external
change
 Treat applicants fairly in line with equality of opportunity, the principles of
diversity and inclusion and with transparency and accountability
 Maintain the minimum possible cost and administrative burden balanced
against the direct and indirect financial costs of getting the selection process
wrong.
The selection process in this Toolkit starts when shortlisting of applicants has been
carried out and ends with an offer of employment.
The Toolkit is not intended to be prescriptive and decisions taken throughout the
selection and offer processes should be in the context of, and proportionate to the
scale of, the individual organisation and managing and mitigating against its risks.
The Toolkit is not written for the purpose of selecting or offering posts to volunteers,
Trustees or service users who are being supported within employment although many
of the principles and practices applied within it may be useful in those scenarios.

2. Selection Principles
2.1 Everyone should have a right to equal access to employment and, when
employed, should have equal pay and equal access to training and
development.
2.2 The overall aim of a recruitment and selection process is to appoint into a
vacancy the candidate in the employment market at that time who best meets
your selection criteria and is willing to work for your organisation at the salary
offered. Good recruitment and selection processes therefore focus on obtaining
as much relevant evidence about potential candidates as is possible and using
this to make a decision based on merit about the best candidate who will be
offered the role.
2.3 All recruitment and selection decisions inevitably include subjectivity to some
extent. The purpose of a robust selection process therefore is to increase the
likelihood of appointing the best candidate for the role, who meets all of the
essential requirements on the Person Specification and whose fit with the team
and the organisation means they are likely to settle in and remain in
employment for a reasonable period of time.

2.4 All recruitment and selection should be managed in a w ay that reflects, as well
as supports, the organisation in being a good employer and complying with
statutory and regulatory requirements.
2.5 Recruitment and selection is a two-way process and is not just about selecting
a candidate but is also an opportunity for candidates to consider whether this
is the role and organisation for them. A change in job could be a significant
lifestyle change for the individual and their family. As such, every stage of the
recruitment and selection process is an opportunity to show the organisation as
an attractive employer.
2.6 All those involved in recruitment and selection are representing the organisation
and should provide positive but realistic impressions of the job and
organisation, enabling prospective employees to make an informed decision.
2.7 Recruitment and selection processes should be efficient and cost effective,
using the most appropriate methods of assessment for the role. The process
should be expedited promptly and within agreed and published timescales, to
benefit those involved as well as to provide a positive impression to candidates
and of course to avoid losing good candidates to other employers.
2.8 All recruitment and selection activity should comply with the requirements of
the General Data Protection Regulations. See Section 12 for further
information.
2.9 This Toolkit is applicable to internal and external recruitment and selection. As
such the same considerations should be given to internal candidates,
particularly at the shortlist stage, as would be for external candidates.
2.10 It is the responsibility of the employer and the people involved in recruitment
and selection decisions to ensure that they are appropriately competent and
confident in their role and to ensure that candidates are treated fairly and that
decisions are made objectively.
2.11 The guidance in this Toolkit should be considered in conjunction with any
policies your organisation may have in place on recruitment and selection, data
protection, equality, diversity and inclusion and on the employment of ex-
offenders and safeguarding as relevant to the nature and scope of the
organisation.

3. Equality, diversity and inclusion in the Selection process
3.1 Equality of opportunity should be central to any selection process, not only
because it is unlawful to discriminate based on a protected characteristic, but
also because discrimination, stereotyping and favouritism reduces the
opportunity to identify and select the best person for the job based on merit.
3.2 As well as getting the best person for the job, a fair and consistent selection
process will help minimise the risk of:
 Staff turnover
 Absenteeism
 Performance and/or disciplinary issues
 Tribunal claims and the related legal costs, management time and
reputational risk.
3.3 Diversity recognises that, though people have things in common with each
other, they are also different in many ways. In selection, this can, consciously
or unconsciously, lead those responsible for designing and/or implementing the
selection process to make decisions which create barriers or result in excluding
people in some way from the selection process, not because they would be
unable to perform the role but because they do not conform, perhaps to the
organisation’s previous norms or to perceptions held by the interview panel.
3.4 Inclusion is where the differences between people are seen as a benefit, and
where perspectives and differences are shared, leading to better decisions.
Organisations are increasingly committed to demonstrating their position on
inclusion, for reasons of business performance, reputation and ethics, and
recruitment is a key opportunity in the employee journey to do this. We have
seen the Black Lives Matter movement have a positive impact within the sector,
with many organisations actively taking lawful steps to increase ethnic diversity
within their workforce.

3.5 Candidates should therefore be selected solely on th eir ability to do the job
irrespective of age, disability, gender, gender reassignment, race and ethnicity,
religion or belief, sexual orientation, pregnancy or maternity and marriage or
civil partnership. These are known as “protected characteristics”.
3.6 Equality legislation covers direct and indirect discrimination, as well as
discrimination by association or perception, and the responsibility lies with the
employer to show that they have not unlawfully discriminated on the grounds of
the factors above. Further information on these definitions can be found here:
https://www.equalityhumanrights.com/sites/default/files/ea_legal_definitions_0
.pdf
3.7 Individuals involved in recruitment and selection who take discriminatory
actions or decisions are potentially accountable, as well as the organisation
itself, unless the organisation can demonstrate it took all reasonable steps to
prevent the individual from discriminating against someone.
3.8 This does not prevent an employer from taking positive action to address under-
representation of certain groups in its workforce. A way to do this is, for
example, to ensure the advert for a role reaches the under-represented group,
perhaps by placing it in a targeted form of media aiming to reach that
demographic.
3.9 It is generally unlawful for employers to select a candidate over another on the
basis they are in a protected category (see 3.5 above). However:
a) Section 158 of the Equality Act 2010 permits employers to take positive action
to:
 enable or encourage people who share a protected characteristic to
overcome or minimise a disadvantage connected to that characteristic
 meet the needs of people who share a protected characteristic where
those needs are different to those who do not have that characteristic
 encourage and train people from under-represented groups, to help
them participate in an activity in which their participation is low.
b) Section 159 of the Act permits employers to treat one person more favourably
than another equally qualified person in connection with recruitment or
promotion. This enables employers to take into account the under-
representation of disadvantaged groups when selecting between two equally
qualified job candidates. This part of the Act is entirely voluntary but the

provisions are complex and must be handled carefu lly. Any positive action
steps an employer takes must be proportionate to meet its needs. The question
of whether one person is as qualified as another is not only a matter of
academic qualifications but also how the employer has decided who is best for
the job (team fit in accordance with the person specification, high levels of
desirable skills and so on). Employers cannot automatically treat all people with
a particular protected characteristic more favourably than others. Each case
must be considered on its merits. Candidates for senior roles will have
extensive and varied experience, so it will be very hard to decide who is ‘equally
qualified’. If employers assess this incorrectly, a discrimination claim may arise.
The Equality and Human Rights Commission has published guidance on
positive action and recruitment here and the government has a quick start
guide to positive action in recruitment and promotion here.
3.10 Sector regulators, commissioners, grant-makers, funders, customers and
partners are also likely to expect an objective and fair recruitment and selection
process that enables the organisation to identify and select the best candidate
for the role. They may also expect to see initiatives underway to address under-
representation and to promote diversity and inclusion within the workforce.
3.11 Ways in which objectivity can be demonstrated and discrimination avoided in
the selection process generally include:
 Advertising vacancies and holding a selection process and not just offering
roles to family members, friends or a current employee
 Reality-checking the selection criteria to ensure that they do not include any
content that is potentially discriminatory or unreasonably excludes certain
groups without justification
 Not making assumptions about candidates, for example based on name,
how long ago they were at school, whether they went to university, if not
relevant to the role, family or caring responsibilities etc., but instead
focussing on assessing each candidate objectively on how well they meet
your selection criteria based on the evidence they provide to you
 Not making assumptions about the ability of internal people to do the job
based on what you know about them, but instead, providing them with the
same opportunity provided to external people to provide evidence (which

may include previous experience or voluntary work etc of which you are not
aware) to demonstrate their suitability for the role
 Not including age-related criteria in selection criteria and avoiding words like
‘young’, ‘recently qualified’ and ‘mature’, as these could potentially be seen
to be discriminatory to older or younger workers
 Ensuring job and person specifications, selection criteria and advert wording
avoid language which is gender specific or gender coded
 Not requiring candidates to have experience or qualifications that cannot be

 Not specifying a required length of experience. Using such a requirement,

 Being prepared to make reasonable adjustments if you are informed by a

declaring at the earliest possible stage if they know an applicant.

4. The interview panel
4.1 Every selection process will require the assessment of candidates who are
successful in the shortlisting process. This may be undertaken by one person
or it may be by one or more officers of the organisation who form a “panel” to
assess the candidates. It need not be the same person or panel who conducted
the shortlisting. References to the “interview panel” in these guidance notes
should be interpreted as meaning anyone who is responsible for selection
decisions in a recruitment campaign, regardless of whether this is one person
or a number of people. It is best practice to include within the panel the line
manager for the role wherever possible. If the panel includes more than one
person, it is good practice to decide who will lead and co-ordinate the panel and
the process; this person is referred to as the “Chair of the Interview Panel”.
4.2 It can be hard to secure diary time, especially in recruitment processes for
senior roles where the interview panel may constitute Trustees who are
volunteer officers responsible for the governance of the organisation. Therefore
it makes sense to identify the interview panel at the earliest opportunity - we
recommend doing this at the point of deciding the closing date for advertising.
4.3 It goes without saying that those on the interview panel should not create a
conflict of interest to the assessment of the candidates. Usually declaring and
noting this at the shortlisting and interview stage, as appropriate, will be
sufficient.
4.4 It is imperative for a fair process that the interview panel is recently trained in
your organisation’s assessment techniques, recruitment and selection
generally and in its approach to equality, diversity and inclusion. The Training
Presentation accompanying this toolkit covers this only briefly and generically.
Please contact Roots HR on hello@rootshr.org.uk / 01562 840060 for further
information on suitable training for interview panels and those involved in
recruitment and selection decisions.

4.5 Some employers have recruitment and selection pol icies or processes that
include staff members and/or service users on the interview panel and this is
often viewed as best practice in the social sector and can even be a
requirement of commissioners. Careful consideration should be given to their
training and any other special needs, to ensure a positive experience for
everyone and to protect confidentiality throughout the process.
4.6 We sometimes see wider stakeholders of the post under recruitment being
invited into the process to meet the candidates, for example to have lunch with
them on the selection day. Whilst this can be a good way for candidates to
gauge the culture of the organisation they are thinking of joining, it is important
to clarify that this is not a scored or assessed part of the process and that no
input from these stakeholders will be sought or brought to bear upon the
assessors or the selection process.
4.7 The interview panel should approve the interview, any selection tests and the
scoring system (see Sections 7, 8 and 9 below), ahead of the selection day.
5. The selection process – design and preparation
5.1 The selection process itself should be agreed at the earliest opportunity, ideally
when the post is planned for advertising. This includes determining the methods
of assessment to be used at the interview stage and the timetable.
5.2 Typically a selection process will always include an interview but this may not
robustly test all aspects of the Essential criteria on the Person Specification and
additional types of selection testing have become increasingly popular over the
last 10-20 years to provide additional insight into the candidates’ abilities. The
aim is to assess each candidate for each of the Essential criteria and any of the
highly Desirable criteria on the Person Specification at least once throughout
the process.
5.3 When a series of tests are applied to candidates over the course of a day or
more, it may be referred to as an “assessment centre”. Any tests the candidate
undertakes should be directly relevant to the job and designed to clearly supply
evidence for one or more important criterion in the Person Specification. Many
of the most useful tests also require a significant time input in design and
assessment, so the value of the additional evidence needs to be weighed up
against that cost. Those routinely used include a range of the following:

 a presentation, for which the title is usually provided in advance of the
assessment day to enable the candidate to prepare
 a work-sample exercise, for example a written exercise, usually based
on a true-to-life scenario, case study and/or company data
 a group exercise
 a roleplay scenario
 an in-tray exercise
 literacy, numeracy, spatial awareness tests and / or management
scenario tests (aptitude tests)
 personality profiling.
5.4 When designing tests, it is usual to limit the amount of time allowed for each
exercise. Employers must also make reasonable adjustments for candidates
with a disability (which may include extending the amount of time allowed to
undertake a test).
5.5 A typical way to ensure that all the Essential and highly Desirable criteria are
covered off is to list them out in a table setting out the tests through which you
intend to put the candidates. An assessment planning template (and example
of its use) is included in the Template pack accompanying this Toolkit.
5.6 Remember that some evidence of how the candidate meets the criteria for the
role will come through the application process (especially if it includes a
personal statement in which the candidate is required to state how they meet
the essential criteria for the role).
5.7 Also, some evidence will usually be provided during on-boarding, such as
certificates evidencing qualifications.
5.8 It is worth considering at this point whether the interview and assessment
process is intended to be a one-stage process or whether the panel wishes to
see the final 2 or 3 candidates in a second interview. If the latter, think about
what criteria the panel wishes to assess at first interview stage and what it will
assess at second interview stage. It is becoming increasingly common,
especially for senior roles, to use personality profiling and some aptitude testing
at second interview stage (see point 8.3).
5.9 For work-sample exercises, it is usual to allow candidates access to the
information and facilities they would have in the course of their working day,
such as the internet, Google and other professional resources.

5.10 Tests should be used in conjunction with other evidenc e available through the
recruitment and selection process (i.e. application and interview) to support an
evidence-based judgement on the suitability of a candidate for the role. They
should not be used in isolation to rule candidates out of the process.
5.11 It is not imperative for the whole interview panel to assess each exercise. For
example, in-tray exercises could be invigilated by a trained administrator and
scored by the line manager or another competent person alone and the scores
provided to the interview panel. The principle here is one of consistency; the
same person or people should assess and score each part of the interview
process.
5.12 A scoring mechanism should be included in the design of the interview and
tests to ensure that the panel have a consistent process by which to allocate a
numerical score to each interview question and to the tests, depending on the
performance of the candidate. Further information can be found on scoring in
Section 9 below.
5.13 Aptitude testing and personality profiling (sometimes referred to as
psychometric testing) are designed by occupational psychologists and should
only be administered by a trained professional. In a small organisation, this
means that this will usually be outsourced to a consultant. More information can
be found in point 8.3 below.
5.14 Whatever the assessment process, it should be professionally planned and run.
This is important in terms of the impression that it gives candidates of the
organisation. The person(s) responsible for designing the selection process
may be a member or members of the interview panel, an HR professional or
other suitable officer of the organisation or an external consultant.
5.15 The selection timetable is a critical part of selection planning process. It sets
out the time required for each shortlisted candidate to undertake the interview
and any required tests and allocates resources to the assessment and scoring
processes and to invigilation. It is important for the smooth running of the day
to ensure that sufficient time is allocated to enable candidates and assessors
to move between rooms and tests and to access toilets and refreshments and
for assessors to conclude their scoring for each candidate.
5.16 Following on from this, where candidates are coming to your premises for an
assessment centre, it will be important to have suitably trained persons to:

6. Inviting candidates to interview
6.1 Candidates should be invited to interview or assessment centre in writing, either
by email or letter. The letter should include:
 the date, time and location of the process
 the length of time the candidate should expect to be with you
 the nature of the tests they will undergo in addition to an interview (if any)
 the names of the people on the interview panel
 any preparation the candidate is required to do in advance of the day (eg
preparing to make a presentation as part of the assessment process)
 whom they should contact re reasonable adjustments for a disability
 the deadline by which the candidate should confirm attendance at the
interview and to whom.
A template letter for inviting candidates to interview is included in the Template
pack with this Toolkit.
6.2 Ideally you will get the invitations to the interview or assessment process out
within 48 hours of the closing date and try to give 5 – 7 days’ notice of the
interview date, particularly if there is preparation the candidates need to do in
advance. Without being unreasonable with your timescale, you should seek to
move through the recruitment and selection process briskly and without lengthy
or unexplained delays for the candidates. Avoid elongating the process
because you will find that good candidates are going for, and being offered,
other jobs and you will lose them. Ideally you will have included the date(s) of
the interview / assessment process in the candidate pack, meaning candidates
Page 12 of 24

SELECTION TOOLKIT
will be holding the date(s) for interview and you wi ll be able to maximise
attendance from your preferred candidates.
6.3 If you have shortlisted candidate(s) who cannot attend on the date you are
holding interviews or your assessment centre, it is down to the interview panel
to decide if it can realistically convene on an alternative date / time and if the
testing environment can be replicated on another day. It may be preferable to
invite another applicant instead. For this reason, we recommend “standing
down” those applicants that are not initially invited to interview only once the
selection process has taken place.
7. The selection process - interview
7.1 If your selection process relies solely on an interview, then progress to 7.2
below. If your selection process includes other tests in addition to an interview,
then the assessment planning template (see Section 5 above) will enable you
to identify the criteria in the Person Specification which you will need to test
through interview.
7.2 First interviews in themselves are rarely planned to exceed an hour and usually
include up to 10 questions.
7.3 When drafting interview questions, avoid “closed” questions (which lead to a
Yes or No answer) and use “open” questions (those which start with “Who”,
“What”, “When”, Where” and “How” which mean that the candidate is required
to provide a fuller answer.
Example:
“Have you any relevant experience for this role?” (closed question)
Versus
“What relevant experience would you bring to this post from previous roles, were you
appointed?” (open question)
7.4 Draft questions in clear, simple sentences and ensure that only one question is
asked at a time. Draft additional follow-on questions to probe further, where
necessary.
Page 13 of 24

SELECTION TOOLKIT
Example:
“What have you achieved in marketing and have you ever been responsible for social
media?”
Versus
“What marketing achievements are you most proud of?”
With a follow-on question of:
“What level of responsibility have you held for your organisation’s social media?”
7.5 Past performance is generally regarded as the best indicator of future
performance so use competency-based questions to allow candidates to bring
forward their real-life examples of achievements at work.
Example:
To test how a candidate may be able to cope well with the line management of a
challenging team:
“Tell us about the most demanding or complex people management situation you have
had to manage. Describe what made it demanding or complex and what you did to
address it”.
Follow-on questions could include:
- “What help did you have from others?”
- “What would you do differently if you were managing a similar situation in the future?”
This will enable the panel to see if the candidate has in fact had sufficient experience
to manage the team they would lead were they appointed to your role.
7.6 Use only questions that relate to the criteria in your Job Description and Person
Specification.

SELECTION TOOLKIT
Example:
“Tell us about your previous experience”
Versus
“Tell us how your previous experience in fundraising will enable you to meet the
requirements of this role”.
7.7 When you have drafted all of your questions, think about the sequencing of
them within the interview. It’s a good idea to ask questions early in the process
which will be easy for the candidate to answer and therefore will break the ice
and get them talking. Questions about the technical aspects of a role seem to
sit well in the middle of an interview and questions around values and
motivation always feel like a positive ending. The questions should be cut and
pasted into an Interview Notes template, included within the Template pack
accompanying this Toolkit and which provides the panel with the questions,
space for them to make notes of the candidate’s answers and space to record
the score they give for each question.
7.8 Think also about the quantity of questions and the likely amount of time it will
take the candidates to answer each one fully, where they can. Competency
questions usually take around 5 minutes each to answer so this will inform the
number of questions you can include in the time allocated to the interview in
your Assessment Plan.

9.4 A typical scoring system for interview questions is as follows:

- A little evidence that the candidate meets a few of the requirements – 1 point

### Why is good practice in selection important?

Cost

Productivity

Reputation

So why is good practice in selection processes so important?

If you received the Roots HR Recruitment Toolkit, launched in July 2021, and went through the training presentation to that Toolkit, you’ll know this already because the answer to this question is pretty much the same for recruitment as it is for selection.

Getting the right person first time is important. The sooner a new employee is on board and working for you, the sooner you feel the benefit of their knowledge and experience. This ups your productivity and takes the burden off of other people. 

It will inevitably take a certain amount of time to get new people onboard – for example, to advertise, wait for applications and then to interview the candidates usually takes at least 1 month for a permanent role but then, assuming they are currently in work, there is the wait for them to join you once you have made them an offer, they have accepted it and given their notice to their current employer. That may be another month for many roles but a notice period can be 3 or even 6 months for a senior role. So you want to make the right decision first time.

The CIPD’s Resourcing and Talent Planning Survey Report 2020 puts the overall median cost of recruiting a senior role at £5,000 and the cost of other roles at £2,000. So you definitely want to make the right decision first time.

Getting it wrong is expensive. It impacts on your business adversely in terms of productivity and reputation. It will affect the bottom line, service delivery and performance as well as team morale. Making a poor recruitment or selection decision will involve resources spent on managing underperformance and the risk of dismissing someone in the future. It is demoralising for the employee and frustrating for the manager. It can impact on your organisational reputation (in many ways, eg word of mouth about what you are like as an employer, service delivery, how it looks if you have to repeat advert again etc). There are also the practical costs of re-recruiting and covering the vacancy until a suitable candidate is found and inducted. 

One of the worst indicators of an unfair recruitment and selection process is when there is no process  at all, ie when a decision is taken to appoint or promote someone either without giving other candidates an opportunity to apply or considering them for a role. Effectively that is favouritism and whilst it might shorten the time taken to recruit and reduce the amount of management resources involved in that process, it is no guarantee that the person appointed will be successful in the role, nor that they will stay in it. Nor will it look or feel fair to other employees who may have wanted to apply for that role or who don’t feel the individual is suitable for it. 

Savvy employers recognise that their recruitment and selection process is a window into their organisation and is a way for their stakeholders to judge them – either positively or negatively. If you subscribe to that view, and let’s face it, it makes sense, then you’ll want to make sure that your recruitment and selection process reflects your standards, your products and services and your ethics to very best effect. 

Furthermore, organisations are increasingly committed to demonstrating their position on inclusion, for reasons of business performance reputation and ethics, and the selection process is a key opportunity in the employee journey to do this. We have seen the Black Lives Matter movement have a positive impact within the sector, with many organisations actively taking lawful steps to increase ethnic diversity within their workforce. 

So it is important to design and implement a robust assessment process to test your shortlisted candidates against the requirements of the role – which should be set out in your job description and person specification. This Toolkit is designed to help you to implement a merit-based selection process, ie one which enables you to appoint the best candidate for the detailed and very specific requirements of your role. The benefits of this speak for themselves.

The main legislation relating to selection is the same for recruitment – it is the Equality Act 2010, which makes it unlawful to discriminate directly or indirectly, by association or by perception.

This is about treating people differently in a way that disadvantages them, for no justifiable reason. 

There are anti discriminatory laws in employment for all of the reasons on this slide. In recruitment and selection, anti-discriminatory practice applies to documents such as job descriptions, person specifications and advertisements and to practices such as shortlisting and selection methods (such as interview questions, tests and assessment and scoring processes).

We covered this in the training for our Recruitment Toolkit but in case you haven't seen it and because this is important, I’ll just recap on it again here:

Definitions and examples:
Direct discrimination: treating a person less favourably than others are treated on grounds of gender, race, age etc. 
Eg: selecting a white or male applicant over a better qualified black or female applicant.

Indirect discrimination: applying a provision, criterion or practice equally but which disproportionately disadvantages a certain group without any justifiable reason. 
Eg applying a dress code which makes the role unattractive to certain ethnic groups when it isn’t an essential requirement for the role.

Discrimination by Association: treating a person in a particular way because of their relationship with someone else who has a protected characteristic.
Eg not making a job offer to someone because they have responsibility for caring for an elderly relative  

Discrimination by Perception: treating a person in a particular way because you assume they have a characteristic which may not in fact be the case.
Eg not making a job offer to a female candidate who you consider to have a masculine appearance, because you think she is a lesbian and assume she will not be well-received by the team.

There is also a legal responsibility to make reasonable adjustments to both the interview process and the job for disabled candidates.

We often find ourselves helping employers to reply to communications from candidates where they believe the recruitment or selection process has discriminated against them. When a fair process has been followed, with a good audit trail to support it, it is easy to do this and to close off the problem at the earliest indication. 

And remember, it is not just about ensuring your organisation has a defence in the event of a legal challenge to your selection process; it is also about valuing diversity and being seen to be genuinely inclusive.

There is some really great additional information on pages 3-7 in the Guidance Notes with this Toolkit under Equality, diversity and inclusion in the Selection Process. In particular, have a look at paragraphs 3.8 and 3.9 which set out the circumstances in which positive action is allowed in order that you can work lawfully towards increasing diversity and inclusion in your organisation.

### Planning a selection process

As with the advertising and shortlisting process, there is a certain amount of planning to do, even if your selection process only involves an interview and a panel of one person. If it includes tests and more assessors, there is more work to be done in advance, especially if those assessors are Trustees of your organisation and aren’t familiar with your recruitment and selection policy and practices.  

Timeline
There should already be a timeline in place from when the advert was placed – but this is a good time to revisit it and to make sure that it is still on track and works for everyone. Make sure the assessors and any supporting personnel are still all available on the day you plan to hold the interview and any tests and that the meeting rooms or videolink facilities you require are still available.

Designing the selection process
Your selection process should aim to test, as a minimum, each of the Essential criteria in the person specification for the role at least once. The guidance notes in our Toolkit cover how best to do this in some detail – have a look at Section 5 on pages 8-11 and we cover it further on the next slide in this training presentation. Ideally you will design the selection process whilst the advert is running and candidates are applying, so that you are ready (or almost ready) to invite candidates to interview as soon as possible after the closing date. 

Inviting candidates
You will send out invitations to candidates and the lead-in time from them receiving the invitation ahead of the selection day should be around 3-7 working days. If you published the selection date at the time you advertised the role, it is reasonable to expect most candidates to be holding the date, pending hearing whether they are being invited to interview, in which case 3 days notice will normally be sufficient. However, if you haven’t published the date in advance or if the candidates need to do any preparation in advance, it is only fair to give them notice to do this, usually allowing for this to be done over a weekend.

Remember, at all times, your aim is to progress through the process as quickly as possible because candidates will be looking at other roles in tandem with yours and if you don’t move promptly, you may lose your preferred candidates to other employers. 

Engaging the panel with the selection process
It will take some time to pull the interview, tests and scoring process together and once documents are ready in draft, they should be approved by the Chair of the panel before being finalised. You will need to factor in time ahead of the selection day for briefing in to the panel how to use the interview and particularly the tests and how to score them. The Templates pack accompanying this Toolkit contains a template of Guidance Notes for the Selection Panel to help with briefing them but this will also need tailoring to suit the selection process you have designed.

Candidate pack and Assessor pack
On the selection day, both candidates and assessors will need access to a series of documents to ensure the day runs smoothly and to ensure they have the information they need to take part in the day. Therefore your planning means that you need to put together a pack for both the candidates and the assessors and the assessors will need theirs ahead of the day, to familiarise themselves in advance and to refer to during any briefing they may be receiving. 

If the event is being run virtually (eg by videolink), it  may be practical to email all parties their packs in advance – but assessors in particular may require some or all of the documents in hard copy so you may wish to check this in advance and to allow time for printing and posting if they don’t have printing facilities at home. If the event is being run live and in person, then neither candidates nor assessors can be guaranteed to have access to emails on the day and it will be more practical to provide the packs in hard copy.

### Skills required in a selection process – planning and co-ordination

We’re going to have a think now about developing and applying some of the skills needed in your organisation to prepare for and deliver a successful selection process.

The first 2 are planning and co-ordination. 

Planning can be defined as ability to "think about the future" or mentally anticipate the right way to carry-out a task or reach a specific goal. The sorts of skills that are involved are:

Accurately estimating time and effort required to complete a task
Identifying and organising systems and required resources
Organising personal time to carry out responsibilities
Maintaining adequate preparation time for scheduled meetings and deadlines.

One way to go about this is to put the key milestones and smaller milestones for the work onto a gant chart or just a spreadsheet, in chronological sequence, then to identify the deadline by when each of the milestones has to be done. For a selection process, that might look like the slide (the key milestones are in bold):

Interview questions and selection tests drafted
Interview questions and selection tests reviewed by Chair of Selection Panel
Interview questions and selection tests finalised
Interview questions and selection tests approved by Chair of Selection Panel
Invites to selection day sent out
Selection panel receives assessor pack
Selection panel briefed ahead of selection day
Selection day takes place
Selection panel makes decision on successful candidate
Offer made
Unsuccessful candidates declined

Then under each of these milestones, make a list of all the individual tasks involved in that part of the selection process. For example, for getting the candidate invites out, this might include:
Drafting the template invite letter
Getting this approved by the Chair of the Selection Panel
Creating a version of the letter for each of the individual candidates invited to the selection day
Emailing or posting these out.

Finally, you can add in a column and estimate how long each of the sub-tasks might take you. For example:

Drafting the template invite letter (0.5 hrs using Roots HR’s template)
Getting this approved by the Chair of the Selection Panel (½ a day of turnaround time if the Chair knows the letter is coming to them for approval)
Creating a version of the letter for each of the individual candidates invited to the selection day (0.5 hrs max per candidate including emailing / posting time)
Emailing or posting these out (included above).

In this way you can see you can get the invite letters out in under a day. You can do the same exercise for all of the other milestones, to see exactly what work is involved so that you can estimate how long it is likely to take. 

Co-ordination skills demonstrate that you can manage projects, people and other resources at the same time, bringing them together to achieve a desired outcome. Communication is key. You’ll need to:
Express your needs clearly (which will come from your project planning above) in order that others understand them and give you their time in order that those parts of the work that need their input are achieved
Be able to do this potentially with people more senior than you and more junior than you
Give them advance notice of when you need their time 
Negotiate with them according to their other diary commitments
Get back to them promptly when they respond to you
Use spell checker and other formatting tools to ensure all written outputs are appropriate and professional.

Building these skills in your organisation will mean a successful selection process and one which reflects your organisation positively.

### Skills required in a selection process – assessment

Interviews and selection processes require those involved to make judgments on behalf of the employer and not everyone is comfortable about this. The most important thing is to make no assumptions; you are looking for evidence of how well the candidates meet especially the Essential but also the Desirable criteria on the person specification. Assessing and scoring interviews and tests involves a series of judgments for each criterion, for each candidate. Scoring candidates based on the evidence they provide removes the subjectivity from the scoring decision as far as possible – assessors should award points solely on what is said or written. 

Practical aspects
The scoring system for each interview or test should be included as part of that exercise by the person designing it. This makes it straightforward for the panel to award points with some consistency. 

Our recommended scoring system for interviews awards up to 5 points per question (see 9.4 on page 20 of the Guidance Notes accompanying this Toolkit). 
We have included a suggested scoring system for each of the templates / examples of tests included in the Templates pack with this Toolkit – there is a presentation exercise, a work sample written exercise and an in tray exercise.

When assessing an interview or an oral test such as a presentation, you should concentrate on listening to what the candidate is saying, and make notes as you go. Don’t hesitate to ask the candidate for more information or to clarify a point if you are unsure of what they mean or how much they know. Don’t worry about allocating a score at that time if that means you will miss listening to the answer to the next question! Scoring is best done immediately after the end of the exercise and you can use your written notes as an aide memoire. 

Critical thinking
You will be scoring the candidate using the scoring system provided as part of the interview or test and based on your understanding of what is required from the candidate in the role.  It’s important you apply critical thinking to the information given by the candidate when you are allocating a score to it. It’s neither good enough nor appropriate to award each candidate full marks (or average marks) for each question based on you not knowing the difference between a strong, average or weak answer to a question. You will have the interview questions and tests in advance; make sure as an assessor that you have read the job description and person specification and if necessary, that you have asked the line manager for the role and/or other personnel about the standard of performance that is required in the areas being tested. 

Critical thinking is the skill to develop here. Critical thinking is “thinking about thinking”. It is about using your ability to reason and about being active in your learning and questioning ideas, arguments and findings. Being able to understand and create structured, reasoned arguments is central to critical thinking. In the selection process, it is about evaluating what you read, hear, think, experience and observe. When you are assessing candidates, you should be assessing how well ideas, statements, experience etc are backed up so that you can make a reasoned judgement about how strong they are against the requirements of the role and therefore award a score. 

Here’s an example using the suggested scoring in 9.4 in the Guidance Notes:

If the interview question for a Support Services Manager role is:

“Tell us about the budgets you have managed and what success you have in this area.”

Let’s imagine this question is asked because there is an Essential requirement on the person specification for the postholder to have successfully managed a budget of at least £500,000 pa.

Candidate 1 answers:
“I have been managing budgets in all of my jobs for the last 10 years. For the last 3 years I have been responsible for a budget of over £1m pa. Two years ago I was asked to make in-year savings against my budget of 10% and I did this to some extent by renegotiating with a couple of suppliers but I still had to make one person redundant. I was given a larger budget and span of control in the following year because of the way I am able to manage budgets even in difficult times. 

This answer confirms the candidate has managed budgets for a considerable time and that they have considerable experience in managing a budget greater than the £500,000 pa required for the role. They have given 2 examples of recent achievements in managing that budget, one in making a saving and one in being given a larger span and amount. It would be appropriate to view this as a strong answer and to award this candidate 5 points “Strong evidence that the candidate meets all of the requirements”.

Candidate 2 answers:
“Last year my line manager was off for 3 months and I stepped up into their role and managed our team budget of £850,000 pa. In that time, I identified some savings we could make by changing our waste management company and that has now saved us £10k pa.

This answer confirms that the candidate has managed a budget greater than that required for the role but in an acting up capacity and not for any substantial length of time and the amount of experience may be regarded as insufficient by the panel, especially if this is a key requirement for the role. However, the candidate offers some evidence of success in managing the budget in that short time. A good interview panel would probe further to find out if this is the only time the candidate has managed a budget and if there were any further successes. If no further evidence is forthcoming then this candidate could potentially score 3 points for “Good evidence that the candidate meets some of the requirements”.

Candidate 3 answers:
“I manage a budget of about £650k pa in my current role. I get reports every month from our Finance department and I check that they look right and that all of the income and expenditure is as I expect it to be. If they don’t look right, I report it to Finance.

This answer confirms the candidate has managed a budget greater than the £500,000 pa required for the role but there is no indication of how long they have done this, their role in respect of it is presented as passive and there is no evidence of any success. In fact, it appears to be down to the Finance department to take any action required. A good interview panel would use probing questions here to find out how long the candidate has been responsible for this budget, whether in fact the candidate does have a more active role in managing it than they have described and to try to understand what, if any, achievements have been made. However if no further evidence is forthcoming then this candidate would score 1 or 2 points at best for “A little (or Some) evidence that the candidate meets a few of the requirements”.

For each candidate above, there may be some slight variation in the scoring, and that is to be expected. However, any wide variations can be discussed by the panel at the end of the process, before scores are finalised and an offer is made. This does not mean that any one panel member should be pressured to change their score but it may be that there are different interpretations of what is required of the role or by the organisation and when these are discussed, that may mean one or more panel members wishes to award a different score.

### Skills required in a selection process - feedback

Finally, let’s have a look at feedback.

You’ll no doubt find that you are often asked for feedback throughout the recruitment and selection process. Feedback is about recognising and affirming good performance and offering corrective suggestions and it is a very important part of making sure that applicants and interview candidates feel well treated by your organisation, even if they are unsuccessful at any stage. Many employers these days don’t offer feedback at the application stage due to volume of applicants – after all, it is for the recipient's and not the employer's benefit - although we recommend doing this if you can and we cover it in our Recruitment Toolkit, available on request by emailing info@rootshr.org.uk.

We particularly recommend offering feedback after the interview / selection stage. Remember, all of the candidates who have been through your selection process will have invested considerable time in the opportunity, but only one of them will get the outcome they hoped for. We sometimes talk to clients about making these candidates feel like your job is “the best job they never got”, meaning that they are left with a strongly positive impression of your organisation even though they never got the job. People who feel this way about you as an employer will potentially re-apply in future, advocate for you as an employer and generally support you, your work and your brand. In a competitive labour market, that must surely be a compelling reason to put some time in to feeding back to candidates.  Furthermore, if a candidate receives good feedback and is motivated to act on it, they may reapply to you in the future and be good enough to get the job. 

If you do decide to offer feedback, not everyone will take it up and we recommend only giving feedback in a recruitment and selection situation to those who seek it out. We always recommend it is given verbally only, so that it becomes a 2-way process; also you can gauge how the recipient is feeling about what you are saying and you can use tone to soften or emphasise your remarks. We usually advise clients to do it by phone call or videolink, a few days after the selection process when everyone has had opportunity to reflect on it. 

The person giving feedback on behalf of the interview panel should be confident, competent and experienced in giving feedback. They don’t have to have been on the panel but they will need to be able to put together in advance comments that will feel relevant to the candidate from the panel’s interview and selection test notes. Never give feedback without being very well prepared. 

When preparing the feedback to go through with the candidate, the focus should be both on what the person did and how it was done. 

Feedback should be about behaviour not personality. 
Feedback should describe the effect of the person's behaviour on you (in this case on the selection panel). 
Feedback should be as specific as possible.

Allow up to 30 minutes, although it will often take less. A good way to open up the conversation is to ask the recipient how they thought the selection process went. How did they think they got on in the interview and each of the tests? It can help you considerably if their own assessment of how they did is similar to the feedback you are going to give them and this is often the case.

Then go through each of the interview questions and then the tests with them, refreshing the candidate on what they were asked to do (it is surprising how quickly memories fade after the event!) and then go through with them what the assessors were looking for in terms of scoring and why they allocated the scores they did. It can be helpful to use phrases like:

“You [said / wrote] xx, which is a good answer but others also [talked about / referred to] xx which earned them a higher score”
“This didn’t appear to be a strong area for you; the panel expected candidates to be able to [talk / write] in depth about xx but the evidence of your experience in this area was limited to xx”
“You [wrote / told us] xx which was very much what the assessors were looking for and you scored well / maximum points in that question”.

In the event you decide to offer feedback on the application in writing (which we don’t recommend) make sure it is factual, evidence-based, non-discriminatory and related to the content of the interview and tests and the scores achieved in shortlisting. Ensure it is well presented in terms of spelling and formatting and always keep a copy on the candidate’s file.
