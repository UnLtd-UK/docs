Introduction
This workshop is designed as an introductory module for any UnLtd award winner, or any small- or
medium-sized enterprise looking to review its brand, branding and brand value propositions.
Rationale
The design of this workshop is based on the following rationale.
 It is planned as a half-day day workshop
 All participants have started their social ventures
 It can be adapted to be valuable to award winners at all stages of the journey
 It aims to raise awareness and understanding of key terms around brand, branding and brand
value propositions
 It is interactive – with minimal reading and writing on the day and plenty of opportunity for peer-
to-peer conversations
 It will introduce tools to allow the brand to be built so that it is believable, achievable and
relatable
 It provides a perspective that will greatly inform business planning, marketing and
communications, customer journey planning, recruitment, employee engagement, stakeholder
and supplier management, and investor relations.
 There is a poster, which accompanies this presentation, outlining the process in detail, including
a diagnostic tool (hard copy only)
 There are two additional tools available to develop pen portraits and create a brand value
proposition (soft copies that should be printed out for workshop participants)
 There is a 70-slide PowerPoint presentation to accompany this workshop, upon which this guide
is based.
Facilitation
This workshop is designed to be facilitated by Award Managers. Facilitators have a good understanding
of the content, and can quote relevant examples from their own experiences but they are not
‘deliverers of information or providers of answers’. The expert input will come from the social
entrepreneurs in the room. The role of the facilitator is to draw this knowledge out so it can be shared
v1.0 - 2016

with the others in the room.
Aims and outcomes
Aims
For UnLtd award winners to:
 Gain an understanding of what a brand is (and isn’t)
 Understand why brand is particularly important for social businesses
 See how to review their existing brand using a simple diagnostic tool
 Share a simple process that they can follow for themselves, use in other business planning and integrate
into other activities.
Outcomes
By the end of the workshop participants will have had the opportunity to:
 Think through their organisational purpose
 Create a draft primary pen portrait
 Carry out a simple brand diagnostic
 Develop an initial draft brand value proposition.
Who would benefit from this workshop?
This workshop is primarily targeted at those who have an established small- to medium-sized business, even if it
has only been established for a relatively short period of time. Start-ups can certainly use the tools as part of their
business planning and in developing their brand and branding for the first time, although they will not be making
decisions on the basis of a track record, but rather more on desk and market research, plus their own
experiences.
What you will need for a facilitated workshop
 This guide for facilitators
 A copy of presentation, with the slides hidden that you don’t want to use
 The two worksheets (pen portrait and value proposition canvas) printed on A3, one each per person
 Copies of the poster, A simple guide to reviewing your brand, one person
 PostIt notes (some people prefer these to writing on the worksheets and poster), pens, notepaper.

Slide 4
A good place to start is probably being clear the difference between brand and branding.
Broadly speaking branding is putting logos on things.
There are four types of logo.
The first is a logotype (where the word ‘logo’ came from) and is the brand name in type.
This is probably the most common. For example, Dior or Ford, but also Google and FedEx.
The second is a pictorial symbol, like Apple’s apple, and Shell’s shell.
The third is a more abstract symbol, such as NatWest’s three chevrons, or Nike’s swoosh.
Finally, is a monograph or an insignia. Such as McDonald’s ‘golden arches, or Gucci’s Gs.
There may be a combination of all of these, but in themselves they are not a brand, they
are a brand mark, and part of the brand identity.
So, where did the idea of brand come from?

There are a number of different models that explain how brands are organised. The way
they are organised differs, but essentially everything boils down to the same thing.
The freestanding structure, or sometimes called branded structure
This is where an organisation has lots of independent products and services, each with
their own brand, such as Unilever or P&G. There are lots of advantages of this.
The main ones are that if you spot an opportunity in the market, probably through
customer insight, you can develop a product that people will want. It will probably be
within your general capabilities (for example, a fast moving consumer goods, such as
food, or household products), but it can either by quite different from other products in
your portfolio (washing powder and ice creams) or even competitive to them (Persil and
Surf, for example). What you are trying to achieve is build market share through carefully
managed individual brands. This makes this model very flexible. If a brand doesn’t
succeed, you can drop it and there is unlikely to be an impact on the rest of the portfolio.
The focus is on the features and benefits of the individual products, although some
brands are sufficiently strong to carry their own brand promise, such as Persil and ‘dirt is
good’.
v1.0 - 2016

The principal disadvantage of this is that it is expensive and is a model only really
adopted by big multi-nationals who can sustain the R&D costs and launch marketing.
It also means that the company that owns these brands is quite anonymous. This hasn’t
been a problem in the past, but over the past 10 years or so these companies want
people to know about who is behind their products. This is important for investor
relations, their corporate social responsibility (CSR) activity and recruitment. You will
have noticed the ‘swingy’ Unilever flag at the end of Unilever’s advertisements.
Unified structure, or sometimes called the monolithic approach
This is where there is one single master brand under which everything is unified. There is
one name, with one visual system. The features and benefits of products and services are
less important than an overall brand promise, and therefore this leads to brand loyalty
and trust. Brand extensions are built by descriptors. This is the model organisations are
increasingly moving to as it is more cost effective. Investment in the overarching brand,
rather than individual product and service brands brings huge positive image transfer
benefits. If I told you BMW were to bring out a new car already you will have an idea of
what the car will be like, even without being told what the car is. This, of course, can
work the other way round as Volkswagen has recently discovered.
Branded house structure, family of brands, or endorsed brands
Models differ here, but the essence of them is that there is a clear marketing synergy
between product and service name and the parent. Product and service each has a clear
market presence, benefiting from the parent association. This is particularly true of
brands where there are distinct target audiences. For example, customers of cargo in
aviation are distinct from first class customers as are communications that attract
employees. The difference between family of brands and branded house is that the
former is based on an idea (which is a much stronger, or although potentially riskier
option) as it has increased flexibility and is the reason Richard Branson has an empire
from aviation, to broadband, to space flight, to trains, to retail. A branded house
structure relies on being within a sector or specialism. While Richard Branson could open
a ice cream parlour tomorrow, it’s unlikely British Airways would have the credibility to
be able to do so.
In addition to this there is a ‘hybrid’ version. This is one often seen in organisations
where the business has grown by merger and acquisition. Some brands are seen to be
too strong to loose their identity and therefore may compromise the business if it takes
the parent’s brand. One of the problems of this is often described, however, of building a
business of siloes, often with different cultures. It also brings with it speculation by some
people who wonder whether a business’s values will be carried forward by a new parent,

such as was the case for Green & Black’s when they were acquired by Cadbury’s and then
Mondelez International.
Do you recognise these models for your own business? Now that the advantages and
disadvantages have been laid out, is it worth reconsidering how your brand structure is
organised?
One of the main reasons why brands are moving towards a more monolithic approach is
because they now realise that that the ethos of a parent brand is what drives its purpose
and appeal to a wide variety of people. In the past people have often been described in
‘buckets’ – customers aged 18 to 25, employees, shareholders, service users, and so on –
and you used to hear that there was an employee brand, an investor brand and so on.
However, this doesn’t allow for the fact that people can wear many hats.

Slide 31
This is a simple process to help to get to a brand model that will help you make all the
right decisions in your business.
Start with your audiences. List them all. Can you differentiate them better than
customers, staff, shareholders?
Avoid shying away from to specific. The temptation is to say you want to keep your
audiences broad. But in fact you will have one primary audience. This audience will be
your most valuable, your most loyal and your greatest advocates. They will be the ones
that will allow you to drive your business forward. Having a clear idea of who will do this
is important as part of your business planning, but also focusing your brand. They may
not be your existing customers, who may be loyal but not willing to try your new
products, or they may be valuable, but are expensive to maintain, and will leave you at
the drop of hat.
One way to do this is to use pen portraits. These are simple devices to summarise who
you are wanting to engage. They aren’t a real person, just a typical one.

Ask yourself what are these people looking for. Be honest. Don’t try to make it fit what
you want to offer. You should be thinking of their functional needs, their motivations
(looking good in their bosses’ eyes, for example), psychological needs. you could come up
with dozens of reasons here. Simplify the list to five or even less.
When ask yourself what are you asking of them. To buy stuff. To recommend you. To
understand why you’re different. To pay a bit more?
Then understand how they are best engaged. Do they like to get marketing emails? Do
they want to follow you on Twitter? Is your website the most important place to engage
with you? Do they like face-to-face conversations? How about a bit of entertainment? Or
do they like telephone calls?
Finally, work out what it is you should avoid doing. Over promising? Bombarding them
with telesales? Not being completely honest about the true cost, not forgetting their time
and involvement?
