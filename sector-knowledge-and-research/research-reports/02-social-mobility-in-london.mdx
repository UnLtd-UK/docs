## Overview

Published in February 2020 by the Mayor's Fund for London in partnership with Oliver Wyman, "One City, Two Worlds" examines a central puzzle: despite London's strong academic attainment and dynamic job market, young people from the capital's most deprived neighbourhoods do relatively worse in employment than their peers in the rest of England.

The report reveals that only **17% of London's professional jobs** are occupied by people from lower income backgrounds, compared to **30% nationally**. Despite educational success, London's labour market remains one of the hardest in the country for disadvantaged young people to crack.

## The Broken Path to Social Mobility

### Poverty and Inequality in London

London hosts two worlds in one city:

- An estimated **700,000 children** live in relative poverty
- **Three-quarters of young Londoners** live in areas experiencing the worst 40% of national crime rates
- **11 of the top 25** UK local authorities by child poverty rate are in London
- One in six young Londoners is eligible for free school meals

### Educational Success, Yet Low Prosperity

London's educational transformation is remarkable. Prior to 2000, the capital's state schools consistently produced some of England's worst GCSE and A-level results. Today, London hosts some of Britain's leading state schools.

The GCSE attainment gap between low-income students and their peers in London is **15%**, significantly smaller than **28%** across the rest of England. However, this academic success does not translate into employment outcomes.

When comparing how young people in London's boroughs perform at different life stages:

- **At secondary school age** — most London boroughs rank in the top 50 nationally for outcomes
- **In adulthood** — outcomes in London are much worse than the average, with most boroughs placed towards the lower end of the national list

### A Job Market That Favours the Privileged

London's job market has structural characteristics that work against social mobility:

- Professional occupations in London employ **54% of workers from professional backgrounds** — the highest proportion of any English region
- High rents and business costs create jobs most readily at the top or bottom of the career ladder, with fewer opportunities in the middle
- The capital's economy is heavily concentrated in knowledge-based industries (law, management consulting, finance, medicine) which employ the highest proportion of workers from high-income backgrounds nationally
- Even when people from lower income backgrounds enter professional employment, they earn on average **£10,660 less per year** than colleagues whose parents were in professional occupations

## The Four Drivers of Social Mobility

The report uses a "4 As" framework to analyse what drives social mobility outcomes for young Londoners:

### Ability

Ability includes social and emotional skills as well as academic and practical ones. An academic gap opens up in the early years between those from low-income backgrounds and others, which is difficult to close over time. By age 16, young Londoners from low-income backgrounds are, on average, **19 months behind** their better-off peers.

Increasingly, employers cite soft skills — team working, oral communication, customer handling, and time management — as the most critical skill gaps. In 2017, 51% of all skill-shortage vacancies were attributed to lack of time management and task prioritisation abilities.

### Aspiration

While young people from all income backgrounds have similar aspirations in their early years, differences in circumstances and structural inequalities affect ambition levels over time. Two aspects stand out:

- **Social networks shape ambition** — from as young as age 7, 36% of children base their career aspirations on people they know, while fewer than 1% had heard about jobs from people coming to speak at their school
- **Lack of representation lowers aspirations** — more than a quarter of young people from low-income backgrounds believe that "people like me" do not succeed in life

### Awareness

A young person's awareness of opportunities is highly dependent on background. Those from more affluent families have strong cultural capital — an innate understanding of, and access to, employer networks through family and friends.

Research shows that building "proxy cultural capital" through career talks, insight days, and work experience brings measurable benefits. Pupils from lower-income backgrounds who attend school career talks receive wages **8.5% higher** than their peers.

### Access

Access is about levelling the playing field in recruitment. Although recruitment processes often appear meritocratic, young people from particular backgrounds are still more likely to succeed:

- Recruitment is often driven by network relationships and traditional criteria that favour cultural familiarity over ability
- City firms sometimes misrecognise "cultural displays" (appearing polished, strong conversational skills) as accurate proxies for ability
- Low-income students are **underrepresented in sixth forms** but overrepresented in further education colleges and low-skilled employment
- Even apprenticeship schemes underrepresent young people from low-income households

## The London Doughnut Problem

The report identifies a geographic mismatch in the provision of support services. The impacts of rising property prices and welfare reform have been moving the profile of socio-economic need towards the outer boroughs, while many corporates, funders, and charities have traditionally focused on central London boroughs.

This creates a "doughnut" pattern where many outer boroughs are under-served by the very organisations trying to address social mobility. Shifting the dial on social mobility in London requires spreading opportunity far broader than has been done before.

## Recommendations

The report calls for three main areas of action:

### Stronger Cross-Sectoral Leadership

Decision-makers across sectors need to come together to drive development of positive interventions and partnerships across London. There is currently no long-term strategy or agreed approach to addressing social mobility in the capital.

### Workforce Strategy and Employer Action

- Place socio-economic background at the core of workforce diversity strategies
- Make a senior leadership commitment to broadening recruitment
- Expand recruitment beyond traditional talent pools
- Review hiring practices that may inadvertently exclude talented young Londoners from disadvantaged backgrounds
- Use the apprenticeship levy strategically to support social mobility

### Evidence-Based Interventions

- Increase the availability of mentorship schemes and role models
- Ensure interventions are provided consistently across London, not in an ad-hoc manner
- Accelerate success through data sharing and better network coordination
- Focus on providing support from an early age onwards, with a long-term view of what young Londoners need to achieve their potential

## Key Takeaway

London's social mobility challenge is not primarily about education — the capital has largely solved that problem. The challenge lies in the transition from education to employment, where structural barriers, cultural capital gaps, and a polarised job market combine to lock out young people from disadvantaged backgrounds. Real change requires action from employers, educators, charities, and statutory organisations working together with a coordinated, long-term strategy.
