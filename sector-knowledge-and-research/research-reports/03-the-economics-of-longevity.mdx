## Overview

Published in July 2017, The Economist's special report "The Economics of Longevity" argues that longer, healthier lives can be a boon for individuals, economies, and societies — but only if institutions, employers, and financial systems adapt to keep up. The central thesis is that the key to unlocking a "longevity dividend" is turning the over-65s into more active economic participants.

The scale of demographic change is significant. In the OECD, the share of over-65s is set to increase from 16% in 2015 to 25% by 2050. Without adaptation, the warnings about labour shortages, economic stagnation, and fiscal strain may prove justified. But the report argues that a radically different approach to ageing can turn this challenge into an opportunity.

## A New Stage of Life

Today's 65-year-olds are in much better shape than their grandparents were at the same age. In most EU countries, healthy life expectancy from age 50 is growing faster than life expectancy itself, suggesting that the period of diminished health at the end of life is being compressed.

Yet in most countries, the age at which people retire has barely shifted over the past century. When Bismarck introduced the first formal pensions in the 1880s (payable from age 70, later reduced to 65), life expectancy in Prussia was 45. Today, 90% of the rich world's population lives to celebrate their 65th birthday, mostly in good health.

A new stage of life is emerging between the end of conventional working age and the onset of old age as traditionally understood. These "young old" are in relatively good health, often still work, spend money on non-age-specific things, and want financial security with more flexibility than traditional retirement products offer.

## Working Longer

If people in "older" countries such as Germany, Japan, and Spain were to delay retirement by 2 to 2.5 years per decade between 2010 and 2050, it would be enough to offset the effect of demographic change.

### The Gig Economy and Older Workers

The gig economy suits older people in many ways — they are often content to work part-time, are not looking for career progression, and can better handle the precariousness of such jobs. A quarter of drivers for ride-hailing services are over 50, and a quarter of all Americans working in the "sharing economy" are over 55.

### Entrepreneurship

Older people are increasingly becoming entrepreneurs. In America, those between 55 and 65 are now **65% more likely** to start companies than those between 20 and 34. In Britain, 40% of new founders are over 50.

### Busting Myths About Older Workers

Several myths about older workers need challenging:

- **Productivity** — in knowledge-based jobs, age makes no difference to performance. In jobs requiring social skills (such as financial advisers), productivity actually increases with age
- **Innovation** — the idea that only the young can innovate has been successfully challenged
- **Team contribution** — older workers' contributions often show up in increased group performance rather than individual metrics. Multi-generational teams perform better than single-generation ones

As one Deutsche Bank executive explains: "In operational work older employees can be slower, but they make up for that with fewer mistakes, so in total they are no less productive."

### Unpaid Contributions

Older people make enormous unpaid economic contributions. In Italy and Portugal, around one grandmother in five provides daily care for a grandchild. In Britain, unpaid older caregivers save the state around £11.4 billion per year. In America, the over-55s formally volunteered 3.3 billion hours in 2016, contributing an estimated $78 billion in economic value.

## The Consumer Opportunity

Older consumers represent a massive and underserved market:

- In America, the over-50s will shortly account for **70% of disposable income**
- Global spending by households headed by over-60s could amount to **$15 trillion by 2020**
- Between now and 2030, most consumption growth in developed-world cities will come from the over-60s

Yet the market has failed to respond to this opportunity. Key growth areas include:

- **Adventure travel** — more than 40% of American adventure travellers are over 50, and the 65–74 age group is the fastest-growing segment in British travel
- **Dating and companionship** — Americans over 60 are divorcing at twice the 1990 rate, creating demand for dating services. More than a quarter of Match.com members are between 53 and 72
- **Housing alternatives** — the younger old are seeking more convivial living arrangements beyond traditional granny flats
- **Technology and digital services** — older consumers are enthusiastic adopters when products are well designed

Businesses targeting this market should note that older consumers do not see themselves as old and will respond badly to products specifically marketed to "seniors."

## Rethinking Finance

The financial industry needs a fundamental overhaul to serve longer lives:

### The Savings Challenge

- Roughly **40% of Americans** approach retirement with no savings in retirement accounts
- In Britain, 20% of women and 12% of men between 55 and 65 have no retirement savings
- People consistently underestimate how long they will live and overestimate how long their money will last

### The Oversaving Problem

Paradoxically, many pensioners spend less than they can afford. In most rich countries, the elderly are net savers. This "accidental" oversaving — driven by uncertainty about lifespan, health costs, and market conditions — means that dormant wealth is neither invested nor spent, preventing many older people from realising their full economic potential.

### Paying for Care

End-of-life care represents one of the greatest financial risks of ageing:

- A 50-year-old American has a better-than-even chance of ending up in a nursing home
- In Britain, 10% of older people face care costs exceeding £100,000
- Globally, around 47 million people have dementia, with projections suggesting 132 million by 2050 without a medical breakthrough

Care insurance markets have largely failed due to uncertainty about public provision, adverse selection, and unpredictable costs. Solutions may include compulsory insurance (as in Germany), public-private hybrid schemes, or better use of housing equity through reverse mortgages and home-sharing platforms like Airbnb (where the over-60s are the fastest-growing group of hosts).

### Housing as an Asset

For many people, their home is their biggest asset, yet it plays little part in funding longer lives. Reverse mortgages let homeowners exchange some equity for income in retirement, but the market remains small due to consumer nervousness, high costs, and limited competition.

## Technology for Longer Lives

Technology holds great promise for improving the final stage of life, enabling people to retain independence and live at home longer:

### Smart Homes

Sensor systems can transform ordinary homes into supportive environments. Wireless sensors monitoring daily activities can detect changes that predict health problems — increased toilet visits may flag urinary infections, changes in gait can predict falls. These data can alert family members or caregivers to intervene early, preventing expensive hospital admissions.

### On-Demand Services

Ride-hailing, grocery deliveries, handymen, and on-demand care services could all be a boon to older people. Tech-enabled care companies allow carers to be booked round the clock, via an app, on a pay-as-you-go basis — a significant improvement over traditional care companies that insist on advance booking in blocks of hours.

### Technology Built for the Young, Useful for the Old

Perhaps surprisingly, the greatest potential lies in technology built for younger consumers:

- **Smart home devices** — voice-controlled assistants can answer questions, make phone calls, and control smart devices
- **Social media** — older people who might otherwise become isolated stand to benefit most from online connections
- **Smartphones and tablets** — the most popular classes in senior centres worldwide are smartphone and tablet training

The challenge is funding. Unless clear payment mechanisms emerge, these technologies will be available only to those who can pay outright, increasing inequality.

## The Longevity Dividend

The report concludes that making longer lives financially viable, productive, and enjoyable requires a fundamental rethink of life trajectories. The old three-stage life cycle — education, work, retirement — is deeply ingrained but increasingly obsolete.

Two things need to happen:

1. **Employers must adapt** — ageist recruitment practices and inflexible corporate cultures are major barriers. Companies that embrace multi-generational teams report better performance and fewer mistakes.
2. **Benefits must be shared equitably** — greater longevity currently favours the well-off and well-educated. Across the OECD, a highly educated 25-year-old man can expect to live eight years longer than a contemporary with only basic education. Investing in public health, universal healthcare, and lifelong learning is essential to narrow this gap.

Given the right input from governments, employers, and individuals, it should be possible to stretch the productive in-betweener stage and compress the dependent period at the very end of life. The pessimism about ageing populations is based on the assumption that people instantly become net recipients of benefits at 65. If many more remain economically active, the transition becomes far more gradual and nuanced — and the longevity dividend becomes real.
