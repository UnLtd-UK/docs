## Types of Opportunities

There are several routes into public sector work for social enterprises. Understanding these different mechanisms helps you identify the best fit for your organisation's size, specialism, and stage of development.

### Alliance Working

Alliance working involves collaborating with other providers to deliver a whole contract together. Unlike sub-contracting, no single provider is the lead or accountable provider — accountability for the contract is shared across all partners. This model works well for social enterprises that want to be equal partners rather than subordinate to a larger organisation.

### Sub-Contracting

Sub-contracting means delivering a specified piece or amount of work for a lead or primary provider. This is a good way for smaller or newer social enterprises to build their track record and gain experience of public sector service delivery without the burden of managing an entire contract.

### Consortium Working

A consortium is where several providers come together under a legal umbrella to deliver a contract together. This requires setting up a new legal entity, so you should weigh up the time and cost of establishing the legal arrangements against the likely return in terms of business.

### Grants

There are a small (and reducing) number of grants available at a smaller scale to facilitate new areas of work, such as community resilience. Not all grants are held by social care departments — sometimes they come through communities teams. Time-limited grant funding also becomes available through EU funding streams and other sources, with the local authority often holding a coordinating role.

### Spot Contracts

Individual clients sometimes need a package of care that sits outside normal contracts. Local authorities have the ability to commission on an individual basis, creating a bespoke package of care. However, most local authorities try to limit spot purchasing as it can be costly and raise concerns about competitive disadvantage.

### Call-Off Frameworks

Sometimes called approved provider lists, these are pre-arranged lists of providers delivering a particular type of service. To get onto a framework, you typically need to demonstrate how you meet a range of quality criteria by providing information such as insurance certificates, accounts, and details of policies.

Once on the list, you may be commissioned as and when the need arises for a client. Social work staff making placements for people usually use these lists. There is no guarantee of business, but being on the list positions you for opportunities.

### Direct Payments

People receiving social care can choose to have the money to arrange their own services, which is called a direct payment. This can be paid into a bank account or loaded onto a pre-paid card. If commissioners and social work teams are aware of your service, they may signpost enquirers to you.

### Self-Funders

Local authorities must provide information about services to all people in their area, regardless of whether they meet eligibility criteria for funded support. Most local authorities have online directories of services where providers can list their services. Building visibility with self-funders can be a significant revenue stream.

## Collaboration as a Strategy

Collaboration is particularly valuable for social enterprises that are new to the market or have a niche specialism:

- **Building track record** — working with more established or larger providers helps you build your portfolio of work over time and offers reassurance to commissioners
- **Covering geography** — several organisations working together can offer full coverage across a wide area, with each working to its own geographical patch
- **Combining specialisms** — partners can each deliver their own area of expertise within a broader contract

When collaborating, be sure to develop clear working relationships, protocols, and agreements to underpin these arrangements.

## Practical Tips for Getting Started

### 1. Register with Procurement Portals

Sign up to your local procurement portal for larger contract opportunities. You can set the system to send updates about opportunities in certain locations and areas of business. Many public sector organisations use Pro Contract — ask your local commissioners which system they use and how to become a registered supplier.

### 2. Collaborate Before You Compete

If you want to apply for something but cannot meet the full remit on your own, or if you are new to the market, work with established or complementary providers. Just be sure to develop clear agreements underpinning any arrangements.

### 3. Balance Quality and Price

Do not compromise quality for price, but be competitive. Commissioners need to know that services will be delivered to the required standard throughout the contract period.

### 4. Register with Local Directories

Sign up to free local authority databases and service directories to promote your services to self-funders and direct payment clients.

### 5. Build Relationships with Commissioners

Let commissioners and social work teams know about what you are offering. Share any good ideas for new services — a pot of grant funding may suddenly become available that fits what you do.

### 6. Engage Local Councillors

Local councillors each have a small budget to support local causes, often up to £10,000 per year. Local arrangements and variation will apply.

### 7. Weigh Effort Against Return

Assess the amount of work (time and cost, including any new legal arrangements like a consortium) required to apply for a contract against the likely return in terms of business if you are successful.

### 8. Attend Provider Forums

Use the mechanisms and forums that public sector commissioners operate to engage with service providers in their area. This keeps you abreast of developments, trends, and upcoming opportunities.

### 9. Attend Market Engagement Events

Go along to market testing and market engagement events for opportunities that interest you. These events help you understand how a tender will work and allow you to ask questions before applications are invited.

## Using Public Sector Data

Local authorities hold a wealth of useful information to help social entrepreneurs plan and demonstrate need for their enterprises. Key resources include:

- **Joint Strategic Needs Assessment (JSNA)** — provides a wide range of information about local population needs, filterable by location and area of need
- **Market Position Statements** — set out analysis of supply and demand for services in the area
- **Commissioning intentions documents** — outline where authorities plan to commission new or additional services

This data is invaluable for funding applications and business planning, helping you demonstrate that there is a genuine need for the service you want to provide.
