## Why Consider Spinning Out?

Public sector spin-outs — where a team or service leaves a local authority or NHS body to form an independent social enterprise or mutual — offer a route to combine public service values with commercial agility. Leaders who have made this journey consistently report several benefits:

- **Faster decision-making** — freed from large organisational bureaucracy, spin-outs can respond to opportunities and make changes more quickly
- **Staff motivation** — employee ownership and a clearer mission tend to reduce sickness levels and increase productivity
- **Service innovation** — independence enables new approaches to service delivery that would be difficult within a large public body
- **Growth potential** — as an independent organisation, you can win contracts from other bodies and expand your reach

As Kevin Bond of NAViGO (a mental health social enterprise spun out from the NHS) put it: "Thanks to quicker decision-making processes, we have already decided to set up a new regional eating disorder service. Reductions in sickness are steadily happening. There's more involvement of our local community, staff, partners and service users."

## Is Spinning Out Right for You?

Before committing to a spin-out, you need to honestly assess whether the conditions are right:

### The Right Motivation

The strongest spin-outs are led by people who are deeply motivated by a desire to improve services, not simply by frustration with bureaucracy. You need a clear vision of how independence will allow you to serve your community better.

### Personal Readiness

Leading a spin-out is demanding. You will effectively have a second job for a significant period while the new venture is being developed alongside existing service delivery. You need energy, resilience, and enough space in your life to take this on.

### Organisational Readiness

Consider whether your service has a viable market, a strong team, and a track record of good performance. Commissioners need to have confidence that the service will continue to be delivered effectively during and after the transition.

## Choosing the Right Structure

Selecting the right corporate structure is one of the most important decisions you will make. The main options include:

| Structure | Key Features |
|---|---|
| **Community Interest Company (CIC)** | A limited company with a social purpose. Has an asset lock preventing profits being distributed to owners. Lighter regulation than a charity. |
| **Mutual / Employee-Owned** | Staff have a direct ownership stake and voice in how the organisation is run. Can be structured as a co-operative, community benefit society, or employee-owned company. |
| **Charity** | Eligible for tax relief and may attract charitable funding, but comes with greater regulatory requirements and restrictions on trading. |
| **Wholly-Owned Company** | Remains owned by the parent council or NHS body. Offers some operational independence while the public body retains control. |

Many successful spin-outs have chosen the CIC or community benefit society structure because these balance commercial flexibility with a clear social mission. The right choice depends on your specific circumstances, the nature of your service, and the expectations of your commissioners.

## Building Your Team and Board

### Leadership

Every successful spin-out has been forged by the commitment of a few key individuals working long hours and often against the odds. You do not need to do this alone — finding mentors, advisers, and a supportive peer group is essential.

### The Board

You will need a board with the right mix of skills. Consider people with experience in:

- Finance and business management
- The sector in which you operate
- Legal and governance matters
- Commercial strategy and growth

Many spin-outs also include service user representation on their board, which strengthens accountability and keeps the organisation grounded in its mission.

### Staff Engagement

Your staff are your most important asset. Engage them early and honestly about the process. Under TUPE regulations, their employment rights are protected when they transfer to the new organisation, but people naturally feel anxious about change. Clear communication, involvement in decision-making, and a compelling vision for the future all help.

## Working with Commissioners

Commissioners play a crucial role in enabling spin-outs. A supportive commissioner can help smooth the path, while a reluctant one can make things much harder.

### The Commissioner's Perspective

There are genuine benefits for commissioners in supporting spin-outs:

- **Arm's-length relationship** — an external provider allows a more honest, commercially grounded relationship than in-house commissioning
- **Local investment** — a spun-out social enterprise is rooted in the local community and invested in its reputation there
- **Beyond-contract benefits** — social enterprises often deliver additional local benefits around jobs, regeneration, and community engagement
- **Equitable partnership** — you are dealing with an organisation of comparable resources, not a corporate titan

### Building the Relationship

Have an early, honest conversation with commissioners about your intentions. Understand their commissioning plans and ensure your proposal aligns with their strategic priorities. Be prepared to demonstrate how spinning out will deliver better outcomes and better value for the public body.

## Getting the Finances Right

### Building Your First Budget

You will need to develop a detailed business plan and budget. Key areas to address include:

- **Revenue projections** based on your current and potential contracts
- **Cost base** — understand how your costs will change outside the public sector, including back-office functions you may need to procure independently (IT, HR, payroll, premises)
- **Transition costs** — legal, advisory, and setup costs during the spin-out period
- **Working capital** — cash flow in the early months before regular contract payments are established

### Sources of Funding

Funding for spin-outs has evolved over time. Options include:

- **Government grants and loans** — various schemes have supported public sector spin-outs, though availability changes over time
- **Social investment** — organisations like Big Society Capital and social investors provide finance for social enterprises
- **Partnership models** — some spin-outs partner with private or third-sector organisations who bring investment and expertise in exchange for a stake and a long-term contract
- **Trading income** — as you grow, reinvested surpluses become your primary source of investment capital

## Life After Spinning Out

### The First 100 Days

The early period after spinning out is critical. Use it to:

- **Establish your culture** — you are no longer a department within a larger body, but a new type of organisation with its own identity and values
- **Build operational independence** — ensure your back-office functions, IT systems, and premises arrangements are working effectively
- **Demonstrate quick wins** — early improvements in service quality, staff satisfaction, or efficiency help build confidence among staff, commissioners, and service users

### Surviving and Thriving

Spin-outs that succeed in the long term share several characteristics:

- **Strong leadership** with a clear vision and the ability to navigate both commercial and mission-driven priorities
- **Financial discipline** — understanding your costs, managing cash flow, and building reserves
- **Continuous improvement** — using your newfound freedom to innovate, adapt, and respond to changing needs
- **Growth mindset** — looking for opportunities to expand your services, win new contracts, and diversify your income

### Growing Your Business

Growth may come from:

- Winning contracts from other local authorities or NHS bodies
- Developing new services that complement your core offer
- Partnering with other organisations to deliver larger contracts
- Exploring social finance options to fund expansion

## Key Takeaways

The journey from public sector department to independent social enterprise is challenging but rewarding. The evidence from organisations that have made this transition is clear: none would go back. Staff are more engaged, services are more innovative, and the organisations are better placed to respond to the needs of the communities they serve.

The key ingredients are strong leadership, a clear social mission, a solid business plan, supportive commissioners, and the determination to see it through.
