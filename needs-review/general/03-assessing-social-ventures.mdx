## Why Assessment Matters

Whether you are applying for funding, preparing for an investment panel, or simply evaluating your own progress, understanding how social ventures are assessed helps you build a stronger enterprise. Assessment frameworks provide a structured way to examine the key dimensions of your venture and identify where you need to strengthen your offer.

## The Five Assessment Areas

Social ventures are typically evaluated across five core areas. Each area contributes to an overall picture of your venture's readiness and potential.

### The Proposition

Your proposition is the foundation of your venture. Assessors will look at:

- **Value proposition and competitive advantage** — can you clearly and succinctly explain what makes your offer compelling to customers and investors?
- **Theory of change** — do you have clear metrics for social impact that are appropriate to your customers and stakeholders?
- **Organisational structure** — is your legal and organisational structure appropriate for your proposition?

A strong proposition demonstrates a timely opportunity given current market conditions, with a clear link between commercial activity and social outcomes.

### The People

Your team and governance are critical signals of your venture's potential:

- **Team and board** — do they inspire confidence? Do they have the right industry expertise and networks?
- **Key partnerships** — do you have partnerships in place that provide additional credibility or routes to market?

Assessors are looking for evidence that the right people are in place to execute the plan.

### The Performance

Track record and momentum matter:

- **Business momentum** — do your financials evidence growing sales and a strong pipeline?
- **Impact growth** — is your social impact growing in line with your sales?
- **Track record** — if you are early stage, do the individuals involved have a relevant history of performance?

Strong, well-organised financials are essential. Unclear or poorly presented financial information can significantly undermine an otherwise promising application.

### The Plan

Your growth plan should demonstrate ambition tempered by realism:

- **Scale ambition** — does your documentation show a clear ambition to achieve regional or national scale?
- **Scaling logic** — do you demonstrate a clear, logical understanding of what is required to scale?

Overly ambitious plans without a grounded understanding of the steps required can be a red flag.

### The Growth Capital

Finally, assessors will look at your financial needs:

- **Investment need** — do your financials show a clear need for investment, and is the amount appropriate for your stage?
- **Use of funds** — is it clear how you would deploy the capital and what it would enable?

## Understanding Social Impact Assessment

One of the most nuanced aspects of venture assessment is evaluating social impact. There is an important distinction between impact that is inherent to the product or service and additional impact that extends beyond the core offering.

### When the Product Is the Impact

If your product directly addresses a significant need — for example, an assistive device for people with disabilities — then the social impact is embedded in the proposition itself. A product with strong product-market fit among a high-need group creates impact through its very existence and adoption.

In these cases, you should be careful not to overcomplicate your impact narrative. Focus on demonstrating:

- That the product genuinely addresses the need it claims to
- That there is a real and measurable market for it
- That users experience tangible benefits

### Building a Compelling Impact Case

Regardless of your model, you need to articulate your impact clearly. This means:

1. **Conduct thorough market analysis** — understand the size and nature of your target market, and be prepared to discuss it in detail
2. **Define your impact metrics** — what specific outcomes will you track, and how will you measure them?
3. **Gather evidence** — even at an early stage, look for evidence that supports your impact thesis, such as prior interest from partners, pilot results, or user testimonials
4. **Test your assumptions** — if you are uncertain whether your product or service will have the impact you anticipate, frame your work as testing and evidencing that hypothesis

### Common Pitfalls

- **Underestimating the importance of clear financials** — messy or incomplete financial information raises serious concerns, regardless of how strong your social mission is
- **Overestimating market size** — be realistic about your addressable market and back up your estimates with evidence
- **Failing to articulate impact** — even if your impact is obvious to you, assessors need to see that you can explain it clearly and measure it systematically
- **Presenting unrealistic growth plans** — ambitious is good, but plans need to be grounded in a logical understanding of what scaling requires
