## What Is Place-Based Funding?

Place-based funding means targeted investment in defined geographic areas. Rather than funding individual organisations or projects in isolation, it involves a package of support — which may include multiple grants, particularly large investments, grants combined with additional activity such as capacity building, networking, and influencing work — all within a defined place.

This approach has grown in importance as funders recognise that the issues they wish to address, particularly in the most disadvantaged localities, are multi-faceted. Place-based funding offers one way of delivering a more holistic approach.

## Why Funders Choose Place-Based Approaches

Several drivers push funders towards place-based working:

- **Complex local challenges** — recognition that issues like poverty, exclusion, and health inequality require coordinated responses rather than isolated interventions
- **Reductions in statutory funding** — as public funding decreases, remaining resources need to be spent more effectively
- **Devolution and localism** — a policy shift towards local decision-making creates opportunities for locally-tailored funding strategies
- **Austerity responses** — funders are working to both respond to loss of local funding and to spend remaining money more effectively
- **Aligning funding to local need** — making funding more representative locally and more closely tied to what communities actually need

## Key Elements of Effective Collaborative Funding

Research across multiple case studies has identified eight key elements that make collaborative place-based funding work.

### 1. Independent Funding as a Source of Innovation

One of the most valuable contributions independent funders can make is providing space and flexibility to try new approaches. Unlike statutory funders, independent foundations may be willing to provide funding without requiring assurance of success or specific predetermined outcomes.

This flexibility allows partners to:

- Trial new and different strategic approaches
- Build foundations and test receptiveness for new ways of working
- Think creatively during development stages
- Draw on external perspectives and learning from other disciplines

However, this still involves considerable risk. Partners need to demonstrate success to maintain trust and engagement, even when working within a more flexible funding framework.

### 2. Finding Collaborative Champions

Effective collaborative funding depends on key individuals who can drive the work. These people share common characteristics:

- They can look beyond their own organisational boundaries
- They understand and know the place — its context and the needs of service users
- They are well-networked
- They can focus on and drive towards the vision
- They are decision-makers with the authority to commit their organisations
- They are passionate, committed, and open to doing things differently

If these individuals leave, it can put the entire partnership at risk. Structures need to ensure wider institutional commitment to maintain sustainability.

### 3. Developing Processes Through Co-Design

Involving the voluntary and community sector in designing funding processes helps ensure they are appropriate and accessible, even for the smallest organisations. This means:

- Making the demands of application processes proportionate to the level of funding available
- Ensuring monitoring and evaluation requirements are proportionate
- Allowing organisations to take risks without excessive burden
- Using trusted infrastructure bodies to run consultations and provide support

### 4. Establishing Clarity About Roles and Responsibilities

Partnership working requires clear understanding of who is leading, who has sign-off authority, and how differences of opinion are voiced and resolved. Where this clarity is lacking, it leads to uncertainty and can undermine the collaboration.

Consider:

- Appointing independent facilitation or support
- Being explicit about governance structures and decision-making authority
- Recognising that even collaborative working requires some leadership driving the work
- Balancing the desire to operate as a collaboration with the need for clear accountability

### 5. Listening to Service User Voices

Place-based approaches provide an opportunity to understand how service users actually experience living in a locality and trying to access services. Effective partnerships go beyond strategic approaches to leverage funding — they create space to listen to service users, do things differently, and provide services that respond to real needs.

The voice of service users should come directly from them, not filtered through professionals.

### 6. Investing Time and Resources in Relationships

Joint working and building the trust to take risks requires significant time and resources. Collaborative working may save money in the long term, but it requires upfront investment to build partnerships and establish new ways of working.

Core funding from independent funders can enable organisations to invest in building networks and relationships in ways that might not otherwise be possible. The depth and quality of relationships that develop over time underpin the nuanced judgements and decision-making that collaborative funding often requires.

### 7. Developing Shared Understanding About Impact

Different funders often have different expectations about the impact they are seeking. Independent funders may be comfortable with learning-oriented approaches, while statutory funders may need to demonstrate clear "value for money." This tension needs to be managed proactively by:

- Working with all stakeholders to collectively agree what success looks like at the outset
- Co-designing impact measures and evaluation plans
- Accepting that different funders may legitimately need different types of evidence
- Recognising that system outcomes (shifts in behaviour and ways of working) are harder to measure than service delivery outcomes but equally important

### 8. Building Sustainability and Exit Into the Process

From the outset, you should think about what happens when initial funding ends. Effective approaches include:

- Linking into local organisations and making institutional connections that can sustain the work
- Building and funding infrastructure for the sector
- Asking applicants about their plans beyond the initial funding period
- Working in partnership with regional bodies to ensure funded work aligns with local strategy
- Focusing not just on sustaining services, but on sustaining learning
- Embedding approaches into the practice of local statutory agencies

## The Place-Based Funding Framework

When developing a place-based approach, work through these key questions:

### Defining Your Approach

1. **What does "place" mean for you?** — Consider the appropriate geographic scale, from street level to regional
2. **Why are you using a place-based approach?** — Common drivers include targeting a particular issue, addressing cold spots, responding to policy changes, testing a model, targeting areas of high deprivation, or having a specific geographic remit
3. **What contribution are you seeking to make?** — This ranges from responsive funding of good projects through building community assets to strategic systems change

### Managing Risk and Impact

4. **What is your attitude towards risk and uncertainty?** — Place-based working takes time and outcomes may emerge slowly; consider your tolerance for this from multiple perspectives
5. **What is your position on impact?** — If you are embarking on exploratory or community-led work, there may not be a predetermined end point to measure against

### Building Knowledge and Relationships

6. **What is your existing knowledge of the area?** — Consider what you know, how you know it, and what you might need to find out
7. **What duration of involvement is required?** — Longer-term funding gives confidence to communities and allows adaptation to new ways of working
8. **Where will control sit?** — Will the work be community-led or funder-driven?

### Defining Roles and Commitment

9. **What will your role be?** — This can range from arm's length to fully embedded
10. **Who will you need to work with?** — From grantees only through to multiple stakeholders
11. **What kind of relationships are required?** — Contractual, engaged, or relational/collaborative
12. **What commitment of staff and trustee time is needed?** — Place-based working can be resource intensive
