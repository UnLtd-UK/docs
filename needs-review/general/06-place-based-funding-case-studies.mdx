## Learning from Practice

The following case studies illustrate how independent and statutory funders have worked together in specific geographic areas. Each example attempted to join up services, funding, or both in a locality, with a targeted investment in a defined geographic area. They differ in the way money is used, who leads the work, and the degree to which control is shared.

## Bristol Impact Fund

**What it was:** A council initiative consolidating previously separate grants streams, co-designed with the local voluntary sector. Independent funders were drawn into the allocations process to increase alignment of funding strategies.

**Scale of funding:** £3.29 million

### Key Lessons

**Individual leadership matters.** The success of the Bristol Impact Fund depended heavily on lead officers who were willing to take risks and who had buy-in from senior leadership including the CEO and Mayors. These officers were respected across both the local authority and the voluntary and community sector.

**Local knowledge is essential.** National funding initiatives need to be tied into locally sustainable organisations. Getting local people involved reduces the need for costly marketing and community engagement, and ensures interventions connect to something with a long-term presence.

**Time is required.** Building effective partnerships cannot be rushed. You need to invest in developing relationships, and this takes time.

**Innovation with appropriate risk management.** Involving independent funders in local authority grant-making was innovative and increased the chances of alignment, but it required funders to accept a degree of risk. National funders also need to recognise local funders' autonomy.

**Support smaller organisations.** Funding voluntary sector infrastructure to provide independent support was essential, especially for smaller organisations. Systems should allow new and small organisations to apply without facing disproportionately high thresholds.

**Use independent facilitation.** Council officers found value in independent facilitation at all stages, from initial workshops to ongoing support throughout the co-design process.

## Inclusion Plus — Dundee

**What it was:** A partnership supporting young people at risk of exclusion from school, funded by The Robertson Trust, Big Lottery Fund, and Dundee City Council. Initiated by an independent funder wanting to test a new funding model.

**Scale of funding:** Approximately £860,000

### Key Lessons

**Local context cannot be ignored.** You cannot simply drop a model from one area into another. To be successful, an initiative needs to reflect local need and context, including the history and existing relationships in an area.

**Place influences your partners.** The decision to use a place-based approach determines who you need to work with. In Dundee, working city-wide on school exclusion meant the required partners were linked to education and schools specifically.

**Place is not always the best targeting method.** Although schools were selected based on high exclusion levels and deprivation, pupils did not necessarily live in the immediate vicinity of those schools.

**Experiential learning builds trust.** Even when initial financial contributions are small, participation allows partners to trial new approaches and develop working relationships. Once trust is established, partners can contribute more substantially.

**Different funders need different evidence.** The Robertson Trust could be relaxed about outcomes, while Dundee City Council needed to demonstrate value for money. Addressing this tension requires developing a shared understanding of what success looks like from the outset.

**Ownership must be clear.** At times, it was unclear who owned the partnership or the programme. Funders expected voluntary organisations to lead, but may not have been aware of how much they themselves influenced delivery.

**Challenge your assumptions.** Even when working with long-standing partners, do not assume they will always tell you what is needed or speak up about challenges.

## Partnership Drugs Initiative — Scotland

**What it was:** An initiative funding support to children and young people affected by substance issues, aligned to local need and context. Set up by Lloyds TSB Foundation for Scotland (now Corra Foundation), funded in partnership with the Scottish Government and The Robertson Trust. Operating for over 15 years.

**Scale of funding:** Approximately £1.7 million per year

### Key Lessons

**National programmes must work locally.** To fund effectively at a local level, you need individuals with the skills and experience to understand the relevance of place for any given project. This includes recruiting people with a background and perceived legitimacy in the area.

**Managing and sharing power is essential.** The lead organisation established a culture of learning and partnership-working reflected in governance structures. A range of voices — from young people to grantees to subject matter experts — are engaged in strategy setting and decision-making. Careful chairing by someone skilled and respected helps ensure no single voice dominates.

**Sharing learning develops policy and practice.** Acting as a conduit for local-level learning to influence national thinking and policy amplifies the impact of place-based work.

**Plan for sustainability from the start.** The initiative places emphasis on helping funded projects think beyond the funding term. Rather than simply asking how projects will sustain themselves, the focus has shifted to sustaining learning and embedding approaches into the practice of local statutory agencies.

## York Pathways

**What it was:** A strategic-level partnership aimed at improving the response to individuals experiencing mental distress and joining up service delivery. Initiated by North Yorkshire Police and Together (a national mental health charity), funded by Lankelly Chase Foundation, North Yorkshire Police, and York Council.

**Scale of funding:** Approximately £340,000

### Key Lessons

**Place can drive systems change.** Using a geographic focus encourages a holistic approach to supporting individuals and improves cross-sector working. However, geographic boundaries can also be restrictive, and different agencies have their own reporting structures and requirements.

**Personal commitment drives partnership success.** The individuals involved shared a personal passion for the work that helped them persevere through challenges. This determination is key to establishing the willingness to give time and energy to something that does not fit neatly with a day job.

**Put service users at the centre.** Partnering with service users to design and produce the initiative gives legitimacy and allows the partnership to identify genuine gaps in the system based on lived experience.

**Change takes time.** When dealing with complex issues and attempting to change both behaviour and practice, you need to set clear expectations with partners from the outset about the progress they can expect and over what timescale.

**Independent funders add unique value.** The addition of an independent funder provided space and flexibility to think creatively, an external perspective that helped shift mindsets, and resources to trial new ways of designing and implementing services together.

**Invest in relationships as well as work.** People need to feel safe to ask difficult questions and challenge the status quo. Trust lies at the heart of effective responses to complex disadvantage.

## Young Harrow Foundation

**What it was:** A new organisation established to enable a long-term approach to supporting services and funding for young people in Harrow. Funded by John Lyon's Charity and City Bridge Trust. Based on John Lyon's Charity's "Young People's Foundation" model.

**Scale of funding:** £600,000 core funding grant

### Key Lessons

**Place-based approaches bring stakeholders together but create tensions.** It is possible to unite a range of stakeholders within an area around a shared issue, but they will inevitably have different relationships with place. A localised agenda can risk homogenisation when, in reality, there will always be a need for specialist services.

**Power dynamics are shifting.** As independent funders become the primary source of funding, local authorities and other agencies need to consider how they can use their democratic legitimacy and broader networks to add a different kind of value.

**Engaged funders add significant value.** The role of an active funder can include advocating to other funders on your behalf, bridging city-wide and borough-level conversations, and maintaining standards in infrastructure, governance, and resourcing.

**Manage risk and fear of failure.** Core funding from established funders provides some protection from risk, but concerns about perceived failure remain. Building trust with members in the first year is critical.

**Leadership matters enormously.** Each foundation is heavily influenced by the experience and background of its leadership, as well as the local context in which it operates. Finding individuals who are well-networked within an area, or who have the skills to build relationships quickly, is essential.
