## Emotional Intelligence: The Foundation of Leadership

Every leadership style springs from different components of emotional intelligence (EI). While IQ is largely fixed from childhood, the skills of emotional intelligence can be learned at any age. This is the critical insight: you are not locked into a single leadership approach. By developing the underlying EI competencies, you can expand your repertoire of styles and become a more effective leader.

Research shows that leaders with strengths in six or more emotional intelligence competencies were far more effective than peers who lacked them. In one study of division heads at a global food and beverage company, 87% of leaders with a critical mass of EI competencies placed in the top third for annual salary bonuses, and their divisions outperformed yearly revenue targets by 15% to 20%.

<Note>
This framework is based on Daniel Goleman's research on emotional intelligence and leadership, originally published in the *Harvard Business Review*. The guidance below is presented for practical application in developing your own leadership capabilities.
</Note>

## The Four EI Capabilities

Emotional intelligence consists of four fundamental capabilities. Each is composed of specific competencies that you can identify, measure, and develop.

### Self-Awareness

Self-awareness is the foundation on which all other EI capabilities rest. Without it, you cannot accurately gauge your own strengths, limitations, or the impact you have on others.

| Competency | What It Means |
|---|---|
| **Emotional self-awareness** | The ability to read and understand your own emotions, and to recognize how they affect your work performance, relationships, and decisions |
| **Accurate self-assessment** | A realistic evaluation of your strengths and limitations -- knowing where you excel and where you need support |
| **Self-confidence** | A strong, positive sense of self-worth that allows you to take on challenges and stand behind your convictions |

### Self-Management

Self-management is your ability to regulate your internal states so that emotions serve you rather than derail you. It enables consistency, adaptability, and drive.

| Competency | What It Means |
|---|---|
| **Self-control** | The ability to keep disruptive emotions and impulses in check, staying composed under pressure |
| **Trustworthiness** | A consistent display of honesty and integrity that earns credibility |
| **Conscientiousness** | The ability to manage yourself and your responsibilities reliably |
| **Adaptability** | Skill at adjusting to changing situations and overcoming obstacles without losing momentum |
| **Achievement orientation** | The drive to meet an internal standard of excellence -- pushing yourself to improve |
| **Initiative** | A readiness to seize opportunities and act before being asked |

### Social Awareness

Social awareness is your ability to read the currents around you -- sensing emotions in others, understanding group dynamics, and navigating organizational politics.

| Competency | What It Means |
|---|---|
| **Empathy** | Skill at sensing other people's emotions, understanding their perspective, and taking an active interest in their concerns |
| **Organizational awareness** | The ability to read the currents of organizational life, build decision networks, and navigate politics |
| **Service orientation** | The ability to recognize and respond to the needs of customers, clients, or stakeholders |

### Social Skill

Social skill is where leadership becomes visible. It is your ability to move people in the direction you want -- through inspiration, influence, development, and collaboration.

| Competency | What It Means |
|---|---|
| **Visionary leadership** | The ability to take charge and inspire with a compelling vision |
| **Influence** | The ability to wield a range of persuasive tactics effectively |
| **Developing others** | The propensity to bolster the abilities of others through feedback and guidance |
| **Communication** | Skill at listening and sending clear, convincing, well-tuned messages |
| **Change catalyst** | Proficiency in initiating new ideas and leading people in a new direction |
| **Conflict management** | The ability to de-escalate disagreements and orchestrate resolutions |
| **Building bonds** | Proficiency at cultivating and maintaining a web of relationships |
| **Teamwork and collaboration** | Competence at promoting cooperation and building teams |

## How EI Competencies Map to Leadership Styles

Each leadership style draws on a different combination of EI competencies. Understanding these connections reveals exactly what you need to develop in order to expand your repertoire.

| Leadership Style | Key EI Competencies |
|---|---|
| **Coercive** | Drive to achieve, initiative, self-control |
| **Authoritative** | Self-confidence, empathy, change catalyst |
| **Affiliative** | Empathy, building relationships, communication |
| **Democratic** | Collaboration, team leadership, communication |
| **Pacesetting** | Conscientiousness, drive to achieve, initiative |
| **Coaching** | Developing others, empathy, self-awareness |

If you are primarily a pacesetting leader who wants to use the affiliative style more often, you need to improve your empathy and develop your skills in building relationships and communicating effectively. If you are an authoritative leader who wants to add the democratic style, focus on collaboration and communication.

## Growing Your Emotional Intelligence

Unlike technical skills, which the thinking brain (the neocortex) absorbs quickly, emotional intelligence involves the emotional centers of the brain. These centers learn differently -- they require repetition and practice, not just intellectual understanding.

### Why It Takes Months, Not Days

Improving your emotional intelligence is akin to changing a deeply ingrained habit. The brain circuits that carry your current leadership behaviors have to unlearn old patterns and replace them with new ones. The more often a new behavioral sequence is repeated, the stronger the underlying neural pathways become. Over time, the new patterns become your brain's default option -- and effective leadership becomes effortless.

### The Development Process

**1. Get a 360-degree evaluation.** A diagnosis from multiple viewpoints is essential. Research found that top-performing leaders overestimate their strengths on at most one EI ability, while poor performers overrate themselves on four or more. You likely have blind spots -- the people around you can help you see them.

**2. Make the personal connection.** You must see how your EI weaknesses are tied to your inability to use certain leadership styles effectively. If you do not see the value of the change, you will not sustain the effort. This connection between competency gaps and leadership limitations is what creates genuine motivation to improve.

**3. Create a learning plan.** Work with a coach, mentor, or trusted colleague to turn your day-to-day work into a learning laboratory. Identify specific situations where you can practice new behaviors.

**4. Practice deliberately in real situations.** For example, if you need to develop empathy under pressure, commit to stepping back in tense moments, letting the other person speak, and asking clarifying questions before responding. The goal is to replace reactive habits with intentional behaviors.

**5. Build in relapse prevention.** When you fall back on old patterns -- and you will -- dissect what triggered the relapse and plan what to do differently next time. These "relapse prevention" measures protect against giving up.

**6. Review progress regularly.** Meet with your coach or accountability partner every week or two to track your progress and work through specific challenges.

Over a period of several months of consistent practice, you can make real, measurable improvement. Research shows leaders who commit to this process see both climate improvements and performance gains in their teams.

## Expanding Your Style Repertoire

When leaders learn about the six styles, the most common response is: "But I only have two of those!" or "I can't use all those styles -- it wouldn't be natural." These reactions are understandable, but there are concrete paths forward.

### Option 1: Build a Complementary Team

You can build a team whose members employ the styles you lack. For example, one VP for manufacturing successfully ran a global factory system largely by using the affiliative style. She left strategic direction to a trusted lieutenant skilled in the authoritative approach, delegated performance standards to a colleague adept at pacesetting, and always brought that pacesetter along on plant visits. This is a legitimate approach -- but it requires self-awareness about your gaps and the willingness to rely on others.

### Option 2: Develop New Styles Yourself

The recommended approach is to expand your own repertoire. To do so:

1. **Identify which styles you are lacking.** Be honest about the styles you avoid or struggle with.
2. **Map the missing EI competencies.** Use the table above to determine which competencies underlie those styles.
3. **Develop those competencies deliberately.** Emotional intelligence is entirely learnable with practice. Work on the specific capabilities that will unlock the styles you need.
4. **Practice in low-stakes situations first.** Before deploying a new style in a high-pressure moment, try it in everyday interactions where the stakes are lower.
5. **Seek feedback continuously.** Ask trusted colleagues how the new approach is landing and adjust accordingly.

## Reflection Questions

Take time to honestly assess your current leadership approach. Consider writing your answers down -- the act of articulating your patterns makes them more visible and actionable.

  <Accordion title="Which styles do you use least?">
    Consider the styles that feel uncomfortable or unnatural. Do you avoid coaching because it feels slow? Do you shy away from the democratic style because you prefer to decide quickly?   </Accordion>

## Practical Steps for Developing Underused Styles

### If You Need to Develop the Authoritative Style

Focus on building **self-confidence**, **empathy**, and your ability to act as a **change catalyst**. Practice articulating a clear vision for your team's work and connecting individual tasks to that larger purpose. When giving feedback, frame it around whether performance is advancing the shared mission.

### If You Need to Develop the Affiliative Style

Strengthen your **empathy**, **relationship-building**, and **communication** skills. Start by spending one-on-one time with each team member to understand their lives, concerns, and aspirations -- not just their tasks. Practice offering genuine positive feedback on day-to-day efforts.

### If You Need to Develop the Democratic Style

Work on **collaboration**, **team leadership**, and **communication**. In your next team decision, resist the urge to lead with your own answer. Instead, genuinely ask for input, listen fully, and incorporate what you hear. Build the discipline of soliciting ideas before announcing conclusions.

### If You Need to Develop the Coaching Style

Develop your ability to **develop others**, your **empathy**, and your **self-awareness**. In your next one-on-one, ask your direct report about their career aspirations and what skills they want to build. Help them create a development plan. Give them a challenging assignment, even if it means slower short-term results -- and provide consistent feedback along the way.

### If You Need to Moderate the Pacesetting Style

If pacesetting is your default, practice **letting go**. When you feel the urge to take over a task, pause. Ask a clarifying question instead of stepping in. Remind yourself that your job is to develop your team's capability, not to demonstrate your own. Focus on building the coaching and affiliative competencies to balance your drive for excellence.

### If You Need to Moderate the Coercive Style

If you default to coercive behavior under pressure, focus on **self-control** and **empathy**. When you feel tension rising, make it your job to step back, let the other person speak, and ask questions before directing. Practice entering into dialogue rather than issuing commands.

## The Long Game

Leadership will never be an exact science, but it does not have to be a mystery. The research is clear: the business environment changes continually, and you must respond in kind -- hour to hour, day to day, week to week. The leaders who achieve the best results are those who can play their leadership styles like a professional, using the right one at just the right time and in the right measure.

The path to that flexibility runs through emotional intelligence. It takes months of deliberate practice, not a weekend seminar. But the investment pays off -- in your team's climate, in their performance, and in your own growth as a leader.
