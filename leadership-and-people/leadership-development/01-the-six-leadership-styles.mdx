## Leadership Style Is a Strategic Choice

Many managers assume that leadership style is a function of personality -- that you lead a certain way because of who you are. Research tells a different story. The most effective leaders treat style as a strategic tool, choosing the approach that best addresses the demands of a particular situation.

A landmark study by the consulting firm Hay/McBer, drawing on a random sample of 3,871 executives from a database of more than 20,000 worldwide, identified six distinct leadership styles. Each springs from different components of emotional intelligence, and each has a measurable impact on your team's working atmosphere and, in turn, on financial performance.

Think of these styles as clubs in a golf bag. Over the course of a round, you pick and choose based on the demands of each shot. Sometimes you deliberate; often the choice is instinctive. The best leaders operate the same way -- sensing the challenge ahead, pulling out the right style, and putting it to work.

<Note>
This framework is drawn from Daniel Goleman's research on leadership and emotional intelligence, originally published in the *Harvard Business Review*. The concepts below are presented as practical guidance for developing your own leadership approach.
</Note>

## The Six Styles at a Glance

| Style | Phrase | When to Use | Climate Impact |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Coercive** | "Do what I tell you" | Crisis, turnaround, problem employees | Negative |
| **Authoritative** | "Come with me" | New vision needed, clear direction required | Most strongly positive |
| **Affiliative** | "People come first" | Healing rifts, motivating during stress | Positive |
| **Democratic** | "What do you think?" | Building buy-in, getting input from capable employees | Positive |
| **Pacesetting** | "Do as I do, now" | Quick results from competent, self-motivated teams | Negative when overused |
| **Coaching** | "Try this" | Developing long-term strengths, helping employees grow | Positive |

## The Coercive Style

**Modus operandi:** Demands immediate compliance.

**Underlying EI competencies:** Drive to achieve, initiative, self-control.

The coercive style is the least effective in most situations. When you rely on top-down decision-making, new ideas die on the vine. Your people feel disrespected and stop bringing forward suggestions. Their sense of responsibility evaporates -- unable to act on their own initiative, they lose ownership and feel little accountability. The style erodes pride in work, undermines motivation, and leaves people alienated, wondering "How does any of this matter?"

### When It Works

Despite its drawbacks, the coercive style has a narrow but important place in your repertoire:

- **During a genuine crisis** -- a turnaround, a hostile takeover, or an emergency where decisive action is essential.
- **Breaking failed business habits** -- shocking an organization into new ways of working when entrenched patterns are leading to failure.
- **With problem employees** -- when all other approaches have been exhausted and direct, unambiguous demands are the only remaining option.

### When It Fails

If you rely solely on this style, or continue using it once the crisis passes, the long-term impact on morale and engagement will be severe. People stop bringing bad news, flexibility collapses, and commitment plummets. Use it sparingly, and transition to other styles as soon as the immediate emergency subsides.

## The Authoritative Style

**Modus operandi:** Mobilizes people toward a vision.

**Underlying EI competencies:** Self-confidence, empathy, change catalyst.

Of the six styles, the authoritative approach is the most consistently effective, driving up every aspect of organizational climate. When you lead with this style, you make clear to people how their work fits into a larger vision. People understand that what they do matters and why.

You define standards that revolve around the vision. When you give performance feedback -- positive or negative -- the criterion is whether that performance furthers the shared direction. You state the end goal but give people plenty of leeway to devise their own means, which fuels innovation, experimentation, and calculated risk-taking.

### When It Works

- **When a business or team is adrift** and needs a new course charted.
- **When changes require a fresh vision** and people need to see the bigger picture.
- **In almost any business situation** -- it is the most versatile positive style.

### When It Fails

- **When working with experts or peers more experienced than you** -- they may see the approach as pompous or out of touch.
- **When you become overbearing** -- pushing too hard can undermine the egalitarian spirit of an effective team.

## The Affiliative Style

**Modus operandi:** Creates harmony and builds emotional bonds.

**Underlying EI competencies:** Empathy, building relationships, communication.

The affiliative style revolves around people. When you use it, you value individuals and their emotions more than tasks and goals. You strive to keep employees happy and create harmony, building strong emotional bonds and reaping fierce loyalty as a result.

This style has a markedly positive effect on communication -- people who feel connected talk more, share ideas, and share inspiration. It drives up flexibility because trust allows habitual innovation and risk-taking. And affiliative leaders offer ample positive feedback, which is all the more powerful because day-to-day recognition is so rare in most workplaces.

### When It Works

- **Healing rifts** -- rebuilding trust after a toxic predecessor or a period of conflict.
- **Motivating during stressful circumstances** -- when people need emotional support.
- **Building team harmony** and increasing morale or improving communication.

### When It Fails

- **Used exclusively**, it can allow poor performance to go uncorrected. People may perceive that mediocrity is tolerated.
- **Without constructive guidance**, employees are left to figure out how to improve on their own.
- **In complex challenges requiring clear direction**, the affiliative style alone can leave people rudderless.

The most effective affiliative leaders pair this style with the authoritative approach -- setting vision and standards while also caring for and nurturing their people.

## The Democratic Style

**Modus operandi:** Forges consensus through participation.

**Underlying EI competencies:** Collaboration, team leadership, communication.

By giving people a voice in decisions that affect their goals and work methods, you build trust, respect, and commitment. The democratic style drives up flexibility and responsibility, and because people help set their own standards, they tend to be realistic about what can and cannot be accomplished.

### When It Works

- **When you are uncertain about the best direction** and need ideas from capable employees.
- **When building buy-in** for a decision that requires broad commitment.
- **To generate fresh ideas** for executing a vision you have already established.

### When It Fails

- **When employees lack the competence or information** to offer sound advice.
- **In times of crisis** -- building consensus is counterproductive when speed is essential.
- **When overused**, it can produce endless meetings, elusive consensus, and people who feel confused and leaderless.

## The Pacesetting Style

**Modus operandi:** Sets high standards for performance and exemplifies them personally.

**Underlying EI competencies:** Conscientiousness, drive to achieve, initiative.

The pacesetting style sounds admirable on paper -- you set extremely high performance standards and model them yourself. You are obsessive about doing things better and faster and expect the same from everyone. But the research is clear: this style frequently destroys climate.

Many employees feel overwhelmed by your demands for excellence and their morale drops. Guidelines may be clear in your head, but you do not always state them -- you expect people to "just know." Work becomes about second-guessing what you want rather than doing one's best along a clear course. Flexibility and responsibility evaporate as work becomes task-focused and routinized.

### When It Works

- **When all employees are self-motivated, highly competent, and need little direction** -- for example, leading teams of highly skilled professionals such as R&D groups or legal teams.
- **To get work done on time or ahead of schedule** with a talented, driven team.

### When It Fails

- **When team members are not as capable or self-directed as you** -- you risk becoming a micromanager, obsessed with details and taking over when performance slackens.
- **Over extended periods** -- people lose their sense of how personal efforts fit into the big picture, and commitment dwindles.

Like the coercive style, pacesetting should never be used in isolation.

## The Coaching Style

**Modus operandi:** Develops people for the future.

**Underlying EI competencies:** Developing others, empathy, self-awareness.

The coaching style is the least frequently used, yet its impact on climate and performance is markedly positive. When you coach, you help employees identify their unique strengths and weaknesses and tie them to their personal and career aspirations. You encourage long-term development goals, help conceptualize plans for reaching them, and give plentiful instruction and feedback.

The style requires constant dialogue, and that dialogue pushes up every driver of climate. When employees know you are watching and you care about what they do, they feel free to experiment. The ongoing conversation ensures people know what is expected and how their work fits into a larger strategy. And the implicit message -- "I believe in you, I'm investing in you, and I expect your best efforts" -- builds deep commitment.

### When It Works

- **When employees are already aware of their weaknesses** and want to improve.
- **When employees understand how developing new abilities** can help them advance.
- **In almost any business situation** -- although it focuses on personal development rather than immediate tasks, it delivers bottom-line results.

### When It Fails

- **When employees are resistant to learning** or changing their ways.
- **When you lack the expertise** to help the employee develop -- coaching without competence breeds frustration.

Many leaders say they don't have time for coaching. But after an initial investment, it takes little extra time -- and leaders who ignore this style are passing up one of their most powerful tools.

## How Leadership Styles Affect Climate

The research measured the correlation between each leadership style and six key drivers of organizational climate:

| Climate Factor | Description |
|---|---|
| **Flexibility** | How free employees feel to innovate without red tape |
| **Responsibility** | People's sense of ownership and accountability |
| **Standards** | The level of performance expectations people set |
| **Rewards** | The sense of accuracy about feedback and aptness of recognition |
| **Clarity** | How well people understand the mission and values |
| **Commitment** | The level of dedication to a common purpose |

The authoritative style has the strongest overall positive impact on climate, followed by affiliative, democratic, and coaching. The coercive and pacesetting styles have a negative overall impact -- though both have targeted situations where they are the right choice.

Organizational climate, in turn, accounts for nearly a third of financial performance. That is too significant an impact to ignore.

## Master Four or More Styles for the Best Results

The research delivers one finding above all others: **leaders who have mastered four or more styles -- especially the authoritative, affiliative, democratic, and coaching styles -- achieve the very best climate and business performance.**

The most effective leaders do not mechanically match a style to a checklist of situations. They are far more fluid. They read the room in the first minutes of a conversation and seamlessly adjust their approach. They might inspire an underperforming employee by reminding them why their work matters (authoritative), reenergize someone by exploring their dreams and aspirations (coaching), or deliver a direct ultimatum when all else has failed (coercive).

This flexibility is not innate for most people, but it can be learned. The key is understanding the emotional intelligence competencies that underlie each style -- and deliberately developing the ones you are missing.
