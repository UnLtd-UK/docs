## Taking a strategic approach

When your organisation faces a major disruption — whether a pandemic, economic shock, restructuring, or other significant event — bringing your team back to full operation requires careful planning. Do not assume that returning to work is simply the reverse of standing down.

Think strategically about how you bring staff back, considering three key factors:

- **Skills** — which roles and capabilities do you need first to restart effectively?
- **Costs** — what can your organisation afford, and in what order should you rebuild capacity?
- **Ability to work in current circumstances** — can staff work from home, do they have caring responsibilities, and are they able to return to previous working patterns?

Use these factors to prioritise which roles to restore first and how to phase your team's return.

## Communication as a priority

Communication is the single most important factor in a successful return to work. Your team members will have a wide range of emotional responses to coming back, from eagerness to anxiety, and many will have concerns about job security.

### Prepare people early

Start communicating with your team well before the return date. Let them know what to expect, what the timeline looks like, and what support will be available to them.

### Address the full range of emotions

Recognise that staff responses will vary widely:

- Some will be eager to return and re-engage with their work.
- Some will be nervous about changes to their role, working conditions, or the organisation itself.
- Some will fear redundancy or reduced hours.
- Some may have developed anxiety about returning to a workplace environment after an extended absence.

Make sure you are speaking to staff regularly and creating space for them to raise concerns.

### Provide the right infrastructure

Ensure that the practical support your team needs is in place before they return. This includes appropriate equipment, updated policies, health and safety measures, and access to wellbeing support such as counselling.

## Creating a risk register for managing disruption

Develop a risk register that documents the specific risks your organisation faces during and after the disruption. This should be a living document that you review and update regularly.

Consider making your risk register available to:

- **Staff** — so they understand the challenges the organisation is navigating and can contribute to solutions.
- **Board members** — so they can provide informed governance and oversight.
- **Advisors and investors** — so they can offer relevant support and adjust expectations where needed.

A transparent approach to risk management builds trust across your organisation and helps everyone feel informed and involved.

## Assessing whether skills remain adequate

Disruption often changes the skills your organisation needs. Take stock of whether your team's current capabilities match your new requirements.

Ask yourself:

- Have your working practices changed significantly? For example, has your organisation moved to online marketing, remote service delivery, or digital collaboration tools?
- Do staff need training to work effectively under new conditions?
- Are there skills gaps that need to be filled through recruitment, upskilling, or restructuring?

If you anticipate that changes in income or working partners could lead to skill shortages, address this as early as possible. Waiting until the problem becomes urgent limits your options.

## Updating your policies

Use the disruption as an opportunity to update your organisational policies to reflect new realities. If new working practices have been useful and readily adopted, formalise them.

Key areas to review include:

- **Remote working** — if your team has worked remotely during the disruption, consider whether a permanent or hybrid remote working policy would benefit your organisation.
- **Flexible working** — update your flexible working policy to reflect what you have learned about how your team works best.
- **Working from home** — ensure you have clear policies covering equipment, expenses, communication expectations, and health and safety for home-based workers.

Updating policies now signals to your team that you are building on what has been learned, not simply returning to the way things were before.

## Income and financial resilience

Disruption often exposes financial vulnerabilities. Use this period to strengthen your organisation's financial position.

- **Diversify income streams.** If your organisation relies heavily on one or two sources of income, explore additional revenue channels to reduce your exposure to future shocks.
- **Seek flexibility from funders and partners.** Many funders and partners will be understanding about changed circumstances. Approach them early to discuss adjusted timelines, revised deliverables, or repurposed funding.
- **Reassess your reserves.** Review what capital you have available and what can be freed up. Think creatively about how you might use existing capital and assets, including potentially leveraging investment against existing assets.

## Supporting staff wellbeing during transitions

Returning to work after a major disruption is a transition, and transitions are inherently stressful. Invest in your team's wellbeing throughout the process.

### Provide access to counselling

Make counselling or employee assistance programmes available to all staff, not just those who request it. Normalise the use of these services by communicating about them openly and without stigma.

### Ensure appropriate equipment and environments

Whether staff are returning to a physical workplace or continuing to work remotely, make sure they have the equipment and environment they need to work safely and effectively.

### Make policy changes that support wellbeing

Review your policies through the lens of staff wellbeing. Consider whether changes to working hours, leave entitlements, or workload expectations could help your team through the transition.

## For charities: reviewing your objectives

If your organisation is a charity, a major disruption may be an opportunity to review and adapt your charitable objectives. Regulatory bodies may offer fast-tracked processes for updating objectives when your organisation is adapting to changed circumstances.

Reviewing your objectives could allow you to:

- Open up your area of focus to respond to emerging needs.
- Access new sources of support and investment aligned with updated objectives.
- Better serve your beneficiaries in the current environment.

Consider whether your current objectives still reflect the most impactful way your organisation can fulfil its mission, and take action to update them if they do not.
