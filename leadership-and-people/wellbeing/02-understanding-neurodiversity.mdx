## What neurodiversity means

Neurodiversity describes the natural variation in brain structures, connections, and functions that exists across all humans. Every person's brain is different. Humanity is neurodiverse — everyone has a different neurological profile.

Sometimes these variations are substantial enough to give rise to more categorical differences, corresponding to diagnostic labels such as autism and ADHD. An individual alone cannot be neurodiverse, but a group of people may be neurodiverse if they differ in their neurotype.

Like a diverse ecosystem, neurological variation is almost certainly beneficial to society. Neurodivergence in individuals can come with significant advantages.

## Key terminology

Understanding the correct terminology helps you communicate respectfully and accurately about neurodiversity.

### Neurodivergent

A person whose underlying neurology gives rise to differences in both experience and behaviour that can be labelled or categorised. "Neurodivergent" is a useful collective term that encompasses a large range of people who commonly experience barriers in work and learning environments. An individual may or may not choose to self-describe using this term.

### Neurotypical

A term that refers to the majority neurotype. Neurotypical people differ from each other at an individual level, just as people with autism or any other neurotype differ from one another. Nonetheless, "neurotypical" is a useful term to describe the dominant neurotype — the one that workplaces, education systems, and social environments have historically been designed around.

### Neurotype

A category of neurological profile. People may share a neurotype (such as autism or ADHD) while still differing significantly from each other as individuals.

### Using these terms correctly

It is common for the terms "neurodiversity" and "neurodivergent" to be interchanged, but they mean different things. Here is a practical example of correct usage:

> A research centre conducts research into **neurodiversity** and its consequences for learning. The researchers are particularly interested in how **neurodivergent** people learn differently from **neurotypical** people. Workplaces and processes have historically been created for the **neurotypical** majority, so practitioners and teams need to work together to create and evaluate adaptations to accommodate different **neurotypes** and support inclusion.

## Neurodivergent conditions

People with any of the following conditions may be described as neurodivergent:

- **Autism** — differences in social communication, sensory processing, and patterns of thinking and behaviour.
- **Dyslexia** — differences in how the brain processes written and spoken language, affecting reading, writing, and spelling.
- **Dyspraxia** — differences in motor coordination, planning, and organisation of movement.
- **ADHD (Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder)** — differences in attention, impulse control, and activity levels.
- **DLD (Developmental Language Disorder)** — differences in understanding and using spoken language.
- **Tourette syndrome** — a neurological condition characterised by repetitive, involuntary movements and vocalisations called tics.

Each of these conditions presents differently in different individuals. A person may have more than one neurodivergent condition, and the way it affects their work and daily life will be unique to them.

## Legal framework

### The Equality Act 2010

A neurodivergent person may meet the legal definition of disability under the Equality Act 2010. If so, they are entitled to have **reasonable adjustments** made in the workplace. This is a legal right, not a discretionary benefit.

You should be aware of:

- **Who to approach** in your organisation for advice on your legal duties around reasonable adjustments.
- **Your responsibility** to consider whether neurodivergent colleagues may need adjustments, even if they have not formally requested them.

### Additional support needs

A neurodivergent person may be assessed as having additional support needs, in which case they are entitled to receive additional support. However, many neurodivergent people appear to manage well and may not have a diagnosis or any formal recognition of their neurodivergence.

## Challenges in identification

Neurodivergence is often under-identified, which means people may not receive the support they are entitled to. Key patterns to be aware of include:

### Under-identification of girls and women with autism

Girls and women are much less likely to be identified as having autism than boys and men. This means that female colleagues may be neurodivergent without having a diagnosis, and their needs may go unrecognised.

### Late diagnosis of dyslexia

People with dyslexia may not receive a diagnosis until well into adulthood. Some only undergo an assessment at college, university, or later in their careers. If someone has been managing without a diagnosis, they may have developed compensatory strategies that mask their difficulties but come at a significant personal cost.

### Misidentification of neurodivergent behaviours as misconduct

Some neurodivergent behaviours — particularly those associated with ADHD — can be mistakenly interpreted as deliberately challenging, provocative, or disruptive. This can lead to unfair disciplinary actions. These outcomes may amount to discrimination.

When you encounter a colleague or team member whose behaviour seems difficult to explain, pause and consider whether their behaviour may reflect a neurodivergent neurotype rather than a choice to act inappropriately.

## Practical steps for inclusive practice

### Familiarise yourself with your organisation's policies

Make sure you understand:

- How the Equality Act 2010 and the duty to make reasonable adjustments apply in your workplace.
- Who in your organisation can advise on legal duties and best practice around neurodiversity.
- What existing policies and procedures are in place to support neurodivergent colleagues.

### Learn from the individual and their support network

The best source of information about a neurodivergent person's needs is the person themselves and, where appropriate, their family or support network. You do not need to be a specialist in specific neurological conditions to provide good support. Focus on listening, asking, and responding to what the individual tells you about their experience.

### Make adaptations available to everyone

If you have introduced accessible resources, tools, or adaptations for a neurodivergent colleague, consider making these available to the whole team. Many adaptations that benefit neurodivergent people — such as clear written instructions, flexible working arrangements, or quiet workspaces — also benefit neurotypical colleagues.

### Focus on quality of output over productivity

Take a moment to reconsider your expectations around work output. Is it more important to complete every item on a task list, or to complete a subset of tasks well? Shifting your focus from quantity to quality can help create an environment where neurodivergent colleagues can do their best work.

## The power of the neurodiversity concept

Neurodiversity encompasses everyone. This is the real power of the concept and the key way it differs from older approaches to workplace inclusion.

Rather than identifying, labelling, and trying to fix specific difficulties in specific individuals, the neurodiversity framework encourages you to:

- Provide accessible and inclusive working environments for your whole team, not just selected individuals who may also need additional support.
- Approach each person on their own terms, regardless of diagnostic label.
- Recognise that neurological variation is natural, expected, and beneficial.

When you design your workplace, processes, and culture with neurodiversity in mind, you create an environment where everyone — neurodivergent and neurotypical alike — can contribute their best.
