## Why This Matters

Outstanding leaders are both highly self-aware and very thoughtful in how they approach situations and relationships. Before you can effectively motivate others, you need to understand what drives you. This guide explores personal motivation, introduces practical frameworks, and offers concrete approaches to energising your team.

## Understanding What Motivates You

Self-awareness is the foundation of effective leadership. Start by reflecting honestly on these questions:

### What Gets You Out of Bed?

What makes you choose this work rather than something else? Common answers include:
- The challenge of solving complex problems
- Working towards a meaningful mission
- The excitement of building something new
- The opportunity to make a tangible difference

### What Makes You Full of Joy?

When do you feel most alive and engaged? This might be:
- Completing work to a high standard and being "in the zone"
- Moving through a process quickly or skilfully
- Successfully explaining complex information to others
- Seeing the direct impact of your efforts

### What Spurs You into Action?

What overcomes your inertia and gets you moving? Consider:
- Working on something new or groundbreaking
- The urgency of addressing an injustice or inequality
- Engaging in debate and discussing challenging topics
- The satisfaction of completing tasks and clearing your to-do list

### What Would You Fight to the End For?

Understanding your non-negotiables reveals your deepest motivations:
- Standing up against unfairness
- Backing people who cannot advocate for themselves
- Finding a simpler or better way of doing something
- Protecting the mission of your organisation

### What Feedback Do You Find Most Useful?

The type of feedback you value reveals how you are motivated:
- Constant and direct, with clear follow-on actions
- Specific and constructive, tied to outcomes
- Recognition of effort as well as results

### How Do You Like to Be Rewarded?

Knowing your own reward preferences helps you understand your team's:
- Recognition of good work from peers and leaders
- Fair compensation that reflects contribution
- Greater responsibility and autonomy
- Opportunities for growth and learning

## The Motivation Diamond

The Motivation Diamond is a framework for mapping the relationship between what motivates you at work and what motivates you in the rest of your life. It helps you identify alignment or tension between different sources of motivation.

### How to Use It

1. **Map your work motivations** -- What energises you in your professional role? What drains you?
2. **Map your personal motivations** -- What gives you energy outside work? What matters most to you personally?
3. **Look for patterns** -- Where do your work and personal motivations align? Where do they pull in different directions?

### Reflection Questions

- **What do you notice about your patterns?** Are your work and personal motivations broadly aligned, or are they pulling you in different directions?
- **If they align well, what can you do to maintain this?** Identify the conditions that support this alignment and protect them.
- **If they diverge, what can you do to bring them closer together?** Consider what changes -- to your role, your approach, or your priorities -- might help.
- **What else might be at play?** Consider factors like time pressure, competing demands, or compensations you have made.

The Motivation Diamond is not about achieving perfect balance. It is about awareness -- understanding what drives you so you can make better decisions about where to invest your energy.

## Understanding "Strokes"

Drawn from Transactional Analysis, the concept of "strokes" refers to the units of recognition we give and receive. Every interaction involves strokes, and they profoundly affect motivation, engagement, and wellbeing.

### Key Concepts

- **Stroke economy** -- The overall pattern of recognition in your team. Is it generous or scarce? Balanced or one-sided?
- **Stroke currency** -- Different people value different types of recognition. Some value public praise; others prefer private acknowledgement. Some want verbal recognition; others value written feedback or tangible rewards.
- **Stroke balance** -- The ratio between positive and negative strokes someone receives. A healthy balance is weighted towards the positive, but honest constructive feedback is also essential.

### Applying This to Your Team

Ask yourself:
- What do you know about each team member's stroke economy, currency, and balance?
- Who in your team is receiving too little recognition?
- Who might be receiving the wrong type of recognition (something you value but they do not)?
- What might you do differently as a result?

Small, consistent acts of genuine recognition often have a greater impact on motivation than grand gestures.

## Behavioural Drivers

We all carry behavioural drivers -- internal messages that push us to operate in certain ways. Understanding your dominant drivers helps you recognise when they are helping you and when they are getting in the way.

The five common behavioural drivers are:

| Driver | Internal Message | Strength | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Be Perfect** | "I must get everything right" | High standards, attention to detail | Perfectionism, difficulty delegating, fear of mistakes |
| **Try Hard** | "I must give maximum effort" | Persistence, enthusiasm, willingness to tackle tough problems | Burnout, valuing effort over results, difficulty finishing |
| **Hurry Up** | "I must do things quickly" | Efficiency, energy, ability to meet deadlines | Cutting corners, impatience, not listening properly |
| **Be Strong** | "I must not show weakness" | Resilience, dependability, calm under pressure | Difficulty asking for help, emotional suppression, isolation |
| **Please Others** | "I must keep everyone happy" | Empathy, team harmony, responsiveness | Difficulty saying no, conflict avoidance, neglecting own needs |

### Working with Your Drivers

- **Identify your dominant drivers** -- Most people have one or two that are particularly strong
- **Notice when they help** -- Your drivers are not inherently bad; they contain real strengths
- **Notice when they hinder** -- Watch for the moments when a driver is pushing you to act in ways that are counterproductive
- **Give yourself permission to ease off** -- You do not have to be perfect, try harder, hurry up, be strong, or please everyone all the time

Understanding your drivers also helps you recognise them in your team members, which makes you a more empathetic and effective leader.

## Leadership Presence Principles

Outstanding leaders have a distinctive way of being present with their teams. Three principles capture this well:

### "Managing by Walking About"

Be visible and accessible. You do not lead from behind a closed door or from the top of an email chain. Make time to be physically or virtually present with your team, having informal conversations that build trust and give you a real sense of what is happening.

### "Nose In, Fingers Out"

Stay informed about what your team is doing without micromanaging the detail. Show genuine interest in their work, ask thoughtful questions, and understand the challenges they face -- but resist the urge to take over. Your role is to support, not to do.

### "I'll Let You Stumble, But You Won't Graze Your Knees"

Allow your team members the space to learn from their own experiences, including making mistakes. Your job is to create a safe enough environment that people can take risks and grow, while ensuring no one faces consequences that would be genuinely harmful. This builds confidence, capability, and trust.

## Practical Actions for Motivating Your Team

Based on the frameworks above, here are concrete steps you can take:

1. **Learn what motivates each individual** -- Do not assume everyone is motivated by the same things you are. Ask them directly.

2. **Match recognition to the person** -- Tailor your "strokes" to each team member's currency. Public praise for one person might be private acknowledgement for another.

3. **Give feedback regularly** -- Do not save it all for formal reviews. Frequent, specific feedback is more motivating than occasional general comments.

4. **Create opportunities for autonomy** -- People are more motivated when they have some control over how they do their work.

5. **Connect work to purpose** -- Help your team see how their daily tasks contribute to the organisation's mission.

6. **Be aware of your own drivers** -- Notice when your "Be Perfect" or "Hurry Up" tendencies are creating unnecessary pressure for your team.

7. **Model vulnerability** -- Admitting when you do not know something or when you have made a mistake gives others permission to be honest too.

8. **Invest in development** -- Show your team you are invested in their growth, not just their output.

9. **Protect their energy** -- Be thoughtful about workload, deadlines, and the pace you set. Sustainable motivation requires sustainable demands.

10. **Check in regularly** -- Ask your team what is working and what is not. The best motivational strategies evolve with your team's needs.
