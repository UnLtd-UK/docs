## Why This Matters

"Not the right team" is consistently one of the top reasons start-ups fail. Building a strong team is not just about hiring well -- it is about understanding how your organisation's needs change as you grow, structuring roles effectively, and making the shift from doing everything yourself to leading others.

As one memorable quote puts it:

> "CFO to CEO: What would happen if we trained everyone and they left?
> CEO to CFO: What if we didn't and they stayed?"

## Team Size Stages

Operational growth rarely happens in a straight line, which makes it difficult to plan capacity. However, some useful rules of thumb have been identified:

| Stage | Team Size | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Setting the foundation | 1--4 staff members | You need strategic doers who can wear multiple hats |
| Building to scale | 5--10 staff members | This is the team management stage where structure becomes important |
| Being an organisation | 11+ staff members | Transitioning from entrepreneur/founder to CEO |

Understanding which stage you are in helps you make better decisions about who to hire next and how to organise your team.

## Role Categorisation

A useful framework divides all roles into three categories:

| Category | Focus | Relationship to Revenue |
|---|---|---|
| **Green -- Strategy** | Vision, direction, growth planning | Profit (drives long-term value) |
| **Blue -- Revenue** | Sales, delivery, client work | Income (brings in money) |
| **Red -- Back Office** | Administration, finance, HR, operations | Cost (necessary support) |

### How This Plays Out in Practice

As a founder, you typically start as a **Blue** role (bringing in revenue) while also handling **Red** tasks (admin, finance). As you grow:

1. You bring on more Blue roles to help generate revenue, with everyone handling their own Red tasks
2. Eventually you hire a dedicated Red role to take on administration
3. This frees you to move into a **Green** role, focusing on strategy
4. You are now managing three people -- which is the optimum team size

### The Danger Zone

As the business continues to grow, you might take on more Blue and Red roles until you are managing six or more people. At that point, managing can consume over 60 percent of your time, pulling you into a Red role and causing growth to stagnate.

The solution is to create team leads: promote a Blue role to manage the revenue team and bring in a Red role to manage the back-office team. This creates smaller teams of three or four, which work more effectively, and allows you to return to your Green strategic role.

## Optimal and Problematic Team Sizes

Research has identified that certain numbers of people in teams and organisations work well, while others create friction:

- **Numbers that work well:** 4, 12, 24
- **Numbers that cause problems:** 7, 17

The pattern suggests that teams of three to four people are the most effective unit. When you reach problematic numbers, consider splitting into smaller sub-teams with their own leads.

## Moving from "I" to "We"

One of the hardest transitions for founders is letting go. Your organisation is "your baby," and handing over responsibilities can feel risky. But delegation and succession planning are essential for growth.

### Key Challenges for Founders

- **Leadership versus management** -- Understanding that both are needed and they require different skills
- **Balancing roles** -- You cannot do everything yourself as the organisation grows
- **Letting go** -- Trusting others to deliver, even if their approach differs from yours
- **Making time for reflection** -- Social entrepreneurs rarely make time to learn, reflect, plan, or recharge

### Practical Steps for Delegation

- Identify which tasks only you can do and which can be done by others
- Start delegating smaller responsibilities and build trust over time
- Establish clear decision-making processes so team members know their authority
- Accept that others may do things differently -- focus on outcomes, not methods

## Balancing Strategy and Operations

As your organisation scales, you need to spend more time in "helicopter" mode (strategic oversight) and less in "ground command" (day-to-day operations). The key is understanding when and how to shift that balance.

Ask yourself:

- What percentage of your time is spent on strategic versus operational work?
- What internal factors are keeping you operational (habit, control, lack of trust)?
- What external factors are pushing you to stay hands-on (resource constraints, urgent deadlines)?
- What would you need to change to spend more time on strategy?

## Recruitment as Talent-Finding

Recruitment is not just a recycling exercise where you replace like with like. When someone leaves, it is an opportunity to bring in someone better suited to your current business needs.

### Approach Recruitment Proactively

- Get good at telling your organisation's story so that talented people want to join you
- Always be ready to identify talent -- anyone you meet at a conference, event, or workshop could be a future employee
- If you do not have a role for someone right now, keep them in your network for when you do

### Build a Simple, Effective Recruitment Process

1. **Advertise** -- Keep the job advert clear, informative, and to the point. Reflect your organisation's personality, but remember candidates may be reading hundreds of adverts
2. **Application review** -- Involve the hiring manager and relevant colleagues. Consider blind recruitment to remove unconscious bias
3. **Interviewing** -- Use techniques appropriate to the role. Include practical assessments where relevant
4. **Offer** -- Move quickly, especially for candidates who are actively job-seeking. Be clear about why you chose them, the start date, and salary

### What to Include in Job Adverts

Focus on knowledge, skills, and experience required for the role. Make requirements measurable. Keep the list simple -- if it is not essential, leave it out. This helps you attract the right candidates, makes performance management easier later, and avoids discouraging good applicants with an unrealistic wish list.

## Dynamic Job Descriptions

Static job descriptions quickly become outdated in a growing organisation. Consider using **dynamic job descriptions** that are reviewed quarterly, allocating percentage time across key areas:

| Quarter | Sales (%) | Admin (%) | Project Management (%) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Q1 | 10 | 40 | 50 |
| Q2 | 30 | 20 | 50 |
| Q3 | 50 | 10 | 40 |
| Q4 | 50 | 20 | 30 |

This approach gives you the flexibility to adapt roles to the changing needs of the business while giving employees clarity about their priorities each quarter.

## Onboarding Best Practices

The onboarding stage is crucial. A poor start can lead to early departures, wasted recruitment costs, and team disruption.

### Getting It Right

- **Standardise the process** -- Create a consistent onboarding experience, especially if you will be hiring frequently
- **Do not overload people** -- Introduce information in stages rather than everything on day one
- **Help them live your values** -- Give new starters the opportunity to understand and experience your mission and culture
- **Assign a buddy** -- Having a go-to person for questions makes the first weeks much smoother

## Probation Best Practices

Use the probation period thoughtfully:

- Focus on areas of expertise and skills rather than job titles
- Recognise that individuals often enjoy and contribute to more than one function
- Agree the decision-making process for keeping them or letting them go in advance
- Check their compatibility with and commitment to your mission
- Assess whether they trust, value, and respect you, their colleagues, and the organisation
- At the midpoint (around the end of month three for a six-month probation), test alignment with your organisation's values
- If it is not going to work out, be fair rather than just "nice" -- give them opportunities to improve, but set clear deadlines and stick to them
