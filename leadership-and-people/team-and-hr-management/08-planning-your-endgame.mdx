## Why This Matters

If you lead a nonprofit or social enterprise, the most important strategic question you can ask is not "How do you scale up?" It is "What is your endgame?"

Most nonprofits face structural barriers to organisational growth that remain too high to overcome through scaling alone. But by asking a different question and focusing on a different kind of goal, you can maximise your ability to achieve significant social impact -- regardless of how large your organisation becomes.

The scale of your organisation does not necessarily equal the scale of your impact.

## The Social Capital Chasm

The odds of scaling up as a nonprofit are long. Between 1975 and 2008, just 201 US nonprofits reached the 50 million dollar annual revenue mark. Today, more than half of all registered US nonprofits receive less than 100,000 dollars in annual funding, and only 7 percent receive 1 million dollars or more.

This gap between early-stage funding and the budget needed to operate at full scale is what researchers call the **social capital chasm**. Most nonprofits never cross it.

### The Growth Stages

| Stage | Annual Budget | Description |
|---|---|---|
| Start-up | Less than $500,000 | Getting off the ground |
| Proof of concept | $500,000 to $2 million | Demonstrating your model works |
| Early scaling | $2 million to $5 million | Growing the organisation |
| Breakout scaling | $5 million to $10 million | Reaching significant operational scale |
| Full scale | $10 million or greater | Operating at the scale needed for broad impact |

The chasm typically lies between early scaling and breakout scaling, where early-stage foundation funding runs out but commercial or government funding is not yet accessible.

## Four Structural Challenges

Understanding why the chasm exists helps you plan around it. Four factors distinguish the nonprofit sector from the for-profit sector:

### 1. No Equity or Ownership

Nonprofits cannot offer stock options or equity-based compensation, making it harder to attract top talent during early growth stages. There is no "exit value" for founders, removing a key incentive to push for scale. The sector also lacks the incentive structures that drive scale-enhancing mergers and acquisitions in the commercial world.

### 2. Funding and Service Misalignment

In a for-profit business, success at delivering your product or service directly drives revenue. In the social sector, you have to win two games simultaneously: a product game (delivering real social impact) and a revenue game (raising funds). The people who benefit from your work are often not the same people who fund it.

### 3. Bias Against Investment in Growth

A widespread norm in the social sector requires 85 percent or more of an organisation's capital to go toward programmes rather than operations. This severely limits investment in the structures, processes, and capabilities needed for organisational growth. Funders often want to contribute only to programmes that deliver direct, immediate impact, which destabilises the sector and hinders the ability of organisations to scale.

### 4. Skewed Grant Funding Structure

Funders often prefer to support specific programmes over limited periods rather than an organisation's overall mission. This turns fundraising into a continuous scramble for small, piecemeal grants -- an effort that taxes resources and further limits the ability to grow. An organisation might easily secure a one-time grant to install solar lights in schools but struggle to get multiyear funding to train teachers.

## Six Endgame Options

Given these structural realities, nonprofit leaders should shift their focus from the scale of their organisation to the impact their organisation can help achieve. There are six endgame options to consider:

### 1. Open Source

You invest in research and development to create and refine a breakthrough idea or intervention, then spread it by serving as a knowledge hub from which other organisations can draw resources.

**Core approach:** Conducting R&D and sharing knowledge openly

**Future role:** Serving as the knowledge hub for research related to your breakthrough idea

**Example:** Alcoholics Anonymous does not run any AA meetings. It operates a resource centre providing instructional and inspirational materials to local groups, which host their own meetings. The core competency is effective knowledge management.

### 2. Replication

You develop and prove a breakthrough product or model, then find other organisations to deliver it -- rather than expanding your own organisation.

**Core approach:** Defining a replicable operating and impact model, demonstrating its efficacy, and sharing it with other organisations

**Future role:** Providing certification of franchise programmes and training services, and serving as a centre of excellence

**Example:** Successful charter school networks test new approaches to student achievement, then set up replication centres where educators from other communities can learn to follow their model.

### 3. Government Adoption

You prove your concept and demonstrate it can work at significant scale, then mount an advocacy effort to influence policy and budget decisions so that government takes over delivery.

**Core approach:** Delivering results at sufficient scale and efficiency to make the case for public sector involvement

**Future role:** Offering services to government agencies and maintaining research and advocacy efforts

**Example:** Private charities and parochial schools ran the first kindergartens in the United States. After boards of education recognised the developmental benefits, they folded kindergarten into public school systems. By World War I, public kindergarten students outnumbered private ones by almost 19 to 1.

### 4. Commercial Adoption

You address a market failure or inefficiency, demonstrating that a product or service can be delivered profitably, then hand over to commercial providers while continuing to serve harder-to-reach segments.

**Core approach:** Demonstrating the impact and profitability of a product or service, and reducing associated risks

**Future role:** Maintaining advocacy and monitoring efforts, targeting hard-to-reach market segments, and ensuring quality of commercial delivery

**Example:** Early microfinance pioneers like BRAC and the Grameen Foundation showed it was possible to provide financial services to the poor profitably. Commercial banks subsequently moved in to serve the higher end of the market, while nonprofits continue to serve harder-to-reach segments and develop new products.

### 5. Mission Achievement

You have a well-defined and plausibly achievable goal. Once you achieve it, the organisation should wind down -- unless it has especially valuable assets or capabilities that can be redeployed for another social purpose.

**Core approach:** Maintaining a focused effort on a targeted intervention

**Future role:** Applying unique assets and capabilities to additional issue areas, or winding down

**Example:** The March of Dimes was founded to fight polio. After vaccines effectively ended the polio epidemic in the US, the organisation redeployed its core assets -- an extensive grassroots network and a trusted brand -- to serve a new mission: preventing birth defects and infant mortality.

### 6. Sustained Service

You fill an enduring gap in the market or public services that the commercial and government sectors cannot or will not fill. This is the default endgame for many nonprofits, but it is not always the right one.

**Core approach:** Creating a cost-effective model, building a strong organisation, and making continuous efficiency improvements

**Future role:** Continued provision of a core service at an ever-increasing level of efficiency

**Example:** US nonprofit hospitals fill gaps in the health-care system -- particularly gaps related to equity of service -- that government and private sector entities are unlikely to fill. They sustain funding through earned revenue and philanthropy, and they depend on strong community ties.

**Important:** The sustained service model makes sense only when you can satisfy an enduring social need that other sectors will not address. Before defaulting to this endgame, test whether your service could achieve commercial adoption or government adoption instead.

## Choosing Your Endgame

Your endgame should be determined by two factors:

1. **The nature of the social problem** you have targeted -- How large is the total addressable challenge? What would it take to solve it?
2. **The characteristics of your operational model** -- Is your approach easy for others to adopt? Does it require deep local knowledge? Can it generate revenue?

### Budget Implications

Different endgames have different budget trajectories. Only the sustained service model requires budgets to continue increasing indefinitely:

| Endgame | Budget Trajectory After Breakout |
|---|---|
| Open Source | Drops significantly as you become a knowledge hub |
| Replication | Drops significantly as you become a centre of excellence |
| Mission Achievement | Drops significantly as you declare success and wind down |
| Government Adoption | Varies depending on your continuing role |
| Commercial Adoption | Varies depending on your continuing role |
| Sustained Service | Continues to increase |

## Guidance for Leaders

### Define Your Endgame Early

Include an endgame statement alongside your mission, vision, and intended impact statements. Make clear to funders, beneficiaries, and supporters which endgame category you fall into, and under what circumstances your organisation will dissolve, merge, or change scope.

### Focus on Your Core

With a defined endgame, you can spend more time on the core activities that advance your mission. If your ultimate goal is to transfer operations to local partners, then investing in direct implementation -- even if it would expand your reach in the short term -- is not in your best interest.

### Prepare Your Team

You are a steward of both your mission and the people who join your organisation. As you approach your endgame, communicate honestly with your team about the likely changes to your budget and staff size. Managed well, this creates a sense of purpose that motivates people during their time with you.

## Guidance for Funders

### Fill the Gap

Nonprofits need a minimum level of funding to develop core capabilities. Funders can help by filling the social capital chasm and working with grantees to develop sustainable growth strategies, rather than only funding early-stage activity.

### Invest in Catalytic Impact

When evaluating grantees, consider the total impact on a field that each grantee makes or has the potential to make. Look beyond direct impact (delivering services to beneficiaries) and indirect impact (improving outcomes). Consider the catalytic impact a nonprofit can achieve through government adoption, commercial adoption, or replication.

### Provide Endgame Support

Commit to supporting grantees until they reach their endgame stage and then through it. Instead of providing piecemeal assistance to many nonprofits, help the most promising ones achieve large-scale social change. As part of the grantmaking process, explicitly ask each nonprofit: "What is your endgame?"

## The Bigger Picture

The purpose of a nonprofit is not to increase its budget or its reach indefinitely. Its true goal is to achieve social impact. For some organisations, achieving impact at scale will mean slowing the growth of their budget and transferring services to other providers. For others, it will mean winding down entirely once the mission is achieved.

A nonprofit that defines its endgame early will tend to make better use of resources during its initial stages of growth. Perhaps at no time in history has the social sector held so much potential. A new generation of purpose-driven professionals are flowing into the sector with new business models and tools. To achieve the true promise of this work, leaders must start a dialogue about which endgame they will pursue.
