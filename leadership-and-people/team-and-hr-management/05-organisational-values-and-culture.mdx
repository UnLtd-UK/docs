## Why This Matters

Every organisation has a culture, whether you have designed it intentionally or not. Culture is shaped by who gets rewarded, who gets promoted, and who gets let go. The actual values of your organisation are not the ones written on the wall -- they are the behaviours and skills that are truly valued in practice.

This guide helps you define values that genuinely drive culture, build a team environment based on trust and responsibility, and make better decisions together.

## Four Principles for Effective Values

Your organisational values need to be more than aspirational statements. To actually influence behaviour, they must meet four criteria:

### 1. Keep Them Short

Your values are a collection of filters. If you create too many, nobody will be able to get past all of them, and your team will get stuck. Aim for no more than three to five core values. Consider having no more than three -- any more and they will not be remembered.

### 2. Make Them Memorable

People need to recall your values easily if they are going to use them in everyday decisions. A punchy, memorable phrase works far better than a paragraph of explanation. If your team cannot recite your values from memory, they are too complex.

### 3. Align Them with Your Mission

Every value should promote behaviour that moves your organisation closer to its overall goal. Values that do not align with your mission are counterproductive and will frustrate employees or, worse, make them indifferent.

### 4. Keep Them Stable

Consistent values encourage compliance. Inconsistent values confuse people and foster poor behaviour. Your values are the bedrock principles upon which your team bases their actions. If people are unsure what those principles are, they will inevitably make the wrong call.

## Core Values That Drive Culture

Drawing on the most successful high-performance cultures, certain themes emerge repeatedly. Consider whether these resonate with your organisation:

### Judgment

- Making wise decisions despite ambiguity
- Identifying root causes rather than treating symptoms
- Thinking strategically about what you are and are not trying to do
- Separating what must be done well now from what can be improved later

### Communication

- Listening well rather than reacting fast
- Being concise and articulate in speech and writing
- Treating people with respect regardless of status or disagreement
- Maintaining composure in stressful situations

### Courage

- Saying what you think, even when it is controversial
- Making tough decisions without excessive deliberation
- Taking smart risks
- Questioning actions that are inconsistent with your values

### Honesty

- Being known for candour and directness
- Being non-political when you disagree with others
- Only saying things about colleagues that you would say to their face
- Being quick to admit mistakes

### Impact

- Accomplishing significant work, not just staying busy
- Demonstrating consistently strong performance that others can rely on
- Focusing on results rather than process
- Exhibiting a bias toward action over analysis paralysis

## Freedom and Responsibility

As organisations grow, there is a natural temptation to add rules and processes to prevent mistakes. But excessive process drives out the creative, self-motivated people you most want to keep.

### The Growth Trap

The typical pattern looks like this:

1. Growth increases complexity
2. Complexity leads to chaos and errors
3. Process is introduced to stop the chaos
4. Process drives out high-performing, innovative people
5. The organisation becomes efficient at executing the current model but unable to adapt when circumstances change

### A Better Approach

Instead of adding rules, focus on increasing the density of talented, responsible people in your team. Responsible people are:

- Self-motivating
- Self-aware
- Self-disciplined
- Self-improving
- Acting like leaders without waiting to be told what to do

With the right people, you can run informally with self-discipline rather than relying on rules. This informality is what enables and attracts creativity.

### Context, Not Control

The most effective approach to managing talented people is to provide context rather than control:

| Context (embrace) | Control (avoid) |
|---|---|
| Clear strategy and objectives | Top-down decision-making |
| Key metrics and definitions of success | Management approval for routine decisions |
| Clearly defined roles | Committees for every decision |
| Transparency around decision-making | Planning and process valued more than results |
| Knowledge of the stakes | Multi-level approval processes |

When someone on your team makes a poor decision, ask yourself what context you failed to set -- not what rule you should create.

### Good Process vs. Bad Process

Not all process is harmful:

- **Good process** helps talented people get more done (for example, regular strategy meetings, budget frameworks, communication protocols)
- **Bad process** tries to prevent recoverable mistakes through excessive approvals and sign-offs

The test is simple: does this process help people do better work, or does it exist because someone once made a mistake?

## High Performance Culture

### The "Keeper Test"

A useful thought experiment for any leader: if a team member told you they were leaving for a similar role at another organisation, how hard would you fight to keep them? The answer tells you a great deal about whether you have the right people in the right roles.

This is not about creating a culture of fear. It is about being honest with yourself and with your team about performance expectations.

### Stunning Colleagues

A great workplace is not defined by perks and benefits. It is defined by being surrounded by people you respect and learn from. Invest your energy in recruiting and retaining outstanding people, and the culture will take care of itself.

### Brilliant Jerks

Some organisations tolerate high performers who behave badly. The cost to teamwork is almost always too high. Diverse styles and perspectives are valuable, but everyone must embody your core values. Talent without respect is destructive.

## Consent vs. Consensus in Decision-Making

Many leaders assume that getting everyone to agree (consensus) is the hallmark of good leadership. In practice, chasing consensus often leads to watered-down outcomes and slow decision-making.

### The Difference

- **Consensus** means everyone agrees with the decision
- **Consent** means everyone understands the decision and can live with it, even if it is not their first choice

### Why Consent Works Better

- It produces faster, clearer decisions
- It avoids the lowest-common-denominator effect where ideas get diluted to satisfy everyone
- It builds more agile teams that can adapt quickly
- It respects the leader's responsibility to make tough calls

### Putting It Into Practice

- Present your reasoning and the context behind a decision
- Invite questions and concerns
- Listen carefully and adjust where it makes sense
- Seek understanding, not universal agreement
- Make the call and communicate it clearly

The shift from "everyone agrees" to "everyone understands" can transform your decision-making culture.

## Building Your Culture Intentionally

Culture is not something you set once and forget. It requires ongoing attention:

1. **Define your values early** -- and keep the list short, memorable, and aligned with your mission
2. **Hire for values fit** -- skills can be taught, but alignment with your culture is harder to develop
3. **Reward the right behaviours** -- make sure what you celebrate matches what you say you value
4. **Address misalignment quickly** -- when someone's behaviour contradicts your values, deal with it promptly
5. **Lead by example** -- your team watches what you do far more than what you say
6. **Review and refresh** -- revisit your values periodically to make sure they still reflect who you are and where you are going
