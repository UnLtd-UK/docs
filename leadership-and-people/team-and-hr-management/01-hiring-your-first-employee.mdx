## Why This Matters

Hiring your first employee is a milestone for any growing organisation. It brings additional capacity, but it also introduces legal responsibilities you need to understand from day one. This guide walks you through the entire process, from defining the role to welcoming your new team member.

## Defining the Job

Before you advertise, get clear on exactly what you need.

### Write a Job Description

Your job description should include:

- **Job title** -- keep it clear and searchable
- **Main purpose of the role** -- a one-paragraph summary of why this job exists
- **Reporting line** -- who the person will be responsible to
- **Key responsibilities and tasks** -- what they will actually do day-to-day

### Write a Person Specification

The person specification describes the ideal candidate. Separate requirements into:

- **Essential** -- qualifications, experience, and abilities that are non-negotiable
- **Desirable** -- attributes that would be a bonus but are not required

Keep both documents simple and focused. Overly long lists of requirements can discourage strong candidates from applying. Research suggests that men tend to apply when they meet around 60 percent of listed requirements, whereas women often wait until they meet nearly all of them. Be intentional about what you list as essential.

### Decide on Employment Terms

Consider whether the role should be:

- Full-time or part-time
- Suitable for job-sharing or remote working
- Permanent, fixed-term, or temporary

Research what other employers pay for similar roles. You must pay at least the [National Minimum Wage](https://www.gov.uk/national-minimum-wage).

## Advertising and Sourcing Candidates

### Where to Advertise

You can use a range of channels depending on your budget and the role:

- [Jobcentre Plus](https://www.gov.uk/jobcentre-plus-help-for-recruiters/recruitment-advice-and-support)
- Online job boards and recruitment websites
- Social networks such as LinkedIn
- Local newspapers
- Careers services at schools, colleges, and universities
- Job or career fairs

### Writing a Strong Job Advert

A good job advert should include:

- Job title, salary (plus benefits), and whether it is full-time or part-time
- A one-line summary of the candidate you are looking for
- A brief description of your organisation
- An overview of the role and key responsibilities
- Required skills, qualifications, and experience
- How to apply, the closing date, and what the recruitment process involves

### Preparing an Application Pack

Put together a pack that includes the job description, person specification, an application form (if you are using one), and some background on your organisation. Make it available to download where possible.

## Shortlisting and Interviewing

### Shortlisting Applicants

Using a standard application form rather than CVs alone helps you compare candidates fairly and consistently against the person specification. As best practice, involve at least two people in shortlisting to ensure objectivity.

Consider using **blind recruitment** by removing personal information (name, age, gender) from applications before review. This helps reduce unconscious bias in your decision-making.

### Conducting Interviews

If you are interviewing for the first time, keep the following in mind:

- Plan your questions in advance and use the same structure for each candidate
- Use open-ended questions to get the most revealing answers
- Give candidates the opportunity to ask questions about the role and your organisation
- Ensure you will not be disturbed during the interview
- Take notes so you can compare candidates afterwards and provide reasons to unsuccessful applicants who request feedback

If the role requires particular skills such as writing, data entry, or coding, it is reasonable to include a practical assessment as part of the selection process.

## Avoiding Discrimination

### The Equality Act 2010

Under the [Equality Act 2010](https://www.acas.org.uk/discrimination-and-the-law), you must not discriminate against anyone during recruitment on any of the following protected characteristics:

| Protected Characteristic | Examples |
|---|---|
| Gender | Male, female, non-binary |
| Race | Colour, nationality, ethnic or national origin |
| Age | Any age group |
| Marriage and civil partnership | Married or in a civil partnership |
| Pregnancy and maternity | Being pregnant or having recently given birth |
| Disability | Physical, mental, or learning disabilities |
| Religion or belief | Any religion, belief, or lack of religion |
| Gender reassignment | Transsexuality |
| Sexual orientation | Heterosexual, gay, lesbian, bisexual |

### Practical Steps to Avoid Discrimination

- Do not ask applicants about protected characteristics during recruitment, except where legally permitted (for example, asking about adjustments needed for the recruitment process itself)
- Avoid phrases like "recent graduate" in adverts, which could amount to age discrimination
- Advertise through diverse channels to avoid indirect discrimination
- Retain all application forms and rejection letters for six months after recruitment ends, in case you need to demonstrate your process was fair

## Data Protection in Recruitment

When handling applicant information, you must comply with data protection regulations:

- Only collect personal information that is necessary for the recruitment process
- Do not ask for irrelevant data such as banking details at application stage
- Do not use the information for any purpose other than recruitment
- Store all personal information securely and only for as long as necessary
- If you want to keep unsuccessful applicants' details for future roles, you must get their explicit permission
- Allow applicants to view, correct, or request erasure of personal data held about them

For detailed guidance, visit the [ICO's employment guidance](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/guide-to-data-protection/employment/).

## Pre-Employment Checks

### Right to Work in the UK

You must check that every new employee has the right to work in the UK before or on their first day. This involves:

- Examining original documents in the employee's presence
- Keeping a copy of the documents and a written record of the check

Failure to carry out correct right-to-work checks can lead to a fine of up to 20,000 pounds. For more information, visit [gov.uk/check-job-applicant-right-to-work](https://www.gov.uk/check-job-applicant-right-to-work).

### Criminal Records (DBS) Checks

For some roles, such as working with children or vulnerable adults, you must carry out a criminal records check before your new employee begins work. In England and Wales, these are known as DBS checks and are processed by the [Disclosure and Barring Service](https://www.gov.uk/government/organisations/disclosure-and-barring-service).

### Health Checks

You can check aspects of an employee's health only where:

- It is a legal requirement (for example, vision tests for commercial vehicle drivers)
- The job requires it (for example, for insurance purposes)

## Making the Offer

### Verbal and Written Offers

Once you have selected your preferred candidate:

1. **Make a verbal offer** -- clarify that it is subject to right-to-work checks, references, and any other pre-employment conditions
2. **Take up references** -- you need the candidate's permission before approaching a former employer
3. **Send a written offer** once all checks are satisfactory, including:
   - Job title
   - Salary, hours, benefits, pension arrangements, and holiday entitlement
   - Start date
   - Instructions for accepting or declining the offer

Keep a reserve candidate in mind in case your first choice declines or fails pre-employment checks. Send polite rejection letters to unsuccessful candidates once the offer is accepted.

### Written Statement of Employment Particulars

You must provide a written statement of employment particulars within two months of the employee's start date (ideally on their first day). This is a legally binding document covering terms and conditions of employment.

## Setting Up as an Employer

### Register with HMRC

You must [register as an employer with HMRC](https://www.gov.uk/register-employer) so you can report and pay tax and National Insurance contributions via PAYE Online. Allow enough time to receive your registration details and set up payroll before your employee's first payday.

### Set Up Payroll

You need a payroll system to manage salary payments and HMRC reporting. You can use payroll software or hire a payroll service. When your employee starts, ask for their P45 or have them complete the [HMRC New Starter Checklist](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/paye-starter-checklist).

### Get Employers' Liability Insurance

You must have employers' liability insurance as soon as you become an employer. It must:

- Come from an authorised insurer
- Cover you for at least 5 million pounds

You can be fined 2,500 pounds for every day you operate without it.

## Onboarding Your New Employee

### General Induction

Your induction should help the employee understand:

- The purpose and values of your organisation
- Everything they need to know to do their job
- Who they can talk to if they have problems in their role

### Health and Safety Training

You have a legal duty to provide adequate health and safety training under the Management of Health and Safety at Work Regulations 1999. This includes:

- Informing the employee about health and safety risks associated with their role
- Explaining workplace procedures for minimising risks
- Covering emergency procedures, including fire safety

## Your Ongoing Responsibilities

Once your employee is in post, you must observe the following rights:

- Pay the National Minimum Wage or above
- Provide the statutory minimum of 5.6 weeks paid holiday per year (28 days for full-time staff)
- Provide statutory minimum rest breaks
- Do not require more than 48 hours average work per week without a written opt-out
- Provide Statutory Sick Pay (SSP)
- Provide maternity, paternity, and adoption leave and pay
- Do not make unauthorised deductions from wages
- Respect minimum notice periods (usually one week per year of service, up to 12 weeks)
- Do not dismiss without good reason or proper disciplinary procedures
- Consider flexible working requests fairly
- Allow time off for emergencies
- Do not penalise whistleblowers or part-time workers
- Provide statutory redundancy pay where applicable

Most of these rights apply from the employee's first day. Others, such as the right to request flexible working and redundancy pay, apply after a qualifying period of continuous employment.
