## Meeting Structure in Practice

A meeting in a Thinking Environment follows a clear structure designed to generate the best possible thinking from everyone present. The quality of your group's decisions and actions depends on the thinking they do together first.

When all Ten Components of a Thinking Environment are the culture of a meeting, people's thinking and engagement improve dramatically. The structure below applies these components through the building blocks of Thinking Pairs, Dialogue, Rounds, and Open Discussion.

### The Opening Round

The meeting begins with a structured Round. Going systematically around the group, the Chair gives everyone a turn to answer a positively-focused question such as:

- *What is going well in your work at the moment?*
- *What do you think we as a team are currently doing well?*

This is not filler. Starting with appreciation anchors the meeting in an accurate view of reality — one that includes what is working, not just what needs fixing. It also ensures that every voice is heard from the very first minute.

### Setting the Agenda

The Chair confirms the agenda and identifies the first item for discussion. Each agenda item should be framed as a question that focuses on the desired outcome from discussion of that item. Framing items as questions rather than topics gives people's thinking a clear direction.

### Addressing Each Agenda Item

Each agenda item follows a cycle of structured formats:

**Presentation** — If there is a presentation, the Chair introduces the Presenter and reminds the team to wait until the presentation has ended before asking questions or making comments. The Presenter states what question the presentation will answer and restates the discussion question the team will address in the first Round.

**Clarification Round** — Going systematically around the team, the Chair asks everyone for any clarifying questions of the Presenter. The Chair ensures this question-and-answer exchange does not turn into a discussion.

**Question Round** — The Chair asks the agenda item question, going systematically around the team. Only after everyone has had their turn does the Chair allow open discussion.

**Open Discussion** — The Chair ensures that people are not interrupted when they are speaking.

**Round** — The Chair asks a refreshed question and conducts another Round.

**Discussion, Round, Discussion, Round...** — The cycle continues, alternating between open discussion and structured Rounds, until the item reaches a decision point.

**Decision and Action** — Decisions are made and action items agreed by whatever means the team usually uses. The Chair makes clear what that process is.

### Thinking Pairs Within Meetings

At useful points during the meeting, the team can break into pairs to think for a few minutes, each without interruption, about a point under discussion. Then the Chair conducts a Round, asking for a key idea from each person. This process often unlocks thinking that would not emerge in a larger group setting.

### Removing Assumptions

Frequently during the meeting, people can ask themselves or the team these questions:

- *What might we be assuming that could be limiting our thinking on this issue?*
- *If we assumed something more liberating, what might our ideas be?*

### Burning Issues

When the meeting is almost at an end, the Chair asks each person:

- *Is there a burning issue that you think we should address at another time?*

This ensures that nothing important goes unraised while keeping the current meeting focused.

### The Closing Round

Any concerns should have been handled or mentioned by this point, so that the closing can be wholly positive. The Chair asks everyone to answer positively reflective questions such as:

- *What do you think went well in the meeting?*
- *What one quality do you respect in the colleague sitting to your right?*

## Meeting Flow at a Glance

Use this as a quick reference for structuring your meetings:

1. **Welcome** — appreciation and purpose of meeting
2. **Opening Round** — positive reflection on work, group, or issue
3. **First agenda item**
   - Agenda item question confirmed
   - Presentation if appropriate, followed by Clarification Round
   - Round on agenda item question
   - Discussion (with no interruption)
   - Round on refreshed question
   - Discussion (with no interruption)
   - Round on refreshed question
   - Continue the cycle as needed
   - Decision, action, next steps
4. **Next agenda item** — same process as above
5. **Burning Issues Round** — raising issues to be considered another time
6. **Closing Round** — appreciation of meeting and colleagues

## Meeting Guidelines

These guidelines help you sustain a Thinking Environment throughout your meetings:

1. **Only meet when it is necessary.** If the meeting does not require people's thinking, find another way to accomplish your mission. Meetings should make the best possible use of the intelligence, talents, and time of each team member.

2. **Frame each agenda item as a question.** This gives direction to people's thinking before discussion begins.

3. **Prepare people in advance.** If papers are sent ahead of the meeting, introduce each paper with a question that will focus their thinking as they read.

4. **Periodically review the core principles.** Revisit why Rounds, appreciation, non-interruption, and Thinking Pairs matter — do not let the structure become mechanical.

5. **Protect the integrity of Rounds.** If clarification or additional information is needed after someone's turn, provide it efficiently and return to the Round. Do not get into rebuttal or discussion until the Round is finished. Completed Rounds have a particular and valuable effect on the generative quality of the team's ideas.

6. **Do not comment during Rounds.** When it is not your turn, do not react to or comment on what people say in their turns.

7. **Maintain eye contact.** In discussion, keep your eyes on the eyes of the person speaking.

8. **Manage your body language.** Notice nervous habits and replace them with signals of attention and interest.

9. **Stay connected during presentations.** When using slides or visual aids, sustain a strong, supple, and personal connection with the group rather than turning your back to read from a screen.

10. **Scribe accurately.** When recording on a flip chart, do not begin writing until the person speaking has finished formulating their point. Then ask them to condense it and scribe using only their own words. Do not paraphrase.

11. **Appreciate with precision.** When acknowledging colleagues, be succinct, sincere, and specific. Do not begin with caveats ("I don't really know what to say, but...") or self-deprecation ("I only wish I had a tenth of your talent...").

12. **Receive appreciation gracefully.** Listen all the way through, keeping your eyes on the person appreciating you. When they finish, say simply, "Thank you."

13. **Remove digital distractions.** Keep the environment free of phone alerts, laptop use (except for presentations), and other technological interruptions.

14. **Take regular breaks.** Allow for comfort breaks every 80 to 100 minutes. Avoid the practice of people getting up during discussion.

## Telephone and Video Meetings

Meetings held by telephone or video conference can be run as a Thinking Environment with some additional considerations.

### Establishing Place

Even without a physical room, the component of Place still matters:

- Ensure the technology is workable for everyone and is the best available for the meeting
- Send out the agenda ahead of the call, including approximate times for each person's turn per agenda item
- **Create the virtual table** — draw a circle of names of attendees and tell the group who is next to whom, so that Rounds can flow naturally
- Eliminate all forms of distraction, including putting all digital devices to sleep (not just on mute) unless they are in active use for an agenda item

### Conducting the Meeting

Telephone and video meetings use all the same guidelines and processes that work in face-to-face Thinking Environments. A few additional behaviours are important:

- **Remind people about attention.** Their attention for the person speaking should be as seamless and generative as it would be face-to-face. This is particularly challenging when no one can see each other. Remind people that checking emails can be sensed by the speaker.
- **Establish timed turns for Rounds.** Ask everyone to have their own timepiece to keep track of their time during their turn.
- **Signal when you have finished.** Ask participants to say clearly when they have completed their turn, both in Rounds and in Open Discussion.
- **Favour Rounds over Open Discussion.** Aim for a ratio of approximately 3:1 Rounds to Open Discussion — Rounds work better than Open Discussion when people cannot see each other.
- **Schedule breaks.** Take a stretch break after the first hour and keep the total meeting time to two hours.

Look for opportunities to break into Thinking Pairs or Dialogue using breakout rooms or separate calls before returning to the group.

## The Time to Think Council

The Time to Think Council is a structured process that allows the wisdom of a group to make its way into the problem-solving of one person — without telling that person what to think.

You want to solve a problem. You would like to think for yourself about it, but along the way you would like access to the knowledge, experience, and information inside the treasure troves that are your colleagues. The Council makes this possible.

It does not matter what your problem is. When asked and listened to in this way, each member of your group has something valuable to offer. The process also demonstrates that when everyone thinks for themselves and listens well to everyone else, everyone's thinking improves.

### The Eleven Stages

**Stage 1: Presentation (5-10 minutes)**
One person (the Presenter) describes a problem, issue, situation, or opportunity that they would like to get others' views, ideas, and perspectives on. They can draw or write on a flipchart if that helps. The Presenter says as much as they think will be useful for the Council to hear. The Council gives uninterrupted, generative attention throughout.

**Stage 2: Formation of the Question**
The Facilitator works with the Presenter to formulate the question they want the Council to address. Ideally the question is succinct.

**Stage 3: Clarifying Round**
A brief Round where anyone can ask clarifying questions to ensure they have understood what they are being asked to address. Care must be taken not to coach, begin answering the question, or offer solutions. This Round is for clarification only.

**Stage 4: Confirmation of Question**
The Facilitator confirms the Presenter's question and checks that this is still what they would like Council input on.

**Stage 5: Initial Thinking Time**
The Council is given one to two minutes to consider the question and write down initial thoughts on a clean sheet of paper. These notes will eventually be given to the Presenter.

**Stage 6: Council Round (20-30 minutes)**
Each member of the Council responds to the Presenter's question while the others give generative attention to the person speaking. No advice is given — contributions are made only from experience or from what the member might do in that situation.

**Stage 7: End of Round (5-10 minutes)**
The Facilitator asks the Presenter: *What is your freshest thinking now?* The Presenter can say as much or as little as they like. They do not have to approve, agree, or disagree with anything a Council member has said, or say what they are going to do about it — unless they wish to.

**Stage 8: Another Round (if needed)**
If there is desire, time, and the need, the Facilitator checks whether the question is still accurate and asks if another Round would be useful where people could build on their previous ideas.

**Stage 9: End of Rounds**
Each person adds any final thoughts on paper and gives their notes to the Presenter as a gift.

**Stage 10: Appreciation Round**
All members of the Council express, in a Round, a quality that they have seen in the Presenter.

**Stage 11: Participation Appreciation Round**
If there is time, each member of the Council — including the Presenter — says what they have most appreciated about being part of the Council.

### Role of the Presenter

The Presenter brings their issue, problem, or project to the group. As the Presenter, you:

- Need to be relatively clear in your mind about what you want to bring and why
- Bring a topic that is real, live, and involves a degree of complexity
- Describe the issue so that people can make sense of it, without too much back story
- Listen to all contributions during the Round
- Are under no obligation to take away or act on any of the Council's ideas
- Retain full ownership of the problem and decide on any action you would like to take
- Continue to think for yourself throughout the session

### Role of Council Members

As a member of the Council, you:

- Listen attentively to the Presentation and other Council members without interruption
- Do your own best thinking on the problem — think for yourself
- Provide input using the language of experience, not advice
- Listen to all ideas openly and supportively, using the Ten Components as your guide
- Are mindful of equality of time — being succinct so that others can speak
- Avoid diagnosing, advising, recommending, or providing solutions
- Avoid coaching "in the moment"
- Avoid judgement, taking sides, or getting drawn in
- Welcome diversity and respect confidentiality

### Role of the Facilitator

The Facilitator:

- Holds the process and ensures the focus remains on thinking
- Ensures the Council is clear about what is being asked and what the question is
- Provides the direction of the Round and clarifies how long people have to contribute
- Intervenes if needed to ensure the Presenter is not being explicitly or implicitly told what to think or do, or when the group needs support and guidance
- Sets the tone for the Council and models the Ten Components of the Thinking Environment

### Using Experience Language, Not Advice Language

One of the most important disciplines in the Council is speaking from experience rather than giving advice. The distinction is critical to preserving the Presenter's ownership of their problem and their thinking.

**Avoid sentences that start with:**
- "If I were you..."
- "If you were me..."
- "You need to / should do / try / be..."
- "Why haven't / don't you...?"
- "Why would you think that?"

**Instead, use sentences that start with or include "I," "me," or "mine":**
- "In my experience..."
- "This reminds me of a time..."
- "What I would find challenging would be..."
- "If I was faced with a similar problem, I might choose to focus on this area..."
- "I remember when I experienced..."
- "This made me think about..."

This shift in language transforms the contribution from direction-giving into experience-sharing. The Presenter hears what has worked for others and draws their own conclusions, rather than feeling told what to do. It is a subtle but profound difference that preserves the thinking autonomy at the heart of the entire Thinking Environment approach.
